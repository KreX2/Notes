Chapter 2 - Comparative Market Analysis

Who Prices a Home and How

  • Setting a home's listing (asking) price—which is distinct from market value, appraised value, and assessed value—is a collaborative process:
    • The Real Estate Agent's Role: Researches local market data and presents a well-supported suggested price range to the homeowner (often during a pre-listing presentation before a listing agreement is signed).
    • The Seller's Role: Makes the final decision on the listing price.
  • Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)
    • Terminology: In industry practice, CMA stands for either Comparative Market Analysis or Competitive Market Analysis—both terms are used interchangeably as synonyms.
    • Core Purpose: A report that evaluates recently sold, pending, or actively listed homes (comparables or "comps") in the same area to estimate the probable market value of a similar home (the subject property) and help sellers determine a competitive listing price.
    • Manual vs. Digital Tools: While learning manual CMA worksheets teaches the underlying valuation mechanics, modern real estate brokerages primarily rely on specialized software and algorithmic CMA tools.
  • CMA vs. Appraisal Comparison
    • Although both tools estimate fair market value using the Sales Comparison Approach (grounded in the principle of substitution and the principle of contribution), a CMA is not a formal appraisal:

  • Appraisals vs. CMAs
  • Who Needs a CMA? (Sellers and Buyers)
    • For Sellers: Listing agents prepare a Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) to establish a competitive, market-supported listing price range before taking a property to market.
    • For Buyers: Buyer's agents should also prepare a CMA for any property in which a buyer expresses serious, continued interest—and before submitting an offer on the buyer's behalf—to ensure the buyer does not overpay.
  • Practical Application of Supply and Demand (Months of Inventory)
    • Agents evaluate recent closed sales (typically over the last three months) alongside active listings to measure current market absorption and guide pricing strategy:
      • Monthly Sales Rate (Demand): Total homes sold in 3 months ÷ 3 months = Average monthly sales
        • Example: 9 homes sold ÷ 3 months = 3 homes sold per month
      • Months of Housing Supply (Absorption Rate): Total active listings ÷ Average monthly sales = Months of supply
        • High Supply Example: 15 active listings ÷ 3 homes sold per month = 5-month supply (high competition; a seller who wants a quick sale must price competitively against the top 3 or 4 active listings).
        • Low Supply Example: 4 active listings ÷ 3 homes sold per month = 1.33-month supply (tight inventory; a seller has room to price slightly higher and still attract strong buyer interest).
  • The 5 Steps to Creating a CMA ("Outside-In" Approach)
    • Creating a CMA moves progressively from broad external market conditions inward to the specific subject property:
      1. Evaluate the neighborhood (location desirability, local market trends, and surrounding property standards).
      1. Evaluate the subject property (square footage, bed/bath count, lot size, condition, age, and upgrades).
      1. Find comparables (identify recently sold, pending, and active listings in the same area with similar characteristics).
      1. Compare and adjust selected comparables (account for physical, location, and feature differences between the comps and the subject property).
      1. Establish a listing price range (synthesize adjusted comparable values and current supply-and-demand conditions into a recommended pricing range).
  • Step 1: Evaluate the Neighborhood
    • Location cannot be changed and serves as a primary driver of value—even within the same neighborhood, micro-location matters (e.g., waterfront or green-space backing lots command higher demand than homes across the street).
    • Neighborly Principles of Value:
      • Conformity: Values peak when homes in a neighborhood share similar style, size, and standards.
      • Regression: Lower-value surrounding homes pull down the value of a higher-value subject property.
      • Progression: Higher-value surrounding homes pull up the value of a lower-value subject property.
    • Key Neighborhood Factors to Analyze:
      • Occupancy & Distress: Rental-to-owner-occupied ratio (high rental concentration lowers value) and presence of vacancies or foreclosures.
      • Governance & Taxes: HOA rules/codes, zoning (classifications, mixed-use, or areas in transition), and local property tax rates.
      • Infrastructure & Public Services: Street width and condition, available utilities (electric, gas, sewer, cable, internet), and municipal services (police, fire, public transit).
      • Access, Environment & Terrain: Proximity to major roads, employers, schools, retail, and entertainment; environmental factors (noise, traffic, odors, pollution, wind); and topography (flat, hilly, steep, or barren).
  • Step 2: Evaluate the Subject Property
    • Gather complete baseline data on the lot and structure so you can select true "apples-to-apples" comparables.
    • Lot Attributes:
      • Size and dimensions (standard rectangular shapes are most desirable) and frontage (value-adding access to water, parks, or thoroughfares).
      • Landscape/topography, sun orientation and shade, and exposure to wind or noise.
      • Title Concerns: Easements, encroachments, or deed restrictions.
      • Stigmatization: Negative local associations—such as a violent crime, illegal activity, or reputed haunting—that reduce buyer demand despite no physical defect.
    • Structure & Room Inventory (Outside-In):
      • Age, Condition & Builder: Document overall condition, construction/material quality, and the original builder's market reputation.
      • Net Square Footage: Verify exact livable square footage using local municipal measurement standards; appraisers generally keep comps within 10% of the subject property's square footage.
      • Room Counts:
        • Bedrooms & Convertible Spaces: Count bedrooms and note dens or offices that can easily convert into bedrooms, plus distinct separated dining rooms.
        • Bathrooms: Distinguish between a full bath (tub and/or shower, sink, and toilet) and a half bath (sink and toilet).
  • Step 3: Get Your Comparables (Comps)
    • Select at least three recently closed properties that closely match the subject property in age, size, style, and neighborhood.
    • Selection Standards for Sold Comps:
      • Recency: Ideally sold within the last 3 to 6 months (appraisers typically cap at 12 months in slower markets). Note original list price and days on market (DOM).
      • Arm's-Length Transactions Only: Include only impartial open-market sales between unrelated parties; exclude family/friend transfers, pocket listings with discounted fees, or distressed anomalies that distort market value.
      • Proximity: Ideally within 0.25 to 0.5 miles of the subject property (expanding outward only in rural or low-volume markets).
      • Age & Builder Match: Match construction era and builder reputation closely, while accounting for well-maintained or refurbished historic/mid-century homes.
    • How to Treat Non-Sold Listings in a CMA:
      Listing StatusRole & Analytical Caution in a CMA
      Recently Sold (Closed)Primary benchmark—reflects actual prices willing buyers recently paid in arm's-length transactions.
      Sale PendingReflects current buyer demand, but the final contract price is unconfirmed until closing; track DOM and prior price cuts.
      Current Active ListingsShows active competition, not market value ("asking isn't getting"—active listings often show what homes won't sell for).
      Expired & Re-Listed HomesUseful teaching examples for sellers showing the consequences of overpricing; if a re-listed home eventually sold, combine DOM across all listing periods.
  • Step 4: Compare & Adjust the Comparables
    • Because no two homes are identical, adjust the comparable's sale price (never the subject property's price) to reflect feature differences using paired-sales analysis (comparing two otherwise identical recent sales that differ by only one feature to isolate that feature's dollar value).
    • Common Adjustment Categories: Bathrooms, bedrooms, lot size, net square footage, garage capacity, basement (finished vs. unfinished), and property age/condition.
    • The Golden Rules of CMA Adjustments:
      • Comp is Superior (Comp has a feature the Subject lacks): Subtract the feature's value from the comparable's sale price (Comp Sale Price - Feature Value = Adjusted Comp Price).
      • Comp is Inferior (Comp lacks a feature the Subject has): Add the feature's value to the comparable's sale price (Comp Sale Price + Feature Value = Adjusted Comp Price).
    • The Comp Sales Comparison
    • In practice, no comparable property is an identical match to the subject property, so each comparable requires targeted dollar adjustments to create a true "apples-to-apples" comparison:
      • Comp A (Closest Match): Requires only one adjustment—adding the contributory value of a half-bath because Comp A has 0.5 fewer bathrooms than the subject property (Comp A Sale Price + Half-Bath Value = Comp A Adjusted Sales Price).
      • Comps B & C (Secondary Matches): Each requires two adjustments to account for their specific feature or condition differences relative to the subject property.
    • The Resulting Adjusted Sales Prices:
      • Once all positive and negative feature adjustments are applied to each comparable's actual sale price, the resulting adjusted sales prices estimate what each comparable would have sold for if it had the exact same features as the subject property.
      • Weighting Reliability: Because Comp A required the fewest adjustments (only one), it is the most closely aligned with the subject property and will typically carry the greatest weight when reconciling the adjusted prices into Step 5: Establish a Listing Price Range.
  • Step 5: Establishing a Listing Price Range
    • Once comparable sale prices have been adjusted to match the subject property, you synthesize those figures into a recommended listing price range for the seller.
    • Constructing the Suggested Price Range:
      • Range Boundaries: Typically spans from at or just below the lowest adjusted comparable sale price up to at or just above the highest adjusted comparable sale price.
      • Weighting Comparables: Place the greatest analytical weight on the comparable(s) that required the fewest adjustments, as those properties are the closest natural match to the subject property.
      • Final Decision Authority: Your role as the agent is to provide a realistic, data-backed starting range; the property owner always has the final say on the actual listing price.
    • Adjusting the Listing Strategy for Current Market Conditions
      • After establishing the baseline adjusted comp range, help the seller position their exact asking price according to current supply-and-demand momentum:
        Market EnvironmentRecommended Listing Price StrategyMarket Rationale & Calculation Rule
        Buyer's Market (High supply, lower buyer demand)Set a buyer-friendly price just below the top end of the suggested rangeSellers face stiffer competition and should anticipate that the final negotiated sale price will likely close below the asking price.
        Neutral Market (Balanced supply and demand)Start closer to the top end of the suggested range and apply a time/trend adjustmentAccount for steady market appreciation since the last comp closed:

        Last Comp Price + (Last Comp Price × Monthly Appreciation % × Months Elapsed) = Adjusted List Price

        (e.g., adding 1% per month for each month elapsed since the last comparable closed).
        Seller's Market (Low inventory, rising prices)Price 10%–12% above the last comparable sale:

        Last Comp Price × 1.10 to 1.12 = Aggressive List Price        		Shrinking inventory and upward price momentum allow sellers to push above recent closed sales and still attract strong buyer demand.
  • Benefits of a Properly Priced Property
    • Setting the right listing price combines market research, financial math, and strategic positioning:
      • Fast Buyer Responses (Capturing the "New Factor"): Studies show a property's first two weeks on the market generate peak buyer attention. Pricing accurately from day one maximizes initial traffic; cutting the price later cannot recapture that lost novelty and often makes buyers suspicious ("What's wrong with this house?").
      • Increased Buyer Competition: Pricing competitively—or strategically slightly below market value—attracts strong early traffic and can spark multiple offers or a bidding war, driving the final contract price up to or above market value.
  • The Dangers of Overpricing & Managing Seller Expectations
    • Overpricing is one of the primary reasons listings fail to sell, typically driven by a seller's emotional attachment and sentimental memories (which add zero market value for buyers).
    • The Overpriced Listing Cycle: An overpriced property receives minimal showings, sits stale on the market, frustrates the seller, and eventually expires—often resulting in the seller hiring a different agent who prices it properly.
    • When to Walk Away: If a prospective seller refuses to list within a realistic, CMA-supported price range, an agent may need to decline the listing rather than invest time and marketing budget into an unsellable property.
    • Terminology Tip ("Price Adjustment" vs. "Price Reduction"): When correcting an overpriced listing, always refer to the change as a price adjustment rather than a "price reduction"—the word "reduction" triggers emotional resistance and defensiveness from sellers.
  • Sales Price vs. Listing Price & Strategic Pricing
    • What matters most to a seller's bottom line is the final sales price, not the initial asking price. Listing high with the plan to "drop the price later" frequently backfires for two reasons:
      1. Stigma of High Days on Market (DOM): Extended market time leads buyers to assume the home has hidden defects, lowering the offers they are willing to make.
      1. Loss of Negotiating Leverage: A buyer's agent reviewing a listing history with multiple price drops will recognize seller weakness and demand heavier repairs, closing credits, or price concessions—often netting the seller less than if they had priced accurately from day one.
        Pricing ApproachMarket ExecutionTypical Outcome
        OverpricingListing well above CMA value to "leave room to negotiate"Wastes the critical first two weeks, increases DOM, triggers buyer skepticism, and forces concessions that yield a lower final sales price.
        Strategic Slight UnderpricingListing just below the CMA-suggested market value (especially effective in a seller's market)Generates immediate buyer urgency, higher showing volume, and multiple offers that can bid the final sales price above market value.
  • Next Step: Publishing to the Multiple Listing Service (MLS)
    • Once the seller and agent finalize a well-researched listing price, the property is entered into the Multiple Listing Service (MLS)—a shared database where member brokers distribute property listings so cooperating brokers can bring qualified buyers to the transaction.
  • CMA Comparable Adjustment Practice
    • Core Rule: Always adjust the comparable property's sale price to match the subject property—never adjust the subject property:
      • Comp has a superior feature (more beds/baths/sq. ft. or newer age) → Subtract the feature's value from the comp.
      • Comp has an inferior feature (fewer beds/baths/sq. ft. or older age) → Add the feature's value to the comp.

Key Terms

  • comparable - any property which has sold and is similar enough in features, location, and proximity in time to inform the value of a subject property
  • comparative market analysis (CMA) - a report that compares the prices of recently sold or listed homes ("comparables") in order to estimate the market value of a similar property (the "subject property") located in the same area
