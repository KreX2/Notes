  • Essential Qualities & Skills of Commercial Leasing Agents
    • Networking & Reputation: Because the commercial real estate sector is tight-knit, building strong business community relationships and a trusted reputation is a licensee's most valuable asset.
    • Mastery of Complex Lease Clauses: Commercial contracts involve custom operational terms—such as tenant improvements/build-outs, maintenance allocations, and expense pass-throughs. Licensees must thoroughly understand these clauses while always advising clients to have a real estate attorney review final commercial leases.
    • Patience & Negotiation Stamina: Unlike standardized residential leases, commercial leases involve higher dollar values, longer commitments, and extensive custom negotiations, resulting in significantly longer transaction timelines.
  • Commercial Leases Classified by Rent Calculation
    • While commercial leases also establish traditional leasehold estates (for years, periodic, at will, or at sufferance), they are primarily categorized by how rent and property expenses are calculated and paid—and a single commercial lease often blends features from multiple categories:
      1. Gross Lease
      1. Net Lease
      1. Percentage Lease
      1. Variable Lease
  • Gross Lease (Full-Service Lease)
    • Core Definition: A lease structure in which the tenant pays a fixed, all-inclusive monthly rent, while the landlord assumes responsibility for paying all property operating expenses (taxes, insurance, utilities, and maintenance).
    • Pricing & Usage: Because the landlord builds estimated operating costs into the base rental rate, the monthly rent appears higher than in net leases. Gross leases are standard for residential apartments and are frequently used in commercial office and industrial spaces where tenants prefer predictable, lump-sum monthly expenses.
    • Expense Stop Provision:
      • Definition: A protective cap negotiated into a gross lease setting the maximum dollar amount the landlord will pay toward annual operating expenses; if actual expenses exceed that cap, the tenant pays the overage.
      • Worked Example: Zied's retail lease includes a $50,000 annual expense stop. When annual operating expenses total $60,000, the landlord pays $50,000 and Zied pays the remaining $10,000 ($60,000 - $50,000 = $10,000).
  • Net Lease
    • Core Definition: A commercial lease structure (common in retail, office, and industrial properties) in which the tenant pays a lower base rent plus all or a prorated share of the property's operating expenses (fixed costs like property taxes and variable costs like maintenance).
    • The Three Tiers of Net Leases: Each additional "net" shifts another core operating expense category from the landlord onto the tenant.
      Net Lease TierAlternative NamesWhat the Tenant PaysKey Characteristics
      Single-Net Lease (N)Net leaseBase rent + Property taxesTenant pays their share of real estate taxes; landlord retains insurance and maintenance costs
      Double-Net Lease (NN)Net-net leaseBase rent + Property taxes + InsuranceTenant covers taxes (first net) and property insurance (second net); landlord covers structural maintenance
      Triple-Net Lease (NNN)Net-net-net leaseBase rent + Property taxes + Insurance + Maintenance (all operating expenses)Tenant pays a prorated share of all operating expenses—and in some agreements, even a portion of interest on the landlord's loan
  • Gross Lease vs. Net Lease Comparison
    • Both lease structures determine how property operating expenses (taxes, insurance, utilities, and maintenance) are divided between the landlord and the tenant.

  • Percentage Lease
    • Core Definition: A commercial lease structure (most commonly used for retail properties, such as storefronts and shopping malls) in which the tenant pays a fixed base rent plus a specified percentage of their gross sales generated at the property.
    • Rent Variability: Total monthly rent fluctuates directly with the tenant's business revenue—aligning the landlord's return with the retail location's commercial performance.
  • Percentage Lease Rent Formula
    • Fixed rent + (Overage × Sales rate) = Rent
      • Fixed rent: The guaranteed minimum base rent paid each period.
      • Overage: The tenant's gross sales volume (or gross sales exceeding a stipulated breakpoint, depending on lease terms).
      • Sales rate: The percentage of gross sales applied toward rent (converted to a decimal during calculation).
  • Worked Example: Candy Store Monthly Rent
    • Given Variables:
      • Fixed Monthly Rent: $3,000
      • Gross Monthly Sales (Overage): $50,000
      • Sales Rate: 2% (0.02)
    • Step-by-Step Calculation:
      1. Insert known variables: $3,000 + ($50,000 × 2%) = Rent
      1. Calculate percentage of sales (convert 2% to 0.02): $50,000 × 0.02 = $1,000
      1. Add fixed rent and sales-based rent: $3,000 + $1,000 = $4,000
    • Final Result: The candy store's total monthly rent is $4,000.
  • Variable Lease
    • Core Definition: A commercial leasehold agreement in which the base rent changes over time rather than remaining fixed throughout the entire lease term.
    • Purpose & Property Fit: Used almost exclusively in long-term commercial leases (e.g., 20- to 50-year terms) to protect landlords and property managers against inflation, rising utility costs, and shifting property values while locking in a long-term tenant. Residential leases rarely use variable structures because their shorter terms (typically one year or less) allow landlords to simply adjust rent upon renewal.
    • Escalation Clause: Both forms of variable leases rely on an escalation clause—a contract provision that legally secures the lessor's right to increase rent payments at specified intervals based on a predetermined factor or economic benchmark.
  • Two Types of Variable Leases
    Variable Lease TypeAlternative NameMechanism for Rent AdjustmentBenefits & Practical Example
    Graduated LeaseStep-up leaseRent increases at specific intervals by fixed, predetermined amounts or percentages written into the escalation clause• Tenant benefit: Lower initial rent payments make move-in affordable for growing businesses.

    • Landlord benefit: Predictable rent growth matching rising property values.

    • Example: A 20-year lease with an escalation clause increasing rent by 5% every year.
    Index Lease—Rent adjusts at periodic intervals based on changes in an external economic indicator, such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI)• Tenant benefit: Protects against arbitrary hikes by tying increases to real economic inflation.

    • Landlord benefit: Preserves profitability as broader market costs shift.

    • Mechanism: Escalation clause ties rent adjustments directly to fluctuations in the specified index.
  • Recap: Commercial Leases Classified by How Rent Is Paid
    • Gross Lease (Full-Service Lease): Tenant pays a single fixed rent; landlord pays all operating expenses (unless capped by an expense stop).
    • Net Lease (Single, Double, Triple Net): Tenant pays base rent plus one, two, or all three core property expenses (taxes, insurance, and maintenance).
    • Percentage Lease: Tenant pays a fixed base rent plus a specified percentage of gross retail sales (Fixed rent + (Overage × Sales rate) = Rent).
    • Variable Lease: Base rent adjusts over a long-term lease via an escalation clause, structured either as a graduated (step-up) lease or an index lease.

  • Oil & Gas Leases (Tennessee Statutory Rules)
    • Payment Structure: The lessee (energy company) typically pays the lessor (landowner) an upfront signing bonus, followed by ongoing lease payments plus royalties based on a percentage of well production.
    • Tennessee Statutory Expiration & Reversion:
      • Leaseholds for oil and natural gas automatically expire 10 years from the execution date unless the company is actively producing oil or natural gas for marketable uses at the end of that 10-year period.
      • If the lessee ceases production for six consecutive months after the 10-year period expires, all mineral/leasehold rights immediately revert back to the lessor.
  • Commercial Property Improvements, Maintenance & Trade Fixtures
    • Common Area Maintenance (CAM) & Anchor Tenants:
      • CAM Charges: Regular fees paid by commercial tenants in addition to base rent to fund shared spaces (lobbies, elevators, parking lots, walkways).
      • Anchor Tenant: The largest, highest-traffic business in a multi-tenant retail center (such as a major supermarket or department store) that draws customers to neighboring shops.
    • Handling Commercial Tenant Improvements (Build-Outs):
      1. Improvement Allowances (Tenant Improvement / TI Allowance): The landlord grants the tenant a negotiated cash allowance to manage and pay for their own interior construction.
      1. Turn-Key Projects: The landlord completes and funds all agreed-upon renovations upfront so the space is completely ready for the tenant to move in and operate immediately.
    • Trade Fixtures vs. Permanent Improvements:
      • Trade Fixtures: Specialized equipment or installations attached by a commercial tenant strictly to conduct their business; these remain the tenant's personal property and go with the tenant when the lease ends (provided the tenant repairs any removal damage).
      • Permanent Improvements: Structural or general building upgrades that are not trade fixtures; these become real property and remain with the landlord upon lease expiration.

  • Comprehensive Lease Classification Recap
    • Leases are categorized across three distinct dimensions—and because these categories are not mutually exclusive, a single lease contract regularly combines features from multiple groups (for example, a commercial contract can be both a triple-net lease and an estate for years, or a ground lease with a graduated rent schedule):
    1. Leases by Leasehold Estate (Duration & Renewal):
      • Estate for Years: Fixed starting and ending dates; terminates automatically with no automatic renewal.
      • Periodic Estate: Recurring periods (e.g., month-to-month) that automatically renew until either party gives proper notice.
      • Estate at Will: Permissive occupancy without a formal lease; terminable at any time by either party.
      • Estate at Sufferance: Holdover tenancy where a tenant remains after lease expiration without landlord consent.
    1. Leases by Rent Calculation (Payment Structure):
      • Gross Lease (Full-Service): Fixed all-inclusive rent; landlord pays operating expenses (unless capped by an expense stop).
      • Net Lease (Single, Double, Triple Net): Base rent plus property taxes (N), insurance (NN), and maintenance/operating costs (NNN).
      • Percentage Lease: Fixed base rent plus a percentage of the retail tenant's gross sales (Fixed rent + (Overage × Sales rate) = Rent).
      • Variable Lease: Base rent adjusts over time via an escalation clause—either by fixed steps (Graduated / Step-up Lease) or an economic benchmark like the CPI (Index Lease).
    1. Leases by Property Condition & Financing Purpose:
      • Ground Lease (Land Lease): Long-term lease (50+ years) of bare, undeveloped land where the tenant constructs improvements that revert to the landowner at expiration.
      • Shell Lease: Lease of an unfinished building structure where the tenant completes the interior build-out.
      • Proprietary Lease: Exclusive long-term lease granted by a housing cooperative to a resident shareholder.
      • Lease-Option / Lease-Purchase: Rent-to-own structures giving the tenant either a unilateral option or a binding obligation to buy the property.

Key Terms

  • escalation clause - a clause in a lease allowing payments to increase in relation to a specified factor or index
  • graduated lease - a lease in which the rental rate increases over time in specific increments
  • gross lease - a lease in which the tenant will be responsible for the payment of a fixed monthly charge, while the landlord is responsible for paying all operating expenses; aka full-service lease
  • ground lease - the lease of bare, undeveloped land; aka land lease
  • net lease - a lease in which the tenant pays a base rent rate plus all or part of the operating expenses
  • percentage lease - type of (usually commercial) lease in which the tenant pays a base rent amount and a percentage of their business profits to the landlord
  • triple-net lease - a type of lease requiring the tenant to pay a prorated portion of ALL the operating expenses for the property

  • Ground Leases
    • A ground lease, also known as a land lease, is the lease of bare, undeveloped land. These leases usually last for very long terms, like 50 years or more. This accounts for the fact that the lessee will be performing construction (making improvements) upon the land. In a ground lease, the owner grants the tenant a leasehold interest in the land only.
    • Upon expiration of a ground lease, the land, as well as any construction on the land, reverts back to the original landowner.
  • Oil and gas leases in Tennessee carry the following regulations:
    • They expire 10 years from the date executed, unless companies are producing natural gas or oil for marketable uses at the end of the 10-year period.
    • If the lessee ceases production for six months after the 10-year period has expired, then all rights will revert back to the lessor.
    • A building shell lease (or simply, a shell lease) is a type of commercial lease that involves renting an unfinished building.
  • Tenant Improvements
    • Improvements are usually handled in one of the two following ways:
      • Improvement allowances: The tenant negotiates to receive a cash allowance from their landlord. The tenant will use this money to pay for construction costs.
      • Turn-key projects: The improvements are handled completely by the landlord. The tenant can move in and have the use of all the amenities and features they need for doing business.
      • In either situation, the property manager should be clear about which improvements will be deemed trade fixtures and which will not.
