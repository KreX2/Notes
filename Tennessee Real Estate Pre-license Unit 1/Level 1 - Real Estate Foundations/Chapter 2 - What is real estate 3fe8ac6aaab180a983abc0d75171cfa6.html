Chapter 2 - What is real estate?

Land, Real Estate, and Real Property:

  • Land the surface of the earth, including all permanently attached natural features, extending downwards to the center of the earth and upwards to infinity.
  • Improvements - any non-natural structure built on or affixed to the land (Buildings, fences, decks, and roads are all examples of improvements, but a tree is NOT). Improvements MUST be permanently attached to the land. Otherwise, they're what is known as personal property
  • Real estate - is the land plus improvements. is also referred to as realty. (Land + Improvements)
  • Real property - refers to all that's stated above PLUS the bundle of rights associated with real estate ownership.
  • Note - (There is a big difference between real estate and real property: the bundle of rights. And while that is absolutely true, you should know that in the real world, people generally use the two terms interchangeably. You need to know this for the test, but after that, feel free to let it go.)

The Bundle of Rights

  • ⤷ All the things you can do with the land you own
  • Right of EXCLUSION
    • A landowner with the right of exclusion can decide who gets to be on their property (and who doesn't), gives an owner the right to tell trespassers (or anyone) to get off their land.
  • Right of POSSESSION
    • The right to occupy, live, breathe, and do whatever on your property because you own it. This is also known as the right to USE.
  • Right of DISPOSITION (or TRANSFER)
    • The right to sell, lease, and/or will a property to anyone you choose. You can sell your property whenever you want, to whoever you want, for however much they're willing to pay. This is also called the right to TRANSFER..
  • Right of quiet ENJOYMENT
    • The right of quiet enjoyment is the right to use your property without interference. That interference could come from past owners trying to exert possession claims, or from literal interference like unreasonable noise. We're not talking about objecting to your neighbor's band practice, though. It's more like if the city planned to build an airport next door: That would violate your right to quiet enjoyment.
    • The right of quiet enjoyment isn't just a right that property owners have. When a person rents a property, they also get the right of quiet enjoyment. This right covers a lessee's right to possess a property without their landlord trying to use the part of the property the tenant is paying to lease.
  • Right of CONTROL
    • Gives the title holder the right to use the property in any way that is not illegal. They can control what's built on it, who enters it, the landscaping — all the good stuff. Anything that's not prohibited by a deed restriction or by the law can be controlled by the owner. The right of control also includes the right to encumber the property: to use it as collateral for a debt.
    • The difference between the right of possession and the right of control is that possession allows someone to be in and use the property, while control allows someone to make changes to the property. A renter has the right of possession, but probably not the right of control.

Property

  • ⤷ For our purposes, property is any item that may be owned. Keep in mind that when we talk about property, we're not just talking about buildings. Refrigerators, ceiling fans, cars, cans of tomato sauce, elaborate cat trees: These are all types of property, as well.
  • Real and Personal property - (More on Next Chapter)
  • Tangible property - possessions that physically exist, such as a house or a bagel
  • Intangible property: stocks, patents, and other incorporeal things
    • Though there is some intangible property in the real estate world (co-op buyers are really just buying ownership in a company, for example) for the most part, real estate is tangible

Conveyance

  • ⤷ The business of real estate is all about property changing hands (or being conveyed). As a real estate agent, you will mostly be concerned with the conveyance of real estate: how it changes hands from buyer to seller or landlord to tenant.
  • Asset When someone buys real estate and real estate is conveyed, real estate is an asset, or item of value. The buyer typically hopes their asset will increase in value, earning them equity.
  • Equity the amount of a property's value the title holder owns (property value minus debt). In other words, equity is all the value of a property above what an owner owes their lender or other debt-holder.
    • Ahmed bought a home that cost $200,000. He made a $20,000 down payment on that house, meaning that initially, his equity = $20,000. As he made more mortgage payments, his equity (or the amount of the house he actually owned) gradually increased
    • As an agent, you will be helping clients attain assets and gain equity in those assets
  • Legal Title Real property is conveyed by a written instrument called a deed, which gives the deed-holder legal title. Legal title is the complete legal ownership of real property and the bundle of rights associated with it. When you officially assume ownership of a property, it's often referred to as taking title.
  • Deed There are many types of ownership and ways of transferring those ownership rights, all of which we will cover later on. But, at the most basic level, a transfer of legal title (by deed) is how real property moves around.
    • Deeds are used to transfer estates, and they can even include restrictions on property use

Who Makes the Rules?

  • ⤷ Real estate is an industry where a lot of money moves around. Houses are expensive. And anywhere there is a lot of money changing hands, you'll find unscrupulous types trying to skim a little off the top. That's why there are lots of rules and regulations that govern the conveyance of real estate, and the real estate industry more generally
  • Federal Government - Regulates broad issues, such as general land usage, natural disaster relief, environmental legislation, and anti-discrimination legislation.
  • State Government The primary regulators of real estate law. They address things like water rights, local environmental rules, and mineral rights. State laws empower real estate commissions to create rules around real estate licensure, ethics, and practices.
  • Local Governments - Cover real estate on an even smaller scale, addressing issues such as land use control, taxation, and zoning.
  • Common Law Conveyance rules, and the rules of real estate in general, are largely governed by common law. Common law is the side of law that comes from decisions made in courts, rather than from laws passed by lawmakers. Think of common law as something that is generally accepted by society
  • As an agent, you'll need to pay particular attention to laws regarding things such as:
    • Fair housing rules
    • Agency
    • Contracts
    • Disclosure
    • Environmental regulations

History Lesson

  • This bundle of rights didn't always exist as we know it today. In the past, in many parts of the world, land was owned and used according to the feudal system.

Feudal System

  • was a system of land ownership in which all the land in the kingdom was owned by the head of the state (King/Queen)
  • The king would allow a few lords to use some of the land in exchange for taxes and allegiance. The lords, following the same system, would allow their vassals (peasants) to live on and work the land in exchange for military service and a portion of the crops.
  • Symbiotic Relationship
    • The feudal system created a kind of symbiotic relationship between the lords and the peasants. Lords let the peasants use the land to farm and raise livestock. The lords offered protection from anyone trying to conquer the land. The peasants, in return, had to give part of what they raised on the land to the lords. They were also required to serve in the lord's military if the lord went to war. Vassals and peasants had some rights in the property they used, but none had actual ownership. The king or queen owned everything.
  • Allodial System
    • Feudalism was a kind of abbreviated, or incomplete, land ownership. An individual’s ownership was tied to the lords of the land. An allodial, or “complete,” ownership system is one in which the land is owned completely without an obligation of services or duties to another. This new system is what most real estate ownership looks like today.
  • Land Ownership
    • In an allodial system, a person can have full ownership of their land, including all of the rights in the bundle of rights. They can also pass their land down to their heirs. This is called fee simple ownership. We're going to talk all about the different kinds of ownership a person can have in a property in a later level.

The Precious Table (Field Notes)

  • A real estate transaction fell apart because the buyer insisted a $200 dining room table be included in the sale, and the seller refused. Because the broker proactively negotiated this personal property request in the purchase offer, both parties avoided a disastrous post-sale dispute, and the home was eventually sold to a different buyer.
    • Put it in writing: Always clearly specify and document exactly which pieces of personal property (like furniture or specific appliances) will convey with the real estate.
    • Communicate proactively: Never assume the buyer and seller share the same expectations. Transparent communication during negotiations ensures everyone is on the same page before closing.
    • Expect the unexpected: Deals can collapse over minor, seemingly insignificant details. Treat every inclusion request with professional diligence.

Key Terms

  • Commercial real estate - wherein agents help rent or sell commercial property or find companies office space to rent.
  • Investment real estate - helping investors buy properties that will generate income for them.
  • Property development - working with a builder or developer to build new buildings
  • Property management - helping the owner of a residential or commercial building run and maintain their investment
  • Appraisal - assigning an official value to a property for a bank, lender, or individual
  • Business brokerage - helping with the purchase and sale of businesses
  • Financial and investment services - helping investment firms invest in real estate
  • Mortgage brokerage and banking - helping buyers find mortgages or servicing mortgages that have already been made
  • Real estate education - helping real estate students get their licenses like your ol' buddy Ace
  • Urban planning - studying land use and planning cities
  • Government work - working in real estate as part of the Bureau of Land Management, the EPA, HUD, and other federal agencies
  • Escrow services - arranging real estate escrow services and helping with closings
  • Title services - researching real estate titles and providing title insurance (two vital tasks you'll learn more about later in the course)
  • Note - (some of these jobs require extra licensure or schooling (for example, appraisers and mortgage brokers)
