Chapter 3 - Businesses and Trusts

The Business of Real Estate Ownership

  • Some clients buy a home to live in, while others buy property as an investment.
  • A CoreLogic study found that investors bought 11.8% of homes sold in 2018. Many investors purchase property as a business, organized as a sole proprietorship, partnership, or corporation. Understanding these structures is useful for clients and anyone interested in investing.
  • Business Legal Structures
    • Sole proprietorships
    • Partnerships (general and limited)
    • Corporations (C and S corps)
    • Limited liability companies (LLCs)
    • Limited liability partnerships (LLPs)
  • Sole Proprietorships
    • A sole proprietorship is owned by one person, or by a married couple treated legally as one person.
    • How Property Is Held
      • The business is not a separate property-owning entity; the owner holds property personally.
    • Liability
      • The owner is personally responsible for business profits, debts, and obligations, putting personal assets at risk.
      • It is easy to set up with little paperwork, but offers no liability protection or special tax benefits.
      • The business may use the owner’s name or another name properly registered with the state.
  • Sole Proprietorship Example: Orion Owns Buildings
    • Leti and James gave each child a small investment property at age 18. Orion expanded his studio condo into several rentals and kept them in his own name, even while managing larger Myers Holdings projects.
    • Fully Responsible
      • Orion operates as a sole proprietor under his own name, so no business-name filing was needed.
      • He is personally liable for property-related losses, debts, and obligations. His mother urges him to incorporate to reduce liability, but he prefers the risk and less paperwork.
  • Partnerships
    • A partnership is a for-profit business owned by two or more people who share its risks and rewards.
    • Uniform Partnership Act
      • In many states, the UPA and RUPA govern how partnerships are formed, operate, and dissolve. RUPA treats a partnership as a separate entity; Tennessee law allows it to hold property in its own name.
    • Owning Property as a Partnership
      • A partnership can sue, be sued, and hold property separately from partners’ personal property. Partnership property is separate from partners’ assets, and RUPA provides limited-liability protections to general partners in LLPs.
    • Partnerships: How Property Is Held
      • As Tenants in Partnership: The partnership owns the property as a separate entity, while partners share equal, undivided interests.
      • As Individual Partners: Partners hold property as tenants in common or joint tenants. A partner may transfer a share only with approval from all partnership members.
    • General vs. Limited Partnerships
      • Partnerships involve two or more people sharing the risks and rewards of a for-profit business. The two types are general and limited partnerships.
      • General Partnerships
        • General partners actively manage day-to-day operations and share unlimited personal liability for business debts and obligations.
        • Partners may use this structure to share the work and resources needed to manage investments. A partner’s death, bankruptcy, or withdrawal may require the partnership to dissolve or reorganize.
      • Limited Partnerships
        • A limited partnership has at least one general partner and one limited partner. Limited partners are primarily investors with restricted control and participation.
        • Limited partners’ liability is generally limited to their investment; general partners remain personally liable.
        • The partnership may continue after a partner dies or leaves if its agreement provides for it.
  • Corporations
    • Corporations are state-created legal entities that may be public or private, for-profit or nonprofit.
    • A Corporation Is a Person
      • The corporation is legally separate from its shareholders and can own real estate in severalty. Shareholders generally are not liable for corporate debts.
    • Who's Who in a Corporation
      • Shareholders: Own the corporation through stock.
      • Board of directors: Elected by shareholders to represent the owners.
      • Corporate officers: Manage day-to-day operations.
    • Corporations: Two Types
      • C Corporations
        • The most common corporate structure, C corps can be any size and may operate internationally.
        • They must hold annual shareholder meetings, publish annual reports, and meet other state reporting requirements; they can issue different classes of stock.
        • C corps face double taxation: the corporation pays income tax, and shareholders pay tax on dividends.
      • Subchapter S Corporations
        • S corps use pass-through taxation: shareholders report business income or losses on their personal returns, and the corporation generally pays no separate income tax.
        • S corps are limited to 100 shareholders, who must be U.S. citizens or legal residents.
  • Limited Liability Companies & Limited Liability Partnerships
    • LLCs and LLPs can own property and combine corporate-style liability protection with partnership-style pass-through taxation. State laws govern their formation and operation.
    • LLCs
      • An LLC is an unincorporated business owned by one or more members. It can have individuals, corporations, foreign entities, or other LLCs as members.
      • Members may manage the business or hire a manager. LLCs have fewer reporting requirements than corporations and generally use pass-through taxation.
      • Members are usually protected from business debts, though the liability shield is not absolute.
      • LLCs in Tennessee: Most businesses may form an LLC under the state LLC Act, except banks and insurance companies. Formation requires filing Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State. An operating agreement is recommended, though not required.
    • LLC Scenario: Constellation Properties, LLC
      • After a large tax bill and a tenant’s legal threat made his personal exposure clear, Orion moved his rental business from sole proprietorship to an LLC with help from a lawyer.
      • Advantages: The LLC can hold his properties and help separate business liabilities from personal assets. It offers pass-through taxation, fewer corporate reporting requirements, and possible self-employment tax savings depending on his tax election.
      • Costs: Orion pays setup and annual fees and takes on more reporting than a sole proprietor.
    • Key Takeaways
      • An LLC may combine liability separation with pass-through taxation, but protection is not absolute.
      • Weigh setup, annual fees, and reporting obligations against the benefits; consult legal and tax professionals about the right structure.
    • LLPs
      • An LLP is a partnership where all partners have limited liability; there is no general partner, and partners may participate in management.
      • Partners generally are not personally liable for another partner’s mistakes, though their investment remains at risk. LLP profits and losses pass through to partners’ tax returns.
      • LLPs in Tennessee: Formation requires filing a Registration Statement with the Secretary of State.
    • Both LLCs and LLPs: They combine a partial liability shield with pass-through taxation.
  • Trusts: Vehicles of Ownership Transference
    • A trust is a legal arrangement in which a grantor (trustor) transfers assets or property to a trustee to manage for a beneficiary.
    • Many Kinds of Trusts: Trusts range from simple will-related arrangements to complex planning tools. Setting one up typically requires legal, financial, and tax advice.
    • Trusts: The Three Roles
      • Trustor or grantor: Creates the trust and transfers the assets.
      • Trustee: Holds and manages the assets.
      • Beneficiary: Receives the trust’s benefits or assets.
      • Depending on the trust, one person may fill two or all three roles.
  • Trusts Require Trust
    • The trustor relies on the trustee to act ethically and follow the trust’s instructions.
    • Once designated, the trustee holds legal title to the trust assets until they pass to the beneficiary.
    • The trustee manages the property according to the trustor’s directions and may be sued for failing to do so.
  • Three Types of Trusts
    • Living trusts
      • Established while the trustor is alive (an inter vivos trust). The trustor may remain trustee and/or beneficiary to manage or benefit from the assets.
      • Name a backup trustee and beneficiary to take over if the trustor becomes incapacitated or dies.
      • Advantages
        • Avoids probate, which may cost 3–7% of property value in some states and may require separate proceedings for property in multiple states.
        • Lets a backup trustee manage assets if the trustor becomes incapacitated.
      • Two Types of Living Trusts
        • Revocable Trusts: The trustor can change or dissolve the trust, add or remove assets, and change trustees or beneficiaries. Property usually must be removed from the trust before it is sold.
          • Often supplements a will; property generally retains applicable capital-gains and mortgage-interest tax treatment.
        • Irrevocable Trusts: Usually cannot be changed after assets are transferred; the trustee holds them for the beneficiary.
          • Assets may receive estate-tax or creditor protection, but legal and tax effects vary—get professional advice.
        • Both types generally help avoid probate. A revocable trust becomes irrevocable when the trustor dies.
    • Testamentary Trusts
      • Created through a will while the trustor is alive, but takes effect after death. The trustor cannot serve as trustee or beneficiary.
      • Can set conditions for distributing assets, help manage heirs’ inheritances, or offer creditor protection—but does not avoid probate.
      • Gotta Go to Probate
        • Assets transfer into the trust only after death and remain part of the trustor’s estate, so they must go through probate.
        • Estate taxes may apply. If the estate lacks enough cash to pay them, the trustee may need to sell property.
    • Land Trusts
      • A land trust holds land long-term: the trustor transfers title to a trustee and names themself as beneficiary. The beneficiary guides management under a trust agreement or deed.
      • Why Use a Land Trust?
        • Land trusts may be used to avoid probate, shield property from certain liens or judgments, prevent partition, avoid marital co-interest, or keep ownership private.
        • Example: Walt Disney used multiple entities and trusts to acquire Florida land for Disney World discreetly, seeking to avoid price increases. Similar approaches may be used for large projects.
      • Conservation Land Trusts
        • Individuals or conservation groups hold land to prevent development and protect habitats, natural areas, or recreational space. Public access varies.
      • Community Land Trusts
        • A nonprofit owns land and leases lots long-term to homeowners, with the goal of keeping housing affordable permanently.

Key Terms

  • beneficiary — the person who receives benefits from a trust, will, insurance policy, or similar arrangement.
  • general partnership — a business where all co-owners actively share control, responsibilities, liability, and profits.
  • limited partnership — a business with managing general partners and passive limited partners whose liability is generally limited to their investment.
  • living trust — a trust created during the trustor’s life, transferring legal title to a trustee for a beneficiary.
  • sole proprietorship — a business owned by one person or by a married couple treated as one person.
  • trust — a legal arrangement in which a grantor (trustor) transfers control of assets to a trustee for a beneficiary.

Business Legal Structure

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Trusts: The Three Roles
    • Trustor: Creates the trust.
    • Trustee: Holds and manages the assets.
    • Beneficiary: Receives the benefits or assets.
  • Property
    • Property includes anything that can be owned and the rights tied to ownership; it may be tangible or intangible.
    • Tangible: Physical possessions, such as a house or a bagel.
    • Intangible: Nonphysical assets, such as stocks or patents.
  • Three Types of Trusts
    • Living trusts: Created while the trustor is alive.
      • Revocable trusts can be changed; irrevocable trusts generally cannot.
    • Testamentary trusts: Take effect when the trustor dies and do not avoid probate.
    • Land trusts: Hold land long-term and may be used for privacy, estate transfer, or other property-planning purposes.
