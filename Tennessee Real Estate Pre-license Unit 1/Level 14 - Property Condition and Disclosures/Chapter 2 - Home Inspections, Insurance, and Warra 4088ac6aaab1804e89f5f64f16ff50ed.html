Chapter 2 - Home Inspections, Insurance, and Warranties

The Purpose and Vital Role of Home Inspections

  • Objective: To uncover hidden physical faults, structural defects, and safety or value-affecting issues that matter greatly to buyers and mortgage lenders.
  • Deal-Maker or Breaker: A home inspection is a critical phase of the transaction that can heavily influence negotiations or completely alter a deal.
  • Financial Responsibility: Typically paid for by the buyer, though inspection costs and arrangements remain 100% negotiable.
  • The Agent's Absolute Duty: Always Recommend Inspections
    • Agents must strongly advise every buyer to obtain a general home inspection—regardless of whether the home is an older property or brand new construction.
    • Sellers' disclosure statements are never a substitute for a professional inspection.
    • Waiver Protocol: If a buyer stubbornly refuses to get an inspection against the agent's strong recommendation, the agent must secure a signed written statement from the buyer confirming they made that choice against professional advice.
  • Inspection Contingencies & Reports
    • Contingency Clauses: Contracts include inspection contingencies tied to strict expiration deadlines. Once reports are reviewed and resolved to the buyer's satisfaction, the transaction moves forward.
    • Generalists vs. Specialists: General inspectors assess broad functionality and safety, and will frequently recommend calling in specialized tradespeople (roofers, structural engineers, electricians, plumbers) for deep-dive evaluations if red flags appear.
    • Focus on "Utility": Inspectors are not evaluating the exact age, premium quality, or remaining lifespan of components; they assess utility—whether a system, appliance, or structural element performs its desired function properly at the time of inspection (e.g., a furnace that fails to heat has zero utility).
  • General Inspector Duties & Scope of Work
    • Core Responsibilities:
      • Operating fixed or permanently installed equipment and appliances.
      • Conducting visual inspections of accessible systems and components, including attics and crawl spaces.
      • Compiling and delivering a standard home inspection report.
    • Inspection Report Categories: Highlights issues spanning construction, design, mechanical components, electrical wiring and systems, and plumbing.
    • The Agent's Cross-Check Duty: One of your primary responsibilities as an agent is to compare the home inspection report directly against the seller's written property disclosure statement to catch discrepancies, omissions, or hidden contradictions.
    • Nature of the Inspection: Limited Visual Survey
      • The Professional Advantage: General inspectors bring trained eyes to spot critical flaws that ordinary buyers might easily miss.
      • Inherent Limitations: An inspection is strictly a limited visual survey, not an exhaustive forensic investigation. While inspectors can catch visible issues like foundation cracks or surface wood rot, they cannot inspect inaccessible areas (such as conditions hidden behind drywall, underground plumbing, or sealed wall cavities).
    • Limitations of General Home Inspections
      • Inherent Boundaries: Even highly experienced general inspectors cannot detect every flaw. Issues like hidden water damage, environmental toxins, or compromised sewer lines often require specialized tools and expert evaluation beyond a standard visual survey.
    • Specific Areas Beyond General Inspection Scope
      1. HVAC Deficiencies:
        • Inspectors may refuse to run air conditioners in freezing weather or heaters during extreme heat because operating units in adverse temperatures can permanently damage the equipment.
        • Solution: Advise clients with specific HVAC concerns to hire a licensed HVAC specialist for a dedicated inspection.
      1. Water Damage & Leaks:
        • Roof assessments are typically done from the ground or can be obstructed by weather conditions (snow, ice, fallen leaves).
        • Vacant homes can have dormant plumbing where dry pipes temporarily mask leaks that only reappear after days of active use. Cosmetic cover-ups (like fresh paint over water stains) can also easily hide ceiling and wall damage.
      1. Environmental Toxins:
        • Standard inspectors generally do not check for environmental hazards such as radon gas, asbestos, or lead-based paint.
        • If these toxins are discovered before closing, buyers can negotiate with the seller to cover remediation, containment, or abatement costs.
      1. Blocked or Damaged Sewer Lines:
        • Standard inspections evaluate drain pipe types and approximate ages, but cannot see inside underground lines.
        • For homes over 20 years old, buyers should invest a few hundred dollars in a specialized video sewer inspection to check for clogs or breaks, as homeowners are fully responsible for the private line connecting to the municipal main.
    • The Role of Specialized Inspections
      • Transitioning from General to Specialist: When a general home inspector uncovers potential red flags they are not professionally qualified or licensed to diagnose fully, they will recommend bringing in a specialized professional. This is a standard, highly common occurrence in real estate transactions.
      • The Structural Engineer Example: If an inspector notes concerning foundation cracks or shifting, they will typically advise the buyer to hire a structural engineer to perform a rigorous evaluation of the foundation's load-bearing integrity.
      • Best Practice for Buyers: Going beyond the standard general inspection to secure targeted specialist evaluations is the best way to safeguard against catastrophic, unexpected post-closing repair costs. While specialty inspections vary in price, many are surprisingly fast and affordable (and sometimes even free for initial estimates).
  • For Your Protection: Get a Home Inspection
    • You may find it helpful to present your buyer with this handy pamphlet published by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). The pamphlet outlines what the buyer can expect from a professional home inspection. Print yourself a few copies and keep them handy to give out to buyers. Thanks, HUD!
  • The Buyer's Agent's Role in the Inspection Process
    • Recommending Inspectors: Assisting the buyer in finding and selecting a qualified, reputable home inspector tailored to their needs.
    • Consulting on Reports: Meeting with the buyer and inspector to review, analyze, and discuss the findings in the home inspection report.
    • Repair Negotiations: Helping the buyer strategize and negotiate repair requests, credits, or price adjustments with the seller based on inspection findings.
    • Client Conversations & Professional Boundaries
      • Referring to Specialists: If a client or customer asks for professional opinions or technical diagnoses on inspection results, agents must refer them to qualified specialists (e.g., structural engineers, licensed plumbers, electricians, or attorneys) for unbiased, expert assistance.
      • No Unauthorized Legal Advice: Agents must strictly abstain from offering legal counsel or acting outside their licensing scope.
    • The Golden Rule of "Giving Opinions"
      • Moving Beyond "In My Opinion": While older training taught agents to prefix answers with "In my opinion...", modern practice requires grounding advice in verifiable facts or expert sources.
      • Cite the Source: When providing factual information, agents should name the source, not be the source (e.g., attributing data to a survey, public record, or professional inspector) or direct the client to hire an expert to secure the answer.
  • The Agent's Due Diligence Property Inspection
    • Purpose: A walk-through visual inspection performed by the agent to proactively discover material facts and red flags regarding a property's condition that the client might overlook.
    • The Liability Boundary: Agents are held accountable for material facts they should have been aware of through reasonable professional competence and diligence. However, liability only extends to known or discoverable facts—agents are never held liable for truly unknown or inaccessible conditions (e.g., pulling apart electrical switches or inspecting inside breaker boxes to uncover hidden aluminum wiring).
    • Verifying Material Facts
      • Cross-Checking Data: Agents have an affirmative obligation to verify information supplied by the seller or listing agent by cross-referencing disclosure statements, marketing listings, and objective public records.
      • The Red Flag Example: If a seller claims on the disclosure form that the roof has never leaked, but you spot visible water damage in a closet during your walk-through, you cannot simply ignore it. You have a duty to investigate, ask follow-up questions, or check for alternative causes (like a burst pipe).
      • Public Records Resources: Agents can verify property data (such as lot dimensions, easements, deed restrictions, zoning, utility costs, and tax assessments) by consulting official public records rather than taking verbal claims at face value.
  • Red Flags: Spotting Warning Signs of Underlying Property Issues
    • Old Plumbing: Low water pressure when turning on faucets can signal that the plumbing system is aging, degraded, or in urgent need of updating.
    • Sewer Problems: Standing water in the yard or basement flooding can point to serious underground sewer line breaks or blockages.
    • Worn Roofing: Water stains on ceilings signal roof leaks. Critical Warning: Freshly painted ceilings in a specific spot often indicate that a seller is attempting to cosmetically conceal water damage—consider this a major red flag!
    • Old Electrical Wiring: Older homes may contain hazardous aluminum wiring, presenting a significant hidden fire risk.
    • Pest Damage: Small piles of wood dust on the floor suggest carpenter ant infestations, while hollow-sounding wooden beams when knocked point strongly to termite damage.
    • Foundation Issues (The Big One): Cracks in foundations or basement floors larger than a quarter-inch, unexplained bumps in basement flooring, or sticking/misaligned door frames and windows are classic red flags signaling potential structural shifting or failure.
  • Distinguishing Red Flags from Cosmetic Defects
    • Cosmetic Flaws vs. Structural Red Flags: Minor surface imperfections (like a broken door handle or chipped paint) are not red flags. While they require repairs, they are low-cost cosmetic fixes that do not indicate larger, hidden structural failures.
  • Post-Inspection Decision Framework
    • Once the home inspection report is delivered, the buyer must evaluate the findings and determine their next course of action across five key fronts:
      1. Repairs Needed: Deciding which identified defects are serious enough to demand fixing.
      1. Responsible Party: Determining who will physically perform or pay for the repairs.
      1. Credits & Concessions: Evaluating whether financial credits or price reductions should be requested in lieu of physical repairs, and calculating exact amounts.
      1. Items to Ignore: Sorting out minor cosmetic wear or insignificant flaws that can safely be overlooked.
      1. Deal-Breakers: Identifying catastrophic structural, safety, or environmental issues that give the buyer grounds to walk away from the purchase entirely.
  • The Agent's Post-Inspection Role
    • Advisory Support: When inspection or appraisal reports uncover unexpected or concerning conditions, the buyer's agent provides experienced insights, information, and guidance to help the client navigate their options.
    • Buffer & Conflict Resolution: Acting as a professional buffer, the buyer's agent shields the client and handles negotiations directly with the seller and listing agent if tensions or disagreements arise over property condition issues.
  • Requesting Repairs
  • The Resolution Period & Repair Negotiations
    • Definition: Once a home inspection reveals issues, the buyer can submit a formal request for repairs, kicking off a negotiated window known as the "resolution period" (as outlined in the Purchase and Sale Agreement).
    • Written Request vs. Full Inspection Report: The buyer provides a specific written list of requested repairs, not the full inspection report. The inspection report is strictly for the buyer’s eyes and confidential use; handing the raw report over to the listing agent can create severe liability and disclosure complications.
    • What Is NOT Allowed on the Repair Request List
      • Buyers cannot demand just anything. Two major categories are strictly excluded:
        1. Cosmetic Repairs: Purely decorative, aesthetic updates that do not impact the core structural integrity or functionality of the home (e.g., outdated paint color, worn carpet, or scuffed walls).
        1. Building Code Upgrades: Buyers cannot demand repairs solely to bring an older home up to current modern building codes unless mandated by local government authorities. (Older homes are generally grandfathered in unless major unpermitted work or safety violations exist).
    • Seller Obligations and Deal Walkaways
      • No Automatic Duty to Repair: Sellers are never automatically obligated to agree to requested repairs; every repair, credit, or concession is a matter of open negotiation between the buyer and seller.
      • The Deal-Breaker Reality: If negotiations break down and the seller refuses to make critical repairs, the buyer may choose to walk away from the purchase using their inspection contingency. That is simply a natural part of the real estate transaction process.
    • The Disclosure Duty Triggered by Inspections
      • If a transaction falls apart—or if new defects are uncovered during the inspection—the seller's legal obligations shift.
      • New Material Facts: Any newly discovered physical defect or material fact brought to light during the inspection now becomes part of the seller's actual knowledge. Consequently, the seller must update their property disclosure statement to reflect these newly discovered issues before putting the home back on the market or selling to a new buyer.
  • Home Warranties vs. Hazard Insurance: Post-Closing Protection
    • Home Warranties:
      • A service contract offered by a third-party company covering the repair or replacement of major home appliances, electrical/plumbing systems, and HVAC units for a specified period (typically one year).
      • Requires an upfront annual fee and a small per-service call fee rather than full retail repair costs.
      • Systems must be in working condition at the time of purchase. Unexpired warranties can be transferred to a new buyer upon resale, or a new policy can be negotiated and purchased by either the buyer or seller.
    • Hazard Insurance (Homeowner's Insurance):
      • A strict necessity (and a mandatory requirement for any mortgaged property) protecting real property improvements against catastrophic losses (e.g., fire, severe storms, structural accidents).
      • Covers necessary repairs and replacements resulting from sudden damages, but never covers general wear and tear.
      • Buyers should always shop around to secure adequate coverage.
    • Flood Insurance Realities
      • The Standard Exclusion: Standard hazard/homeowners insurance does not cover flooding from hurricanes, tropical storms, or heavy rain.
      • Buyer Due Diligence Responsibilities: Buyers are personally responsible for:
        • Obtaining a flood certification or lender determination to see if the property sits within a Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA).
        • Reviewing official flood hazard maps.
        • Determining if flood insurance is required or necessary.
      • Lender Mandate: If the property lies in a designated flood zone and is financed with a mortgage, the lender will strictly require flood insurance.
      • Elevation Certificates: Newly constructed or improved properties located in a flood zone may require an official elevation certificate to prove the structure is elevated properly to minimize flood risks and secure insurance coverage.

Key Terms

  • hazard insurance -insurance against loss or damage to real property improvements; often required by lenders
  • property inspection - an inspection conducted to discover material facts or defects that exist within a property
  • utility - the measure of the degree to which something performs a desired function

Key Concepts & Principles

  • The Purpose of Home Inspections
    • The purpose of a home inspection is to locate any physical faults that may affect the safety or value of a home. This is information most buyers will want to know. And if they're financing the purchase, their lender will want to know as well.
    • The home inspection is a vital part of the home-buying process because it can reveal important defects and issues. The buyer typically pays for inspections, though this (like many things in real estate deals) is negotiable.
  • Inspection Reports - will list issues related to things like:
    • Construction
    • Design
    • Mechanical components
    • Electrical wiring and systems
    • Plumbing
    • Just remember that a home inspection is a limited visual survey. It's not a comprehensive investigation. As such, home inspectors reveal readily seen things like foundation cracks and wood deterioration. What they cannot do, however, is inspect inaccessible areas of the home.
  • After the Inspection
    • After they see the results of the home inspection, buyers can terminate the agreement, accept the property as is, or request repairs from the seller. When buyers make repair requests, they waive their objections to cosmetic issues. They also do not have the right to request repairs purely to meet current building code unless the government requires them to do so.
  • Home Warranties and Insurance
    • A home warranty is a product offered by a company that maintains, repairs, or replaces any applicable component of a home for a specified period of time. This usually applies to major home appliances, electrical and plumbing systems, and the heating and air conditioning systems of a residence. Coverage differs from warranty to warranty.
    • Hazard insurance, which is more likely to be required, is insurance against loss or damage to real property improvements. It's generally limited to necessary replacements and repairs resulting from damages. Some natural disasters and accidents will be covered, but general wear and tear won't be. Policies vary, so encourage buyers to shop around and make sure they can secure adequate insurance for the property they're buying.
