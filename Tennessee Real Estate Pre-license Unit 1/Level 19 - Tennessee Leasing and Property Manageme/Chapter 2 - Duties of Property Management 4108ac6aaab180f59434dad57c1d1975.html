Chapter 2 - Duties of Property Management

Leasing Roles & Tenant Relationships

  • Lessor vs. Lessee: The property owner (or the property manager acting on their behalf) is the lessor (landlord) who grants the lease, while the tenant renting the property is the lessee.
  • Retention & Profitability: A rental property's profitability depends directly on acquiring qualified tenants and retaining them. High turnover erodes net income through lost rent during vacancies and increased marketing/turnover costs. While property managers cannot change a building's physical location, they directly control policies, communication, and living conditions to keep tenants satisfied before they choose to vacate at lease expiration.
  • Strategic Property Marketing Plans
    • To minimize vacancies and attract ideal tenants, property managers develop a structured marketing plan containing:
      • Market Analysis: Evaluates the subject property alongside regional and neighborhood characteristics (transportation access, zoning regulations, population demographics, comparable properties, and local vacancy/demand rates) to set competitive, profitable rental rates.
      • Marketing Budget: Allocates financial resources across specific advertising channels based on current vacancy levels.
      • Target Market, Goals & Mission Statement: Defines the ideal tenant profile and establishes clear occupancy objectives.
  • Fair Housing & Legal Marketing Compliance
    • Anti-Discrimination Laws: All property marketing and tenant selection practices must strictly comply with federal, state, and local fair housing and civil rights laws—prohibiting discrimination based on protected characteristics (such as race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national origin).
    • Equal Housing Opportunity: Marketing graphics and advertisements should display the Equal Housing Opportunity logo to affirm compliance with fair housing guidelines.
  • Property Marketing Channels & Strategies
    • Property managers utilize multiple channels to reach prospective tenants, tailoring tactics to the property type and target audience:
      • Print & Social Media Ads: Local newspapers and targeted digital platforms (such as Facebook) to reach specific demographics.
      • Word of Mouth & Referral Programs: Leveraging reputation and incentivizing existing tenants (e.g., offering a $50 rent credit) for successful tenant referrals.
      • On-Site Signage: High-visibility signs displaying clear contact information.
      • Brochures & Direct Mail: Concise, visually appealing flyers distributed locally.
      • Property Websites: User-friendly sites featuring high-quality property photos and clear contact details.
      • Press Releases: Media announcements highlighting major upgrades, new amenities, or notable property features.
  • Evaluating Marketing Effectiveness: Cost Per Prospect
    • The Concept: Property managers must continually track lead sources and weigh marketing expenditures against actual prospect generation to maximize return on advertising spend and adjust the marketing plan accordingly.
    • The Formula Cost of advertisement ÷ Number of prospects = Cost per prospect
  • Worked Examples & Campaign Comparison

  • Strategic Takeaway: Documenting cost-per-prospect metrics within the marketing plan allows the property manager to cut underperforming channels (such as expensive brochures or billboards yielding low leads) and reallocate funds toward high-converting platforms to meet the owner's occupancy goals.
  • Evaluating Prospective Tenants (Tenant Screening)
    • Objective Evaluation via Standard Lease Applications: Property managers screen applicants consistently using a standardized lease application to assess financial qualification and property fit without bias.
    • Information Collected on the Application:
      • Basic Identification: Full name, contact details, government ID number, and date of birth.
      • Financial & Background Verification: Employment status, income verification, credit history, personal/landlord references, and pet ownership.
      • Risk & Safety Screening: Permitted checks on criminal background, bankruptcy history, and prior evictions to ensure community safety and financial reliability.
    • Legal Compliance: Screening criteria must strictly avoid protected classes (race, gender, religion, national origin, disability, familial status, etc.) in compliance with federal and state fair housing laws.
  • Executing Lease Agreements
    • Once qualified applicants are selected, the property manager (acting as lessor on the owner's behalf) executes a written lease agreement with each tenant (lessee).
    • The lease establishes property rules and regulations, mutual expectations, payment obligations, and legal remedies (and may involve negotiated terms depending on the tenancy).
  • Setting Competitive Rental Rates
    • Financial & Market Alignment: Establishing rent requires balancing the property's financial requirements against local market conditions to achieve maximum occupancy and return on investment (ROI).
    • Key Factors in Rate Determination:
      • Operating Budget Coverage: Rental income must cover ongoing operating expenses, pay fixed charges (such as property taxes, insurance, and debt service), and yield a fair profit for the owner.
      • Competitive Market Research ("Sleuthing"): Analyzing comparable local properties—comparing asking rents, interior quality, offered amenities, and move-in concessions.
      • Annual Adjustments: Reviewing and adjusting rates annually to reflect shifting market demand and expense inflation.
    • Finding the Optimal Pricing Balance:
      Pricing StrategyConsequenceFinancial Impact
      Rent Set Too HighExtended vacancies and slow lease-upLost rental income and lower overall profitability
      Rent Set Too LowRapid occupancy, but revenue falls shortFails to cover operating costs and reduces owner ROI
      Balanced Market RateCompetitive with area comps while covering expensesMaximizes occupancy, covers fixed/operating costs, and optimizes ROI
  • The Rent Roll
    • Definition: A comprehensive management report acting as a master roster of a property's rental units, tenants, and financial standing.
    • Core Information Included on a Rent Roll:
      • Total number of units in the property (including occupied vs. vacant units).
      • Identities of current tenants occupying each unit.
      • Lease terms (exact start and expiration dates of each lease).
      • Monthly rental income generated by each unit.
      • Any outstanding or past-due balances owed by tenants.
  • Rent Collection & Delinquency Management
    • Proactive Rent Collection: Because a property manager's primary fiduciary duty is to generate and protect income for the property owner, managers must actively monitor past-due accounts on the rent roll and promptly execute structured collection plans when tenants fail to pay on time.
    • Early Warning Indicators: Late or partial payments frequently signal deeper financial distress and future collection challenges, requiring immediate, consistent follow-up.
  • Delinquent Account Report
    • Purpose: A specialized financial report submitted to the property owner detailing all tenant accounts that have fallen into delinquency (past-due status) and the exact amounts owed.
    • Additional Financial Disclosures: Beyond overdue rent balances, the delinquent account report typically documents any ongoing legal proceedings (such as eviction filings, collection lawsuits, or tenant disputes) that may impact the property's cash flow and financial performance.

  • Accommodating Unique Tenants: Construction & Alterations
    • The Concept: Because individual tenants have distinct operational and physical requirements, property managers frequently coordinate structural modifications, updates, or safety improvements to rental units.
    • Execution & Oversight: Depending on the scale of work and property size, alterations may be completed by on-site maintenance staff or outsourced to third-party contractors—in all cases, the property manager should actively supervise the work to ensure quality, budget compliance, and safety.

    • Definition: Structural upgrades, additions, or replacements that fall outside the scope of normal repairs and routine maintenance, typically undertaken to substantially increase the property's value, prolong its useful life, or adapt it to new uses.
    • Common Examples:

      • Constructing a new backyard deck
      • Finishing an unfinished basement
      • Replacing an entire roof (as opposed to patching a leak)

  • Financial Objectives of a Property Manager
    • Property managers are tasked with achieving and documenting five primary financial goals for the owner:
      1. Preserving the physical and market value of the property.
      1. Controlling and minimizing operating expenses.
      1. Generating consistent primary income through rent collection.
      1. Maximizing ancillary income beyond rent (e.g., laundry facilities, parking fees, vending, pet fees).
      1. Fulfilling any specialized financial stipulations outlined in the property management agreement.
  • Financial Reporting & The Property Management Report
    • Core Reporting Metrics: Financial reports are prepared at regular intervals specified in the management agreement to communicate profit, loss, revenue, expenditures, and tax data.
    • The Property Management Report: A comprehensive monthly report prepared by the property manager for the owner detailing all property income, operating expenses, and disbursements. It ensures accurate accounting and guides rent-setting decisions so rental revenue adequately covers property costs.
  • Foundational Financial Accounting Concepts
    • Before generating detailed financial reports, property managers must track four core financial building blocks:
      • Income: Funds received by the property (categorized into Gross Income and Net Operating Income).
      • Expenses: Ongoing costs required to operate and maintain the property.
      • Capital Expenditures (CapEx): Major long-term structural investments or upgrades that add value or extend the property's useful life.
      • Cash Reserve: Dedicated funds set aside from property revenue to cover unexpected repairs, emergencies, or future capital expenditures.
  • Categories of Property Income
    • Property income is broken down into two primary tiers:
      1. Gross Income:
        • Potential Gross Income (PGI): The maximum total rental revenue a property could generate if 100% of units were occupied at full market rent with zero collection losses.
        • Effective Gross Income (EGI): The actual gross revenue realized after subtracting vacancy losses and bad-debt/collection losses from Potential Gross Income, plus any non-rental ancillary income.
      1. Net Operating Income (NOI): The net cash flow remaining after deducting all operating expenses from Effective Gross Income (calculated prior to mortgage debt service and income taxes).
    • Potential Gross Income (PGI) vs. Effective Gross Income (EGI)
      • Gross Income: The total top-line revenue generated by an income-producing property, broken down into theoretical maximum capacity (PGI) and actual realized revenue (EGI).
      • Potential Gross Income (PGI / Gross Potential Income): The maximum rental revenue a property could generate if 100% of its units were leased at full market rate with zero vacancies or collection losses.
      • Effective Gross Income (EGI): The actual gross revenue a property realizes after subtracting vacancy and collection losses from PGI, and adding any ancillary non-rental income (e.g., laundry, parking, vending, or late fees).
    • Formulas
      • Total units × Market rent per unit = Potential Gross Income
      • Potential Gross Income - Vacancy and collection losses + Ancillary income = Effective Gross Income
    • Worked Scenario Comparison (20-Unit Apartment Building at $600/Unit)
      Income MetricOccupancy StatusMath CalculationResulting Income
      Potential Gross Income (PGI)100% Occupied (20 of 20 units leased; 0% vacancy)20 units × $600 = $12,000$12,000
      Effective Gross Income (EGI)50% Occupied (10 of 20 units leased; 50% vacancy)$12,000 - $6,000 vacancy loss = $6,000 (or 10 units × $600 = $6,000)$6,000
  • Net Operating Income (NOI) & Cash Flow
    • Net Operating Income (NOI): Measures a property's actual profitability from operations by subtracting all necessary operating expenses from effective gross income (prior to mortgage debt service and income taxes).
    • NOI Formula (Plaintext Formatting):Effective gross income - Operating expenses = Net operating income
    • Cash Flow: Measures the total net movement of money flowing into and out of a property over a given period, tracked via periodic cash flow reports issued to the owner:
      • Positive Cash Flow (+): Indicates the property is generating net profit after all expenses and debt obligations.
      • Negative Cash Flow (-): Indicates the property is losing money.
  • Calculating Operating Cash
    • Definition: Cash generated from the property's core operations after covering both operating expenses and debt service (principal and interest payments on property loans).
    • Operating Cash Formula (Plaintext Formatting):Income - Expenses - Debt service = Operating cash
    • Worked Example:
      • Given Variables: Annual Income = $864,000 | Operating Expenses = $500,000 | Annual Debt Service = $15,000
      • Step-by-Step Calculation: $864,000 - $500,000 - $15,000 = $349,000
      • Result: The property generates $349,000 in operating cash.

  • Core Property Management Financial Reports
    • Property managers regularly generate two primary categories of financial reporting to track performance and guide owner decision-making:
      1. Profit and Loss (P&L) Statement
      1. Property Budgets (Operating budget, Stabilized budget, Capital reserve budget, and Budget comparison statement)
  • Profit and Loss (P&L) Statement
    • Purpose: Compares a property's total revenue against its total expenditures over a specific reporting period to determine bottom-line profitability.
    • The P&L Formula :Gross receipts - Operating expenses - Total mortgage payment = Net profit
    • Component Breakdown:
      • Gross Receipts: Total funds collected by the property before subtracting any costs (includes rent plus ancillary revenue such as on-site cafe sales, parking, or laundry).
      • Operating Expenses: Recurring day-to-day costs required to run and maintain the property (taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management fees). Note: Investment properties generally itemize property taxes and insurance separately, though smaller properties may occasionally group them.
      • Total Mortgage Payment: Full debt service obligations (principal and interest) paid during the period.
  • Worked Example: Six-Month Profit and Loss Calculation
    • Given Variables (Six-Month Period):
      • Gross Receipts (Rent + Cafe Sales): $825,000
      • Operating Expenses: $270,000
      • Total Mortgage Payment: $500,000
    • Step-by-Step Calculation:
      • $825,000 - $270,000 - $500,000 = Net profit
      • $825,000 - $270,000 - $500,000 = $55,000
    • Result: The property generated a net profit of $55,000 over the six-month period.
  • Property Management Budgeting Fundamentals
    • Purpose: Establishes financial discipline and forecasts expected cash flows tailored to the specific property asset type.
    • Essential Components of a Property Budget:
      • Specific Dates & Timeframes: Clear start/end dates and monetary totals corresponding to each period.
      • Revenue Specifications & Totals: Itemized projections from residential/commercial rents and ancillary operating incomes.
      • Expenditure Specifications & Totals: Line-item allocations for recurring operating expenses (insurance, security, routine maintenance, utilities, taxes) and reserves.
    • Primary Types of Property Budgets:
      • Operating Budget: Forecasts day-to-day recurring revenues and operating expenses over a set period (typically one year).
      • Stabilized Budget: Projects long-term income and expenses assuming the property has achieved normal, steady occupancy and operational stability.
      • Capital Reserve Budget: Allocates funds into a replacement reserve for major non-recurring capital expenditures (CapEx) like roof replacements or HVAC overhauls.
      • Budget Comparison Statement: Compares budgeted projections against actual financial performance to identify variances and adjust operations.
    • Property Management Budget Types
      • Property managers prepare distinct budgets tailored to different planning horizons, cash-flow variability, and operational needs.
    • Three Core Property Management Budgets
      Budget TypeTime HorizonPrimary Purpose & FocusKey Planning Considerations
      Operating Budget1 Year (Short-term / Forward-looking)Itemizes anticipated revenues and recurring operating expenses for the upcoming yearRelies on historical operating statements from prior periods to accurately project and allocate future costs
      Stabilized Budget5 Years (Medium-to-Long-term)Averages a property's income and expenses over a 5-year period to reflect normal, steady operationsCreated after establishing baseline budgets and completing a comprehensive market analysis
      Capital Reserve BudgetVariable / Seasonal & Long-term ReserveAccounts for irregular, fluctuating, or seasonal variable expensesForecasts spikes in operational demand (e.g., peak January heating costs assuming 100% occupancy) and sets aside dedicated reserve funds
  • Operating Statement vs. Operating Budget
    • Core Distinction: In property finance, a budget is a forward-looking plan, whereas a statement is a historical record.
    • Operating Statement: A retrospective financial record that itemizes every actual revenue and expense over the previous year (or broken down month-by-month) to measure past property performance.
      • Key Metrics Listed:
        • Total Income: Gross rental income plus ancillary non-rental income.
        • Operating Expenses: Itemized historical costs incurred to operate and maintain the property.
        • Net Operating Income (NOI): Total income - Operating expenses = Net operating income (plaintext formatting).
  • Budget Comparison Statements
    • Purpose: Evaluates actual financial results against the projected budget created prior to the start of the year, allowing the property manager to measure variances, analyze cash flow, and verify whether operations align with the owner's goals.
    • Strategic Value: Helps correct overly optimistic or aggressive forecasts by benchmarking projected figures against actual performance so future budgets align with financial reality.
    • Worked Example (Budget Variance):
      • Planned Annual Budget: $175,000
      • Actual Annual Spending: $225,000
      • Budget Variance: $225,000 - $175,000 = $50,000 over budget
      • Management Action: Because sustained overspending undermines the owner's long-term return on investment, the property manager must investigate cost overruns and recalibrate future budgets and expense controls.

  • Major Federal Laws Governing Landlords and Property Managers
    • Property managers and landlords must strictly comply with federal civil rights and accessibility statutes in both employment and leasing operations.
  • Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990
    • Purpose: Prohibits discrimination against individuals with disabilities and mandates equal access to employment and public spaces.
    • Two Key Titles Relevant to Property Management:
      • Title I (Employment): Prohibits employers from discriminating against qualified job applicants and employees with disabilities, requiring equal employment opportunities and reasonable workplace accommodations.
      • Title III (Public Accommodations & Commercial Facilities): Requires all public accommodations and commercial facilities to be accessible and usable by individuals with disabilities (e.g., providing accessible leasing offices, widening doorways for wheelchairs, or offering accommodations for applicants with visual impairments).
    • Manager Responsibility: Property managers must evaluate how existing facilities and operational procedures measure up against ADA standards and consult with the property owner to implement required modifications.
  • The Federal Fair Housing Act
    • Purpose: Prohibits discrimination in housing-related transactions (renting, leasing, advertising, setting terms, or providing services) and requires all applicants to be treated equally.
    • Enforcement: Enforced by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), which regularly conducts fair housing testing to detect unlawful practices. Violations carry severe legal and financial penalties, including the revocation of a real estate license.
    • The Seven Federal Protected Classes:
      1. Religion
      1. Race
      1. Color
      1. National origin
      1. Sex (including gender identity and sexual orientation)
      1. Disability
      1. Familial status (families with children under 18, pregnant individuals, or persons securing custody)

  • The Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (URLTA)
    • Origin & Purpose: Drafted in 1972 by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws (now known as the Uniform Law Commission), URLTA is a model law designed to standardize, clarify, and modernize the legal rights and obligations of landlords and tenants in residential housing.
    • Scope & Adoption: URLTA applies exclusively to residential leases (not commercial, industrial, or agricultural properties). Because it is a model act, states choose whether to adopt it in whole or in part into their state statutes—including Tennessee, which modeled its own Residential Landlord and Tenant Act on URLTA.
    • Relevance to Property Managers: Because property managers act in a landlord capacity on behalf of property owners, they are legally bound to uphold URLTA's statutory standards in daily leasing and operations.
  • Core Mutual Obligations Under URLTA
    • URLTA treats a residential lease as a bilateral contract with interdependent duties—meaning a landlord's right to collect rent is tied to their obligation to maintain habitable housing.

      • Maintain electrical, plumbing, sanitary, HVAC, and supplied appliances in safe working order

      • Provide proper waste receptacles and arrange for garbage removal      		Prohibited from using lease clauses that waive tenant statutory rights, limit landlord liability, or allow rent collection free of maintenance obligations; must also respect tenant privacy (giving advance notice before non-emergency entry) and prohibit retaliatory conduct.

  • Tennessee Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (URLTA) Overview
    • Statutory Purpose (T.C.A. Title 66, Chapter 28): Establishes uniform landlord-tenant law in Tennessee, defines mutual obligations of landlords and tenants, promotes equal protection for all parties to a rental agreement, and ensures safe, habitable, and well-maintained residential housing.
    • Geographic & Property Applicability:
      • Applies to the rental of residential dwelling units exclusively in Tennessee counties with a population of more than 75,000 residents. (In counties with 75,000 or fewer residents, general Tennessee common law and Title 66, Chapter 7 govern unless otherwise opted in).
  • Statutory Exclusions from the Act
    • The Tennessee Residential Landlord and Tenant Act does not apply to the following living arrangements:
      1. Institutional Residence: Public or private institutional housing incidental to detention or the provision of medical, geriatric, educational, counseling, religious, or similar services (e.g., dormitories, hospitals, nursing facilities).
      1. Occupancy Under a Contract of Sale: Occupancy by a buyer (or successor in interest) under an active contract to purchase the dwelling unit or property.
      1. Transient Occupancy: Short-term stays in hotels, motels, or transient lodgings subject to state/local room occupancy taxes under the Excise Tax Act.
      1. Condominium & Cooperative Owners: Occupancy by the owner of a condominium unit or the holder of a proprietary lease in a cooperative.
      1. Agricultural Premises: Rental agreements covering premises used primarily for agricultural purposes.
      1. Government & Non-Profit Housing: Housing owned by a government entity or non-profit corporation regulated directly by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

Key Terms

  • capital expenditure (CapEx) - an expense paid in order to improve a property
  • capital improvement - upgrades or improvements to a property that fall outside the scope of normal repair and maintenance, usually with the intention of increasing value
  • cash flow - measures the amount of cash flowing in and out of a business
  • corrective maintenance - the repair of equipment after it has broken
  • preventative maintenance - conducting periodic inspections to catch minor issues before they become major failures
  • routine maintenance - monitoring, assessing, and attending to the needs of a facility's most necessary features

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Marketing Rental Property
    • Depending on a property's vacancy rate, property managers may have to do a lot of marketing, or maybe just a little. Whatever their situation, they should create a marketing plan that includes:
      • A market analysis
      • A budget
      • An outline of their marketing tactics (i.e., a budget for how much money will be spent on each marketing strategy)
      • The marketing plan will also outline things like the property's target market, goals, and mission statement.
  • Calculating Cost per Prospect
    • Always weigh the cost of advertising and marketing against its own usefulness. A good way to do this is by using the following formula to find the cost per prospect.
    • Cost of advertisement ÷ Number of prospects = Cost per prospect
  • Rent Roll
    • A property manager will be responsible for a report known as a rent roll. A rent roll is a report that lists:
      • Number of units in a property
      • Identities of tenants
      • Lease terms (the start and end date of each lease)
      • Monthly rental income from each unit
      • Any outstanding balances owed by tenants
  • Maintenance
    • Maintenance generally falls into three categories:
      • Routine maintenance
      • Corrective maintenance
      • Preventative maintenance
  • Income
    • In a broad sense, gross income is the total amount of revenue produced by a property.
    • Potential gross income is the revenue a property could generate if 100% of the units in the building were leased at market rate. This means that all units are occupied by tenants and there are no vacancies. (This number may also be called "gross potential income" or "GPI.")
    • Effective gross income is the amount of money a property generates after taking into account other factors such as vacancy losses and additional revenue from sources other than rent.
    • Net operating income is the measurement of revenue (in other words, effective gross income) from the property minus all necessary operating expenses.
  • The Residential Landlord and Tenant Act
    • Under the Landlord & Tenant Act, landlords and tenants each have specific obligations they must follow. In Tennessee, landlords are obligated to:
    • Properly handle security deposits
    • Maintain fit premises
    • Provide a written rental agreement (unless the tenancy is month-to-month)
