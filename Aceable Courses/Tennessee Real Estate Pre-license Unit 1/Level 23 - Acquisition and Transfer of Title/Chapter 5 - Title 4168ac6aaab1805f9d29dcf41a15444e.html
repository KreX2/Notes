Chapter 5 - Title

Title (Abstract Concept vs. Physical Deed)

  • Title: An abstract legal concept—not a physical document or piece of paper—that represents a party's legal ownership rights to a specific parcel of real property. (By contrast, the deed is the physical written document used to transfer that title.)
  • Legal Title vs. Equitable Title
    Type of TitleDefinition & Legal NaturePractical Examples
    Legal TitleComplete legal ownership of real property and the full bundle of legal rights associated with it (possession, control, enjoyment, exclusion, and disposition).Held by the property owner of record on the deed (or by a third-party trustee under a deed of trust until a loan is repaid).
    Equitable TitleA beneficial ownership interest created by a financial investment or contractual right in a property, asserting the right to obtain full legal title once specific conditions are met.• Held by a lender/lienholder with a financial security interest in the property

    • Held by a buyer under an executed purchase contract or installment land contract prior to receiving the final deed
  • Marketable Title & Title Issues
    • Marketable Title: A title that is free from significant encumbrances, liens, or defects that would create reasonable doubt about ownership or prevent a buyer from peacefully enjoying or eventually reselling the property.
    • Not Legally Required, But Strongly Preferred:
      • Having a marketable title is not a strict legal prerequisite to transferring real estate, but it is standard in purchase contracts because title defects can restrict ownership rights, deter mortgage lenders, and reduce property value.
      • Pre-Closing Verification Rule: A buyer must verify the status of title before accepting delivery of the deed—once the deed is accepted, resolving newly discovered title defects typically requires formal legal action (such as a quiet title lawsuit).
  • Title Search Fundamentals
    • Purpose & Who Conducts It: Performed by a title company (or title attorney), a title search is a thorough examination of available public records in the county where the property is located to verify who holds legal rights to the parcel and whether any defects exist in the chain of title.
    • Timeframe Covered:
      • Examiners trace conveyance records backward in time starting from the current owner—potentially all the way back to the property's original source of title.
      • While state statutes establish the minimum required search period, a standard title search typically covers 40 to 60 years.
    • Public Records Examined During a Title Search:
      • Wills (and probate estate records)
      • Judicial proceedings (lawsuits, judgments, divorces, foreclosures)
      • Tax records (unpaid property taxes or tax liens)
      • Special assessments (municipal improvement assessments)
      • Recorded liens, including lien priority (mortgages, deeds of trust, mechanic's liens)
      • Encumbrances that may affect title (easements, restrictive covenants, mineral leases)
  • Marketability & Chain of Title
    • Chain of Title: The complete, unbroken chronological history of a property's ownership from its original government grant (land patent) all the way up to the present-day owner—essentially the "genealogy" of a piece of real estate.
    • How a Chain of Title Is Traced:
      • Title examiners search public grantor-grantee indexes backward in time, starting with the current owner and linking each grantor to the preceding grantee in a continuous recorded sequence.
      • If a link in the chain is missing (e.g., an unrecorded deed or an unprobated estate transfer), it creates a cloud on the title that can render the title unmarketable until resolved (often through a quitclaim deed or a quiet title lawsuit).
    • Adversing the Title: The formal process of combing through public land, tax, and court records to uncover any adverse claims, liens, encumbrances, or competing ownership interests against a property.

  • Marketability: Cloud on the Title
    • Cloud on the Title: Any claim, encumbrance, lien, or defect that casts doubt on the validity of a property owner's title and renders the title unmarketable until cleared.
    • Title Chain Gap & Suit to Quiet Title:
      • Title Chain Gap: Occurs when a link in the chronological ownership history is missing (such as an unrecorded deed or unprobated inheritance), creating a broken chain of title and a cloud on the title.
      • Suit to Quiet Title: A court lawsuit filed by the property owner against anyone holding a potential claim on the land to clear the title record of unrecorded or competing claims. Once the owner wins a quiet title suit, no further challenges to that title issue can be brought.
    • Other Common Clouds on Title:
      • Fraud (e.g., forged signatures or fraudulent conveyances)
      • Liens (unpaid mortgages, tax liens, mechanic's liens, or judgment liens)
      • Inheritance issues (unprobated wills or unknown/missing heirs)
      • Foreclosure proceedings
      • Public record errors (clerical indexing mistakes or misspelled names)
      • Survey/boundary concerns (encroachments or conflicting property descriptions)
    • Scenario Application (Mary and Caitlan):
      • When Mary dies without her estate being probated and her caretaker niece Caitlan simply takes the physical deed, Caitlan lacks clear, marketable title on the public record.
      • Before Caitlan can sell, she may need to locate all other relatives/heirs who could have an inheritance claim so they can sign off (typically via quitclaim deeds) releasing their interest, and her real estate agent should ask probing questions and advise Caitlan to consult a real estate attorney.
    • Color of Title:
      • Refers to a title or written instrument that appears valid on its face but is actually legally defective in some way.
      • Can arise from adverse possession, errors in current or prior deeds, or fraud.
  • Abstract of Title & The Role of the Abstractor
    • Abstract of Title: An abbreviated, chronological written summary of a property's title history—detailing all recorded transfers, grants, wills, conveyances, liens, and encumbrances (along with their current status) uncovered during a title search, plus a bibliography reflecting the scope of records examined.
    • The Abstractor's Role & Strict Limitation:
      • The abstractor searches public records from the original grant (when possible) to the present day and compiles the factual history in chronological order.
      • No Legal Opinions Offered: Abstractors do not evaluate title quality or issue legal opinions on marketability—an attorney must review the completed abstract of title and render a formal legal opinion on the title's condition.
  • Official Evidence of Title
    • Evidence of title provides constructive or actual proof of real property ownership and helps uncover or protect against title defects. The four primary forms are title insurance, an attorney's opinion of title, a certificate of title, and a Torrens certificate:
      Form of Title EvidenceWhat It Is & How It WorksScope of Protection & Limitations
      Opinion of TitleAn official legal opinion written by an attorney evaluating the condition and marketability of a property's title after examining the abstract of title and public records.Reflects the attorney's professional judgment based on recorded documents; does not insure against hidden/unrecorded defects.
      Certificate of TitleA written document prepared by an attorney after a title search that details the chain of title and certifies the attorney's opinion on the marketability of the seller's title.Proves record ownership based strictly on public records, but does NOT guarantee ownership and offers no defense against hidden defects or unrecorded liens.
      Torrens CertificateUsed in states operating the Torrens system, where the state government itself maintains the official register of land ownership. Title passes only when the deed is registered directly onto the certificate of title, and a new Torrens certificate is issued.• Offers the strongest, supreme, unassailable evidence of title where used.

      • Prevents adverse possession (Torrens-registered land cannot be lost to adverse possession).

      • Not used in Tennessee.
      Title InsuranceAn insurance policy issued after a title search that indemnifies the policyholder against financial loss caused by covered title defects, liens, or hidden clouds on title.Protects against both recorded oversights and hidden/unrecorded defects (such as forgeries or undisclosed heirs) that opinions and certificates of title cannot cover.
  • Title Insurance Overview
    • Definition: A specialized insurance policy (also called a title policy or title insurance policy) that protects lenders—and optionally homebuyers—against financial losses caused by pre-existing title issues, such as defects, encumbrances, and liens.
    • One-Time Upfront Payment: Unlike standard insurance policies with recurring monthly or annual premiums, title insurance is non-recurring and paid upfront in one lump sum at closing.
    • Retrospective Coverage (Past Events): Whereas traditional insurance covers future events, title insurance insures against problems that occurred before the date of purchase but are discovered after closing.
    • Go-To Evidence of Title in Tennessee: Unless the Torrens system is used, an active title insurance policy is widely accepted as the best evidence of marketable title and serves as the standard form of title evidence in Tennessee.
  • Purchasing Title Insurance & Tennessee Cost Conventions
    • Buyer's Right to Choose: Buyers have the legal right to select any title agent or title company they prefer—they are never required to use the company recommended by their real estate agent or mortgage lender.
    • Rate Regulation in Tennessee: Title insurance rates in Tennessee are regulated and set by the Tennessee Commissioner of Insurance and filed with the Department of Commerce and Insurance.
    • Who Pays (Negotiable Custom):
      • Seller typically pays for: The owner's policy and the county title examination fee.
      • Buyer typically pays for: The lender's policy (which is mandatory whenever a mortgage loan is used).
      • (Note: Payment allocation is always negotiable between buyer and seller in the purchase contract.)
  • Title Commitment (Preliminary Report)
    • Definition: Before issuing a final policy, the title company examines public records and issues a title commitment (or preliminary report) stating the exact terms and conditions under which it agrees to insure the title.
    • Core Contents of a Title Commitment:
      • Name of the insured party
      • Legal description of the property
      • Estate or interest covered (e.g., fee simple)
      • Schedule of exceptions (found encumbrances/defects and known unrecorded defects)
      • Conditions and stipulations required before policy issuance
  • ALTA Standard Title Commitment: The Four Schedules
    • Created by the American Land Title Association (ALTA) to standardize title commitments nationwide, a standard title commitment is organized into four schedules:
      SchedulePrimary Purpose & ContentsKey Details & Exceptions
      Schedule ATransaction DetailsLists the buyer's name, legal description of the property, types of policies being purchased (owner's/lender's), and the estate/interest being conveyed (e.g., fee simple). Real estate agents should review Schedule A carefully for accuracy.
      Schedule BPolicy Exceptions (Items NOT covered by the policy)Divided into Standard Exceptions and Specific Exceptions:

      • 4 Standard Exceptions (excluded from all standard policies):

      1. Rights of parties currently in possession (e.g., tenants with valid leases)

      2. Encroachments, encumbrances, or boundary issues that an accurate survey would reveal

      3. Unrecorded easements not appearing in public records

      4. Unrecorded mechanic's liens or tax liens not shown in public records

      • Specific Exceptions: Property-specific items already known to the buyer and title company (such as recorded easements, existing liens, or subdivision deed restrictions).
      Schedule CRequirementsLists every action or defect the seller must clear before the title company will issue the final policy and authorize closing (e.g., paying off existing mortgages, mechanic's liens, or delinquent tax liens).
      Schedule DDisclosuresDiscloses all parties receiving a share of the title insurance premiums (such as the title underwriters and issuing agents).

  • Title Policy Coverage: Standard vs. Extended Coverage
    • What a Title Policy Does NOT Cover:
      • Beyond the standard and specific exceptions listed in Schedule B, title insurance does not cover future physical property damage or loss—such as fire, flood, or theft (which are covered by a homeowner's insurance policy).
    • Standard Coverage vs. Extended Coverage:
      Coverage TierWhat It Insures AgainstAvailability & Requirement
      Standard Coverage• Forged documents

      • Certain unrecorded liens unknown to the policyholder

  • Claims & Subrogation in Title Insurance
    • Subrogation: The legal substitution of one party for another in pursuing a lawful claim or right.
    • How It Works in Title Insurance:
      • When the buyer purchases a title insurance policy, the policy agreement grants the title insurer the contractual right of subrogation.
      • If a covered title defect surfaces after closing and the title insurer pays out a claim to indemnify the homeowner, the insurer steps into the homeowner's shoes and pursues the responsible third party (such as a seller who breached warranty covenants or a fraudulent party) to recover the financial loss, if possible.
  • Recorded Documents & Title Records
    • Recording: The formal process of placing documents affecting any interest, right, or title to a parcel of real estate into the public record at the county recorder's office in accordance with state law.
    • Title Records: Public records maintained at the county level cataloging all real estate data for parcels within that county, including:
      • Owners' names
      • Liens
      • Easements
      • Encumbrances
    • Two Primary Purposes of Recording:
      1. Public Accessibility: Enables prospective buyers, lenders, and researchers to discover all existing claims or interests in a property.
      1. Establishing Legal Priority: Legal priority among competing claims is generally determined by the "first in time, first in line" convention (based on the chronological date and time of recording).
        • Priority Exceptions: Property tax liens and special assessments take automatic super-priority over previously recorded private liens regardless of when they were recorded.
  • Notice of Title: Constructive Notice vs. Actual Notice
    • Duty to Give Notice: Real estate owners and interest holders have a duty to make their claim or interest publicly discoverable so subsequent purchasers and lenders are placed on notice.
      Type of NoticeDefinition & Legal NatureHow Notice Is Established
      Constructive Notice (Legal Notice)Legal presumption that a diligent person can discover all present and past ownership interests and claims by inspecting public records or observing the property—binding on everyone regardless of whether they actually checked.• Recording a document in the public title records (Recording a deed = constructive notice)

      • Physical possession of the property (provides constructive notice whether or not a deed or lease is recorded, and even if others are unaware of the occupant)
      Actual NoticeDirect, first-hand personal knowledge obtained through direct experience or communication ("with your own ears or eyeballs").• Physically reading a deed or unrecorded agreement

      • Personally searching title records

      • Physically visiting a property and seeing someone living in or occupying it
  • Homeowners Insurance (Hazard Insurance)
    • Definition: A multi-line insurance policy for residential property that typically bundles property damage coverage, theft protection, and personal liability coverage (which protects the homeowner if someone is injured on the property and files a lawsuit).
    • Lender Requirement & Hazard Insurance:
      • When a buyer finances a home with a mortgage, the lender requires proof of sufficient insurance coverage to satisfy the loan balance if the property is damaged or destroyed.
      • The broad industry term for insurance covering physical loss or damage to a property is hazard insurance, which is available in different policy tiers and coverage limits.
    • Loss Payee Clause:
      • The mortgage lender is named as the loss payee on the homeowner's insurance policy.
      • If the property is destroyed: Even though the policy is in the homeowner's name, the insurance company pays the lender first up to the remaining mortgage balance.
      • If the property is damaged but repairable: The lender will generally allow the homeowner to use the insurance payout to repair the property so the mortgage remains intact.
  • Home Warranties
    • Definition & Distinction from Insurance: A home warranty is not insurance and is not legally or lender-required. Instead, it is a service contract (typically lasting one year) in which a warranty company agrees to maintain, repair, or replace covered home components—usually major appliances, electrical systems, plumbing systems, and heating/air conditioning (HVAC) systems.
    • Providers & Who Pays:
      • New construction: Often provided directly by the builder.
      • Existing homes: Purchased from third-party home warranty companies. Who pays is negotiable, though sellers frequently pay for a one-year warranty to make the listing more attractive to buyers.
    • How a Home Warranty Works:
      1. Covered appliances and systems generally must be in good working condition at the time the warranty is purchased.
      1. A single annual fee is paid upfront.
      1. During the coverage period, covered breakdowns are serviced by licensed technicians in exchange for a small per-visit service fee rather than full out-of-pocket parts and labor costs.
    • Transferability Upon Resale:
      • If a home is resold while an existing warranty is still active, the unexpired warranty transfers to the new buyer.
      • If expired, either the seller (prior to closing) or the buyer can purchase a new policy as part of contract negotiations.
    • Benefits for Buyers & Sellers:
      • Provides buyers with financial peace of mind against unexpected, high-cost system or appliance failures right after closing, while giving sellers a marketing advantage that helps their property stand out.
  • Licensee Responsibilities & Anti-Kickback Rules
    • Educating the Buyer: Real estate agents should ensure buyers understand that:
      • Multiple competing companies offer home warranties.
      • Coverage terms, caps, and exclusions vary significantly from one provider to another.
      • It is the buyer's responsibility to review plan terms and select their preferred coverage.
    • Providing Choices & Avoiding Illegal Kickbacks:
      • When a client asks which home warranty company to use, the agent should provide a list of multiple options rather than steering the client toward a single company.
      • Offering multiple choices protects both the consumer and the licensee and prevents legal/regulatory violations—real estate licensees can face severe penalties (including RESPA violations) for accepting undisclosed kickbacks or referral fees from service providers in exchange for steering clients.

Key Terms

  • abstract of title - an abbreviated history of a property, including information on any transfers, grants, wills, conveyances, liens, and encumbrances
  • certificate of title - a document prepared by a title attorney detailing the chain of title and offering an opinion on the marketability of the seller's title
  • chain of title - the entire chronological record of a property’s ownership
  • color of title - a title transfer that is defective in a way that is unknown to the new owner
  • evidence of title - constructive or actual notice of real property ownership
  • marketable title - a title that is free from significant encumbrances or defects that might otherwise prevent a purchaser from enjoying or eventually selling the property
  • notice of title - the duty of a real estate owner to make their claim or interest on a subject property publicly accessible
  • opinion of title - the official opinion of an attorney regarding the condition of a property’s title
  • title insurance - a policy that protects homeowners (and likewise, lenders) from certain financial losses due to title issues, such as defects, encumbrances, and liens
  • Torrens system - a recording system used in some states in which the state holds all records of land and title ownership, evidenced by a certificate of title

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Marketable Title
    • A marketable title is one that is:
      • Free from serious defect
      • Free from legal exposure
      • Reasonably thought to be sellable should the buyer become a seller at some point in the future
  • Evidence of Title
    • Here is a ranking of the ways to show evidence of title, from best to worst. Remember that Tennessee generally uses title insurance and does NOT use the Torrens system.
  • Title Insurance: Types of Policies
    • Title companies issue several types of insurance policies. The two most common are:
      • Owner’s title insurance policy: protects the buyer and is optional
      • Lender’s title insurance policy (sometimes called mortgagee’s title policy): protects the lender and is mandatory
  • Title Commitment
    • After a title company does a title search, they issue a title commitment. Usually, it is in four parts, Schedules A, B, C, and D. Schedule B has the exceptions.
    • The title commitment states the terms and conditions on which the actual policy will be issued. The document includes:
      • The name of the insured party
      • The legal description of the property
      • The estate or interest covered
      • A schedule of expectations including any found encumbrances and defects and any known unrecorded defects
      • Any conditions or stipulations under which the policy is issued
  • Recording
    • Property information is documented, saved, and recorded in the title records.
    • Recording provides public notice and helps protect both buyers and lienholders.
  • Notice of Title
    • The two kinds of notice of title are:
      • Constructive notice: a diligent individual can search the public record to gain a sufficient understanding of the property, including those with present and past interests in that property
      • Actual notice: direct or first-hand knowledge
  • Homeowners Insurance
    • Homeowners insurance is a multi-line insurance for residential property. Typically, it includes property damage coverage, plus theft protection and liability coverage. Lenders will require a buyer to prove that they have enough insurance to pay back their loan if something happens to the property.
