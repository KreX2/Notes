Chapter 3 - Planning and Zoning

Planning Commission: Definition & Primary Function

  • Core Purpose: An official administrative body tasked with developing, updating, and executing a municipality's long-range master plan (comprehensive plan).
  • Advisory Role: Serves as a consulting entity for municipal boards and legislative bodies regarding land development, zoning actions, and urban planning policies.

  • Specific Responsibilities in Tennessee
    • Master Plan Development: Creates and maintains the municipality's comprehensive master plan.
    • Mandatory Referral: Formally advises local legislative bodies and municipal boards on proposed planning actions and public land-use decisions before official adoption.
    • Subdivision & Site Plan Review: Evaluates preliminary and final subdivision regulations, land division proposals, and individual commercial/residential site plans.
    • Zoning Map & Ordinance Oversight: Prepares initial recommended zoning maps and ordinances, and reviews all proposed amendments or re-zoning applications.
  • Tennessee Statutory Authority
    • Optional Adoption: Creation of a local planning commission or comprehensive master plan is not mandatory under the Tennessee Code.
    • Compliance Mandate: If a local government chooses to establish a planning commission or adopt a master plan, it must strictly adhere to the organizational and procedural requirements outlined in state statutes.
  • Master Plan (Comprehensive Plan): Definition & Core Purpose
    • Definition: A comprehensive, long-range policy document developed by a planning commission that establishes a community's vision, land-use goals, and municipal development priorities (the terms master plan and comprehensive plan are legally interchangeable).
    • Balanced Growth: Systematically balances social, economic, and commercial development needs against environmental sustainability and aesthetic standards.
    • Stakeholder Integration: Formulates future growth strategies by evaluating the needs of current residents, local businesses, environmental resources, and expected regional expansion.
  • Development & Legislative Adoption Process
    • Policy Foundation: Serves as an ongoing blueprint and policy guide representing overall community values to steer future administrative decision-making.
    • Zoning Alignment: Once the master plan is finalized, the planning commission drafts and recommends corresponding zoning maps and zoning regulations to enforce the plan.
    • Legislative Approval: The local municipal government (e.g., city council or county commission) holds ultimate authority to formally vote to adopt or reject the planning commission's recommended master plan and zoning laws.
    • How to create a Master Plan
      • Research & Data Analysis Phase
        • Demographics & Physical Surveys: Evaluates population age distributions, socioeconomic data, physical land surveys, and current housing inventory.
        • Infrastructure & Public Services: Assesses capacity and future demands for transportation/roads, public transit, public schools, water/sewer utilities, and green space.
        • Economic & Historical Preservation: Analyzes local economic trends, commercial growth opportunities, and historic preservation priorities.
        • Growth Management: Evaluates population migration patterns to determine optimal growth rates; master plans may cap building permits or subdivision approvals to regulate development speed and environmental impact.
      • Six-Step Master Plan Creation Process
        1. Focus: Identify major community development issues, challenges, and priorities.
        1. Inventory: Conduct detailed land-use surveys and analyze demographic and infrastructural data.
        1. Identify Goals & Objectives: Define target outcomes based on community needs (e.g., expanding parkland, increasing affordable housing).
        1. Develop Plan: Formulate comprehensive urban planning policies, maps, and development strategies.
        1. Review Plan: Present the draft plan for public input, administrative evaluation, and legislative review.
        1. Implement & Evaluate: Formally adopt the plan, enforce it through zoning maps and ordinances, and periodically measure its long-term effectiveness.
    • Master Plan as a Living Document
      • Continuous Adaptation: Functions as a dynamic, long-term policy guide that is regularly revisited, modified, and expanded to accommodate shifting demographics, changing community priorities, and ongoing regional growth.
      • Goals & Timetables: Establishes specific development objectives paired with structured implementation schedules and problem-solving initiatives (e.g., expanding transit corridors to relieve traffic congestion, or restricting building permits to preserve open green space).
    • Core Purposes & Functions of a Master Plan
      • Legal Prerequisite for Zoning: Serves as the legally required comprehensive framework that a municipality must establish before enacting local zoning regulations and maps.
      • Holistic Alignment: Unifies physical land-use planning with overarching social, environmental, and economic development goals.
      • Legislative Guidance: Provides elected officials and lawmakers with an official policy blueprint to guide future ordinances that incentivize or restrict community growth.
      • Cross-Departmental Coordination: Consolidates public planning priorities across multiple municipal sectors—including transportation, land-use controls, utility infrastructure, road networks, and recreational space—into a single centralized document.
    • Creation of Zoning Ordinances
      • Sequence: Commences immediately following the completion and adoption of the municipal master plan.
      • Two Core Components:
        1. Zoning Map: Dividing the municipality into designated geographic zones (classifications).
      1. Permitted Uses: Codifying rules that specify allowed, conditional, and prohibited property uses, structures, and activities within each zone.
  • Role of Planning Commission vs. Local Government
    • Planning Commission Recommendation: Drafts and submits recommended zoning maps and ordinances based on the goals established in the master plan.
    • Legislative Enactment: The elected local government (e.g., city council or county commission) holds sole legislative authority to officially enact zoning suggestions into binding municipal law.
  • Tennessee Specific Requirements & Procedures
    • Mandatory 15-Day Public Hearing Notice: Tennessee Code mandates that local municipal governments hold a public hearing with at least 15 days advance notice prior to voting on proposed zoning regulations.
    • Legislative Revision Referral: If the elected legislative body modifies the planning commission's recommended zoning plan or regulations, the revised version must be referred back to the planning commission for review before final legislative action (though the legislative body retains ultimate authority).
  • Board of Zoning Appeals (BZA)
    • Administrative & Interpretive Role: The separate local government entity tasked with interpreting, applying, and hearing administrative appeals or variance requests regarding enacted zoning regulations
  • What Does Zoning Regulate?
    • How land is used: Is this land residential? Industrial? Commercial? Depends on the zone.
    • Minimum lot size: What is the smallest "buildable" lot size?
    • Maximum building height: How tall can the tallest building be?
    • Kinds of structures that are allowed: The zone you're in will determine the permitted structures.
    • Setback: This describes how far from the street (and other buildings) a building has to be.
    • Density: This is referring to the maximum allowable number of units — denser areas allow more people per square mile, less dense areas allow fewer.
    • Parking spaces: Zoning regulations can require builders to create parking for residents, especially in dense areas.
    • How air rights can be transferred: Some places allow residents to sell their air rights.
    • How natural resources can be used in the area: Water rights, conservation requirements, wetland protections, mineral rights, and other environmental concerns are part of zoning.
    • How much land is set aside for public use and infrastructure: This includes land for streets, schools, hospitals, utility right-of-ways, parks, green space, and more.
    • Historic preservation and landmark sites: Sometimes these are overseen and regulated by a separate committee and set of laws, and sometimes they are part of zoning.
    • Limits on accessory buildings: What kind of structures other than a home can be built on a residential lot?
    • Lot coverage: What's the maximum square footage that can exist on a given lot?
    • Zoning Regulations & Population Density
      • Density: The concentration of inhabitants per unit of land area (square miles or kilometers).
      • Policy Adjustment: Municipalities modify zoning regulations and land-use maps to manage population density in alignment with evolving master plans.
    • Upzoning vs. Downzoning
      • Upzoning: Modifying zoning regulations to permit higher density and greater population concentration.
        • Common Requirements/Mechanisms: Allowing multi-family residential development (apartments, condos), reducing minimum lot sizes, increasing allowable building heights/square footage, and reducing required setbacks or side yards.
      • Downzoning: Modifying zoning regulations to restrict development and reduce allowable density.
        • Common Requirements/Mechanisms: Increasing minimum lot size requirements, capping maximum building square footage, and expanding required setback and side-yard distances from property lines and roads.
    • Zoning Fairness Test
      • To be legally enforceable and constitutionally sound, zoning ordinances and their administrative application must meet four standard criteria:
      • Clear & Specific: Rules and requirements must be unambiguously defined.
      • Applied Equally: Regulations must be enforced uniformly across all properties within a given zoning classification.
      • Nondiscriminatory: Regulations must be free from unlawful discrimination, complying fully with federal and state fair housing laws.
      • Reasonable: Requirements must bear a rational relationship to protecting public health, safety, morals, or general welfare (police power).
    • Core Concept & Adaptability of Zoning Classifications
      • Local Customization: Municipalities establish specific zoning classifications tailored to their unique geographic features, economic bases, and community needs.
      • General Framework: Standard zoning categories exist across most jurisdictions, though specific permitted uses and regulations vary locally.
    • Primary Zoning Classifications
      • Residential: Reserved for housing ranging from single-family homes to high-density multi-family structures. Frequently permits complementary non-residential community facilities (e.g., schools, houses of worship, medical clinics).
      • Commercial: Designated for business and retail enterprises, including shopping centers, offices, restaurants, hotels, and service establishments.
      • Industrial: Set aside for manufacturing, industrial processing, assembly plants, and storage/distribution warehouses.
      • Agricultural: Reserved for farming, crop cultivation, livestock production, and timber operations; non-agricultural zones restrict animal keeping.
      • Planned Unit Development (PUD): A specialized zoning classification designed for flexible land development that mixes multiple uses (such as residential, retail, office, and green space) within a unified site plan to maximize efficiency.
      • Institutional: Designated for large public, quasi-public, or community infrastructure facilities, including university campuses, major medical centers, government complexes, courthouses, and correctional institutions.
      • Open Space: Land designated to remain largely undeveloped, including undeveloped public forests, municipal parklands, and undeveloped shorelines.
      • Conservation Land: Environmentally sensitive areas subject to strict protection and land-use restrictions, such as wetlands, coastal zones, aquifer recharge areas, and critical wildlife habitats.
      • Recreational: Publicly or privately owned land explicitly dedicated to active community recreational activities, including public beaches, community parks, municipal forests, and multi-use trail networks.
  • As-of Right Zoning & Development
    • Development that fully conforms to existing local zoning ordinances and building codes, allowing it to proceed automatically through standard administrative permitting.
    • Approval Streamlining: Requires no special discretionary review, public hearings, community board votes, or approval from a planning commission or Board of Zoning Appeals (BZA).
    • Application Scope:
      • Routine Projects: Standard and uncontroversial for small developments and single-family homes, preventing unnecessary construction delays and administrative costs.
      • High-Density Controversy: Controversial in dense urban areas for large-scale or supertall developments, as high-impact projects can alter neighborhood character, skylines, and light access without public input.
  • Overview of Advanced Philosophical Zoning Concepts
    • As-of Right Zoning: Automatic entitlement for projects meeting all baseline zoning criteria.
    • Incentive Zoning: Rewards developers with density bonuses or extra building height in exchange for public amenities (e.g., plazas, community centers).
    • Inclusionary Zoning: Requires a set percentage of units in new developments to be reserved as affordable workforce housing.
    • Cluster Zoning: Groups residential buildings closely together on smaller individual lots to preserve the remaining site acreage as permanent open green space.
    • Floating Zones: A zoning classification codified in the text but not fixed to the zoning map until a developer's proposal meets specific qualifying criteria.
    • Aesthetic Zoning: Enforces architectural styles, building materials, and exterior design standards to maintain visual harmony.
    • Historic / Landmarked Districts: Strict overlay zones preserving architectural, historical, or cultural heritage by limiting exterior alterations or demolitions.
    • Buffer Zones: Neutral transition strips of land (e.g., parks or landscaped greenbelts) placed between incompatible land uses, such as separating single-family housing from heavy industry.
    • Incentive Zoning
      • Definition: The relaxation of standard zoning restrictions (typically granting a density bonus permitting greater building capacity or height) in exchange for a developer providing a specified public benefit or amenity.
      • Legal Precedent: Defined in Levitt v. Town of South Kingstown Planning Bd. of Appeal as the process where local authorities grant additional development capacity in exchange for public amenities.
      • Common Public Benefits: Used to fund and secure transit connections, public green spaces, waterfront access, mixed-use community areas, or affordable housing.
    • Inclusionary Zoning
      • Definition: A specialized form of incentive zoning that provides developers with density bonuses specifically for incorporating affordable housing, senior housing, or multi-unit housing into their projects.
      • Key Benefits:
        • Cost-Free to Municipalities: Operates without direct financial expenditure or tax credits for the local government, relying entirely on density allowances.
      • Community Diversity: Successfully promotes mixed-use, mixed-age, and mixed-income neighborhoods.
  • Cluster Zoning Overview
    • Definition: Also known as density zoning or conservation-oriented development, cluster zoning calculates overall community density for an entire parcel rather than on a traditional individual lot-by-lot basis.
    • Core Mechanism: Permits developers to group residential buildings tightly together (housing clusters) on a fraction of the land while preserving the remaining acreage as permanent open green space.
    • Common Metric: Frequently achieves twice the density on half the acreage compared to conventional uniform subdivisions.
    • Affordability & Sustainability Benefits
      • Lower Construction Costs: Building smaller, attached, or multi-unit dwellings is significantly cheaper than constructing widely spaced single-family homes, increasing market affordability.
      • Environmental & Recreational Integration: Preserved open space can be utilized for community parks, shared gardens, wildlife habitats, pedestrian/biking trails, and natural water reclamation.
      • Targeted Communities: Highly effective for senior living facilities, group homes, and accessible developments for adults with disabilities.
  • Pros
    • Creates communities that are walkable/bikeable
    • Allows for more green space
    • Makes offering affordable housing easier, as units are smaller and less expensive to build
    • Is a great set-up for communities of older adults or adults with disabilities
    • Encourages community
    • Allows land to be left uncultivated for wildlife and water reclamation
    • Allows space for shared recreational use and community gardens
  • Floating Zone: Definition & Mechanism
    • Definition: A flexible zoning classification and set of regulations added to the municipal zoning text without being pinned to a specific location on the zoning map; it "floats" until a developer submits an qualifying project proposal.
    • Activation Process: A parcel remains zoned for its current use (e.g., single-family residential) until a developer proposes a specific, desirable project (such as a mixed-use commercial/residential center) that satisfies the planning commission's pre-approved requirements.
    • Planning Commission Role: The planning commission reviews the proposal and recommends mapping the floating zone to the specific parcel.
    • Advantages of Floating Zones
      • Community Selectivity: Allows municipalities to be highly selective about the exact types of development and architecture they permit in certain areas.
      • Eliminates As-of Right Uncertainty: Effectively prevents undesirable as-of right development by requiring discretionary project approval for higher-density or specialized uses.
      • Legal Standing: Unlike unlawful spot zoning, floating zones are legally permissible because they serve a broader public interest and adhere to the comprehensive master plan.
    • Aesthetic Zoning
      • Primary Purpose: Regulates architectural styles, exterior building designs, materials, and overall visual appearance to ensure new construction conforms to community aesthetic standards.
      • Alternative Enforcement: Neighborhoods frequently achieve similar architectural conformity through private restrictive covenants or HOA bylaws rather than municipal zoning.
    • Historic & Landmark Districts
      • Definition: Special municipal overlay zones established to protect and preserve the architectural and cultural heritage of historically significant areas.
      • Regulatory Oversight: Administered by a local municipal agency that may restrict certain types of development, demolition, or exterior modifications.
      • Design Mandates: Property owners within historic districts are typically required to use historically appropriate materials and architectural styles for home repairs and updates.
      • Signage & Interpretation: Agencies may also oversee the placement of historical plaques and interpretive signage on significant buildings.
    • Registers of Historic Places (State & National)
      • Official Listing: A formal inventory of noteworthy, historically significant buildings, structures, and sites.
      • Lack of Mandated Preservation: Inclusion on the historic register alone does not legally compel property owners to preserve or maintain the building in any specific way.
      • Financial Incentives: Listing provides property owners access to special tax credits designed to help offset the cost of period-appropriate repairs, maintenance, and structural restorations.
    • Buffer Zones:
      • Transitional areas of land designed to separate two incompatible zoning classifications (e.g., placing a barrier between a residential zone and an industrial zone) to provide spatial separation and aesthetic relief.
      • Common Elements: Typically consists of parks, landscaped berms, open greenbelts, natural vegetative strips, or recreational spaces.
  • Board of Zoning Appeals (BZA): Definition & Authority
    • Statutory Basis: Authorized under Tennessee Code sections 13-7-304 and 13-7-305 for municipalities that utilize a planning commission.
    • Three Primary Responsibilities:
      1. Hearing and deciding appeals when a building permit or zoning enforcement decision is challenged.
      1. Reviewing and approving or denying special exceptions (uses on appeal).
      1. Reviewing and approving or denying variances.
    • Interpreting Zoning Law & Permit Appeals
      • Appellate Review: Hears appeals from property owners or builders who have been denied a building permit or have received an adverse ruling from municipal zoning officials.
      • Legal Scope: Evaluates whether municipal officials correctly interpreted and enforced existing zoning ordinance provisions.
    • Special Exceptions (Uses on Appeal)
      • Definition: Specific land uses explicitly written into the zoning ordinance as permissible within a given zone, but which cannot be established as-of right.
      • BZA Oversight: Requires formal review and mandatory approval by the BZA (or "use on appeal") to ensure compliance with explicit criteria set forth in the zoning code (e.g., maintaining a required distance buffer from similar establishments).
    • Variances
      • Definition: Official administrative permission to depart from strict physical dimensional requirements of a zoning ordinance (such as setbacks, frontage, minimum lot size, or yard depth).
      • Qualifying Criteria (Hardship Test): To obtain a variance, the property owner must prove "exceptional difficulties" or "undue hardship" caused strictly by a pre-existing, inherent physical condition of the land (such as an unusual lot shape, a steep grade, or an environmental obstacle like a creek running through the property).
      • Self-Created Hardship Exclusion: A variance will be denied if the hardship was self-created by the property owner.
  • Nonconforming
    • Definition: A property use, structure, or lot that was legally established and compliant under previous zoning laws, but became noncompliant due to a subsequent change in municipal zoning regulations.
    • Legacy Exception: Existing nonconforming structures and uses are generally permitted to continue (protected by a "legacy exception," which is the preferred term over grandfathering due to its historical origins).
    • Primary Rule: Nonconforming uses are typically allowed to be maintained and continued, but cannot be expanded, enlarged, or structurally upgraded beyond existing footprints.
    • Loss of Legacy Exception (When Nonconforming Uses Become Illegal)
      • A legal nonconforming status can be terminated under specific circumstances determined by municipal law:
        • Severe Damage or Destruction: If a majority (or specified percentage) of a nonconforming structure is destroyed or heavily damaged (e.g., by fire or storm), it must be rebuilt in strict compliance with current zoning laws.
        • Abandonment / Discontinuance: If a nonconforming use—particularly a commercial enterprise—ceases operation or remains vacant for a set statutory period (typically ranging from six months to two years), the legacy exception expires.
        • Amortization Period: A new zoning ordinance may establish a mandated phase-out period, giving property owners a set number of years to recoup their investment before the nonconforming use must terminate or conform.
        • Change of Ownership / Business: For commercial nonconforming uses, the legacy exception usually applies only to the existing business; if the business closes and a new type of commercial venture moves in, it is classified as an illegal nonconforming use.
  • Spot Zoning
    • Definition: The illegal or improper rezoning of a single small parcel of land for a use classification entirely inconsistent with the surrounding neighborhood.
    • Key Characteristics: Designed primarily to benefit a single property owner at the expense or detriment of neighboring property owners.
    • Legal Status: Generally illegal and invalid because it violates the uniformity principle and contradicts the community's comprehensive master plan.
    • Zoning Amendments
      • Definition: A legal change to the zoning classification of a specific area or property initiated or recommended by the planning commission.
      • Distinction from Spot Zoning: While spot zoning serves private interests exclusively, a valid zoning amendment is enacted to serve the broader public interest and directly aligns with the goals of the municipal master plan.
      • Example: Rezoning an abandoned industrial warehouse into a commercial zone to establish a community center or public museum, benefiting the surrounding community as industrial needs decline.
  • Zoning Enforcement via Building Permits
    • Permit Requirement: Developers and builders must secure an official building permit from the local department of buildings before commencing any construction.
    • Plan Review: Builders and architects must submit detailed construction plans; permits are denied if the proposed plans violate local zoning regulations or building codes.
    • Site Inspections: Municipal inspectors conduct on-site inspections during construction to verify compliance, and can shut down work sites if code violations are discovered.
    • Zoning Ordinances vs. Building Codes
      • Zoning Ordinances ("The Outside"):
        • Regulate external land use, lot sizes, building heights, setbacks, population density, and allowable activities within specific geographic districts.
        • Administered by planning commissions and boards of zoning appeals.
      • Building Codes ("The Inside"):
        • Establish minimum statutory construction standards for structural safety, habitability, electrical wiring, plumbing, and fire prevention within structures.
        • Created and enforced locally by the municipal building department through plan reviews and field inspections.
  • Certificate of Occupancy (CO)
    • Definition: An official document issued by the local building department after a newly constructed or substantially renovated building passes its final inspection.
    • Prerequisite for Use & Sale: Required before a building can be legally occupied, rented, or sold.
    • Renovations: For completed renovation projects, a comparable certificate of compliance is issued to certify that the work meets all current building codes.
    • Critical Importance for Buyers & Lenders
      • Financing Requirement: Mortgage lenders strictly require a valid, matching Certificate of Occupancy before approving funds for property acquisition.
      • Mismatched CO Risks: Discrepancies between the physical structure and the CO (such as a converted single-family home still designated as a two-family property) create major legal hurdles and severe administrative delays.
      • Professional Rule: Real estate agents must never advise a client to purchase any property lacking a valid, accurate Certificate of Occupancy.
  • Post-Construction Enforcement & Building Inspectors
    • Ongoing Compliance: Building safety inspectors conduct routine periodic checks on completed buildings even after a Certificate of Occupancy has been issued.
    • Scope of Checks: Verifies ongoing compliance with municipal building codes, fire regulations, and safety standards.
    • Penalties: Property owners found in violation are subject to administrative fines, civil penalties, or criminal charges.
  • The Judicial Branch as the Ultimate Enforcement Arm
    • Final Authority: The court system serves as the ultimate enforcement and legal arbiter for zoning laws and building codes.
    • Judicial Scope: Handles civil lawsuits, administrative appeals, and criminal proceedings related to violations, while determining whether municipal regulations are legally valid and defensible.
  • Unofficial & Professional Enforcement
    • Industry Practitioners: Day-to-day enforcement is informally upheld by qualified real estate and construction professionals, including architects, urban planners, land surveyors, engineers, contractors, and construction crews.
    • Compliance Role: These professionals act as front-line enforcers by ensuring designs, measurements, and structural plans adhere to municipal land-use controls before formal review.
  • Purpose & Value of Urban Planning
    • Core Objective: To transform cities into more livable, healthy, affordable, and equitable environments by balancing private development with public welfare.
    • Preventing Unregulated Growth: Acts as a vital check against chaotic, purely profit-driven development that would otherwise compromise community livability, infrastructure capacity, and public health.
    • Public Infrastructure Integration: Ensures space is successfully allocated and incentivized for essential municipal functions that private markets alone would neglect (e.g., utility infrastructure, transit hubs, government offices).
    • Key Community Benefits of Effective Urban Planning
      • Expanded open space and protected natural environments
      • Guaranteed access to public parks and green spaces
      • Strategic placement of community facilities and schools
      • Designated recreation areas and waterfront access
      • Integrated public transportation networks and transit hubs
      • Dedicated infrastructure for active transportation (sidewalks, bike lanes, and bikeable/walkable neighborhoods)
      • Inclusionary opportunities for affordable housing
      • Organized corridors for essential municipal utilities
  • Urban Sprawl: Definition & Characteristics
    • Definition: The opposite of thoughtful urban planning; characterized by low-density, monofunctional, and car-dependent communities (often referred to as unchecked suburbanization).
    • Unplanned Growth: Typically expands rapidly across open land outside municipal limits without synchronized infrastructure development (such as expanding roads, building schools, or establishing public utilities).
  • Negative Impacts of Sprawl
    • Environmental Harm: Generates significantly higher vehicle emissions due to increased driving distances and absolute car dependence.
    • Quality of Life: Contributes to longer, draining commute times that reduce individual happiness and daily free time.
    • Economic Costs: Imposes massive societal and economic costs (estimated in studies at roughly $1 trillion annually in the U.S.) and increases individual automobile usage by up to 60%.
  • Smart Development vs. Sprawl
    • Controlled Growth: Contrasts sharply with sprawl by prioritizing smart, compact growth, infrastructure adequacy, and environmental preservation.
    • Core Philosophy: Balances private real estate development with community livability, sustainability, and public welfare.

Key Terms

  • Density: The number of people living in a square mile (or kilometer) of space.
  • Non-conforming Use: Any property use which is inconsistent with the land use controls for that area, regardless of legality.
  • Special Exception (Use on Appeal): Allows an out-of-zone use that benefits the community only with approval from a board of zoning appeals (BZA); is included as a permitted special use in a zoning ordinance.
  • Variance: Permission granted by the government so that property may be used in a manner not allowed by the current zoning.

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Master Plans:
    • The main task of planning commissions is to create a master plan for a municipality.
    • A master plan balances the area's social and economic needs against its environmental and aesthetic needs.
    • The priorities in a master plan will also help to set the zoning regulations. Once the plan is complete, the local legislative body will vote to adopt or reject it.
    • Typically, the steps of a master plan look something like this:
      1. Identify major issues
      1. Survey and analysis
      1. Identify goals and objectives
      1. Develop a plan
      1. Review the plan
      1. Implement and evaluate
    • Once the plan is in place, the next step is zoning.
  • Zoning:
    • Zoning regulations happen at the municipal level, so regulations will vary depending on the municipality. These regulations will change in order to match the needs of a changing municipality.
    • Some common zoning classifications include residential, commercial, agricultural, and recreational land. The zoning classification for a parcel of land will dictate how the land can be used, and what can be built on it.
    • Zoning is enforced through both zoning laws and building code. As a general rule of thumb, zoning deals with land, and building code deals with buildings.
  • Board of Zoning Appeals (BZA):
    • When a municipality creates zoning laws, they usually also create a board of zoning appeals (BZA).
    • The three main jobs of the BZA are:
      • To hear appeals from people denied building permits based on zoning issues (and interpret whether the law was correctly applied)
      • To approve or deny special exceptions (sometimes called a "use on appeal")
      • To approve or deny a variance
  • Special Exceptions:
    • The BZA can also approve or deny special exceptions, which in Tennessee are often called "uses on appeal."
    • A use on appeal is a use written into the zoning law that can't be accessed as-of-right. Instead, it must be specifically approved by the BZA.
    • These uses are included in the zoning law itself as possibilities for a zone, but not things the planning commission wanted to be built without BZA oversight.
    • Special exception uses MUST be:
      • Consistent with what is written in the zoning law
      • Approved by the BZA
  • Variances:
    • Variances, unlike special exceptions, have to do with the physical characteristics of a property.
    • If it would cause a homeowner "exceptional difficulties" or "undue hardship" to comply with a zoning ordinance, they can apply for a variance with the BZA.
    • To qualify for a variance, the size, shape, or topography of the homeowner's lot must be incompatible with meeting the requirement.
