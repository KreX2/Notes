Chapter 2 - Fair Lending Acts

Fair Lending Under the Fair Housing Act of 1968 (42 U.S.C. § 3605)

  • Core Prohibition: In addition to sales and rentals, the Fair Housing Act prohibits any person or entity whose business includes engaging in residential real estate-related transactions from discriminating in making, purchasing, insuring, or servicing loans, or providing other financial assistance, based on any of the seven protected classes (race, color, religion, national origin, sex, familial status, and disability—plus creed under Tennessee law).
  • Examples of Unlawful Mortgage Lending Discrimination:
    • Refusing to originate or approve mortgage loans for Black applicants
    • Requiring women—but not similarly situated men—to provide a co-signer
    • Charging Hispanic borrowers higher interest rates, points, or origination fees than similarly qualified white applicants
  • Where & When Lending Discrimination Can Occur
    • Lending discrimination is illegal across both home purchase loans and refinancing transactions, and can arise at any stage of the lending lifecycle:
      1. Marketing of Mortgage Loan Products: Targeting subprime or predatory loan products toward minority neighborhoods while marketing prime rates exclusively elsewhere.
      1. Mortgage Loan Origination & Underwriting: Applying stricter credit, income, or down-payment standards to protected-class applicants.
      1. Loan Terms & Conditions: Imposing less favorable interest rates, points, adjustable-rate structures, or fees.
      1. Home Appraisal & Valuation: Undervaluing homes in minority neighborhoods or factoring neighborhood demographics into appraised value.
      1. Loan Servicing & Loss Mitigation: Offering inferior loan modification, forbearance, or foreclosure-prevention options based on protected status.
  • Warning Signs (Red Flags) of Predatory or Discriminatory Lending
    • Lending discrimination disproportionately affects racial/ethnic minorities and women. Key red flags licensees and borrowers should watch for include:
      • Unnecessary or padded closing costs not charged to similarly qualified borrowers
      • Inflated mortgage broker or lender origination fees
      • Unnecessary or duplicative recording fees
      • Excessive or punitive prepayment penalties that trap borrowers in high-cost loans
      • Bait-and-switch loan terms: Changing interest rates, loan programs, or fee structures at the closing table without the borrower's prior knowledge and consent
  • Advising Clients
  • Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 et seq., 1974)
    • Core Purpose & Prohibited Actions: Prohibits creditors from discriminating against any applicant in any credit transaction by making it illegal to:
      • Refuse credit to an applicant who qualifies for it
      • Discourage anyone from applying for credit
      • Offer credit on less favorable terms (e.g., higher interest rates or fees) than terms offered to someone with similar financial qualifications
      • Close an existing account based on protected status
    • Protected Classes Under ECOA:
      1. Race or color
      1. Religion
      1. National origin
      1. Sex (explicitly includes sexual orientation and gender identity since 2021)
      1. Marital status
      1. Age (provided the applicant has legal capacity to contract—i.e., at least 18 years old; there is no maximum age limit, so an 80year-old applicant cannot be denied a 30year mortgage solely because of age)
      1. Receipt of income from any public assistance program (e.g., Social Security, disability, SNAP, housing vouchers)
      1. Good-faith exercise of rights under the Consumer Credit Protection Act
    • Regulation B (12 C.F.R. Part 1002) — Notification & Credit Reporting Rules:
      • Creditors must notify applicants of their credit decision within 30 days of receiving a completed application.
      • If denied or offered adverse terms, the creditor must issue an Adverse Action Notice stating the specific reasons for the rejection.
      • Credit history on joint accounts for married couples must be reported separately in the individual name of each spouse.
    • Permissible vs. Impermissible Lender Questions Under ECOA:
      Lenders MAY AskLenders MAY NOT Ask
      • Whether the applicant is married, unmarried, or separated (using only those three exact terms)

      • Whether the applicant is obligated to pay alimony, child support, or separate maintenance

      • Information about a spouse if the spouse will be a co-applicant/party to the contract (or in a community property state / relying on spouse's income)      		• Whether the applicant is divorced or widowed

      • Whether the applicant receives alimony, child support, or separate maintenance—unless the applicant voluntarily chooses to rely on that income to qualify

      • Questions about birth control, family planning, or childbearing/child-rearing intentions

      • Questions about a non-applicant spouse not involved in the loan
  • Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA, 12 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq., 1974)
    • Purpose & Scope: Enacted in 1974 to regulate closing and settlement practices on federally related mortgage loans (most 1–4 family residential mortgages) and protect consumers from abusive or discriminatory closing charges.
    • Loan Estimate (LE) Fair Housing Protection (TRID Rule):
      • Lenders must deliver a standardized Loan Estimate (which replaced the former Good Faith Estimate / GFE) within 3 business days of receiving a completed loan application.
      • By standardizing estimated interest rates, monthly payments, and total closing/settlement costs across lenders, the Loan Estimate empowers borrowers—especially members of protected classes—to comparison-shop across multiple lenders and detect padded or discriminatory fees early.
  • Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA, 12 U.S.C. § 2801 et seq., 1975)
    • Purpose & Anti-Redlining Enforcement: Enacted in 1975, HMDA requires covered mortgage lenders dealing with federally guaranteed or insured loans to collect, report, and publicly disclose geographic and demographic lending data—enabling federal regulators and HUD to detect redlining and discriminatory lending patterns across neighborhoods.
    • Mandatory Demographic Collection (HMDA vs. ECOA):
      • To monitor lending patterns, HMDA requires lenders to request and record each applicant's race, ethnicity, sex/gender, and age (plus loan type, loan amount, pricing/terms, approval/denial status, and census tract).
      • Key Distinction: Asking for race, ethnicity, sex, and age on the demographic monitoring section of a mortgage application is legally required under HMDA for federal fair-lending oversight, not a violation of ECOA—provided the lender never uses that information to make credit decisions or discriminate.
  • Quick Application Summary: Lender Scenarios
    • Asking About Child Support Received: Illegal under ECOA unless the applicant voluntarily indicates she wants child support income counted toward loan qualification (while asking about child support obligations paid is legal because it affects debt-to-income ratio).
    • Checking a Co-Applicant Husband's Credit: Legal under ECOA because the husband is a party to the loan application—and once closed, the lender must report payment history separately under each spouse's individual name.
  • Community Reinvestment Act (CRA, 12 U.S.C. § 2901 et seq., 1977)
    • Core Purpose & Anti-Redlining Mandate: Enacted in 1977 to combat redlining and reduce discriminatory credit practices against low- and moderate-income (LMI) neighborhoods by requiring federally insured depository institutions (banks and savings associations) to help meet the credit needs of the entire local communities in which they are chartered—not just affluent areas.
    • Key Obligations on Banks & Lenders:
      • Equitable Access to Credit: Banks have a continuing, affirmative obligation to serve the convenience and credit needs of all segments of their local assessment areas, including low- and moderate-income borrowers and neighborhoods, consistent with safe and sound banking operations.
      • Periodic Evaluation & Community Development: Federal regulators periodically evaluate each bank's lending record, investments, and participation in local community development projects (such as affordable housing initiatives, neighborhood revitalization, and small-business lending), factoring the bank's CRA rating into applications for mergers, acquisitions, or new branches.
    • Counteracting Neighborhood Disinvestment & "Filtering Down":
      • What "Filtering Down" Is: A housing market cycle in which higher-income households move out of older homes into newly built, higher-priced homes elsewhere, leaving their vacated properties to pass down sequentially to households in lower income brackets.
      • The Disinvestment Problem: When new construction slows or wealthier residents exit older neighborhoods, those "filtered-down" neighborhoods often suffer from declining capital investment and restricted mortgage access.
      • How the CRA Intervenes: By requiring lenders to regularly reassess local credit needs and extend mortgage credit to qualified low- and moderate-income buyers in older or transitioning neighborhoods, the CRA injects capital back into filtered-down areas and helps prevent neighborhood blight and disinvestment.
  • Omnibus Consolidated Appropriations Act of 1997 (Lender Self-Testing Privilege)
    • Voluntary Fair-Lending Self-Testing: Enacted as part of the 1997 Omnibus Appropriations Act (amending ECOA and the Fair Housing Act), this provision expressly authorized mortgage lenders to conduct confidential self-tests—such as mystery shopping or internal underwriting audits—to evaluate whether their lending practices comply with federal fair lending laws.
    • Legal Safe Harbor for Remediation:
      • If a lender identifies potential discriminatory practices through a privileged self-test and takes appropriate corrective action to remedy the issues, the results of that self-test are protected from disclosure or use against the lender in federal lawsuits or regulatory enforcement actions.
      • This safe-harbor rule encourages lenders to proactively audit their own practices, uncover hidden bias early, and implement more equitable lending standards without fear of self-incrimination.

  • Fair Lending Acts: A Summary
  • United States v. Countrywide Financial Corporation (2011) — Record Fair Lending Settlement
    • Landmark Penalty: Resulted in the largest residential fair lending settlement in U.S. Department of Justice history at the time—$335 million in direct compensation for affected borrowers.
    • Pattern or Practice of Discrimination (2004–2008): The DOJ established that Countrywide and its subsidiaries systematically discriminated against more than 200,000 qualified Black and Hispanic/Latino borrowers under both the Fair Housing Act (FHA) and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) by:
      • Charging Black and Hispanic borrowers higher interest rates, fees, and loan costs than similarly qualified white borrowers.
      • Steering qualified minority applicants into high-cost subprime loans even when they met underwriting standards for prime loans—and when similarly qualified white applicants received prime loans.
    • Marital Status Violation Under ECOA: Countrywide also violated ECOA's prohibition against marital status discrimination by encouraging non-applicant spouses of borrowers to execute documents signing away their legal ownership rights and interests in jointly held property to the applicant spouse—exposing the non-applicant spouse to severe financial loss and legal uncertainty.
  • Bank of America Corp. v. City of Miami (2017) — Municipal Standing Under the Fair Housing Act
    • Core Facts & Allegations: The City of Miami sued Bank of America and Wells Fargo under the Fair Housing Act (FHA), presenting statistical data showing the banks disproportionately targeted Black and Hispanic borrowers for predatory, high-interest, high-fee mortgages—and subsequently refused to refinance or modify those loans, triggering widespread foreclosures and vacant properties in minority neighborhoods.
    • The Legal Question ("Aggrieved Person" Standing): The banks argued that only individual borrowers—not a municipality—qualified as an "aggrieved person" authorized to sue under the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3602(i)).
    • U.S. Supreme Court Holding (5–3 Decision):
      • The Supreme Court held that a city qualifies as an "aggrieved person" under the Fair Housing Act because its claimed financial injuries—reduced property-tax revenues and increased municipal spending on police, fire, and blight remediation caused by concentrated foreclosures—fall within the zone of interests protected by the FHA.
      • The Court also clarified that to recover damages, a municipality must demonstrate proximate cause (a direct relation between the alleged discriminatory lending conduct and the city's claimed financial harm), remanding the case for lower courts to apply that standard.
