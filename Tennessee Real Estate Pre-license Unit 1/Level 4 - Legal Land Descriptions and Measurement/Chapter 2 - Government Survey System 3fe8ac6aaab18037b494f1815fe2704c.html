Chapter 2 - Government Survey System

Government Survey System

  • Also Known As: The rectangular survey system or public land survey system.
  • Origin and Purpose: Developed by the federal government during American westward expansion to simplify and standardize the transfer of large amounts of government-owned land into the hands of private citizens.
  • How It Works: It relies on a grid system based on standard global lines of longitude and latitude mapping. Unlike metes and bounds, it does not require physical monuments or specific boundary descriptions.
  • Prevalence: Today, more land in the United States is defined by the government survey system than by any other land description method.
  • Meridians and Parallels
    • Meridians (Longitude Lines): Run north to south, segmenting the globe along Earth's poles.
    • Parallels (Latitude Lines): Run east to west, parallel to the equator.
    • Principal Meridians and Baselines: Specially designated lines that serve as the foundation for dividing land into townships.
      • Principal Meridians: There are 37 named principal meridians in the U.S. system. Their specific names are used to distinguish the various surveys.
      • Baselines: Latitude lines that run perpendicular to the principal meridians.
      • Initial Point: The exact intersection where a baseline and a principal meridian meet. This point serves as the official starting point for a township's survey.
  • Ranges
    • Range Lines: Lines that run parallel to the principal meridian (north to south) and are spaced exactly six miles apart from one another.
    • Ranges: The specific areas of land located between two consecutive range lines, making each range six miles wide.
    • Numbering: Ranges are numbered consecutively east and west from their respective principal meridians.
  • Tiers
    • Township Lines: Lines that run east and west, parallel to the baseline, and are spaced exactly six miles apart from one another.
    • Tiers: The areas of land created between two consecutive township lines, making each tier six miles wide.
    • Numbering: Tiers are numbered consecutively by their specific positions either north or south of the baseline.
  • Township Lines and Range Columns
    • Townships: Formed when a six-mile range (column) intersects with a six-mile tier.
    • Dimensions: Because ranges and tiers are each 6 miles wide, a township is 6 miles by 6 miles, creating a total area of 36 square miles.
  • Correction Lines and Adjustments
    • Correction Lines: Used to account for the Earth's curvature to ensure the grid squares don't become significantly distorted. They are placed every fourth tier (every 24 miles).
    • Government Checks: A large quadrangle created when guide meridians and correction lines intersect.
      • Size: Measures 24 miles on each side.
      • Composition: Each government check breaks down further into 16 townships.
    • Fractional Sections: Also known as corrected sections.
      • A corrected township contains 11 fractional sections and 25 full sections.
      • The correction adjustment is 49.5 feet (a distance equal to three rods).
    • Government Lots: Irregularly shaped parcels of land that are typically located along meandering bodies of water.
  • Township Numbering
    • Tier (Township Row) Numbering: Townships are numbered consecutively north and south of a baseline (e.g., "Township 1 North" / T1N, or "Township 1 South" / T1S).
    • Range Column Numbering: Range columns are numbered consecutively east and west of a principal meridian (e.g., "Range 1 East" / R1E, or "Range 1 West" / R1W).
    • Unique Coordinates: Combining these two designations creates a specific grid coordinate for each 6-by-6 mile square (e.g., "T1N, R1E" = Tier 1 North of the baseline, Range 1 East of the principal meridian), differentiating it from all other townships
  • Sections of a Township
    • Division: Each 36-square-mile township is divided into 36 individual square-mile "sections."
    • Measurements:
      • 1 Section = 1 square mile, which contains 640 acres.
      • 1 Acre = 43,560 square feet.
      • Square Foot: A unit of area measurement equal to a square measuring one foot on each side.
    • Numbering System (The "Snaking" Pattern):
      • Starting Point: Numbering begins in the upper right-hand (top right) corner of the township.
      • Direction: It moves from right to left across the top row, drops down to the second row and moves left to right, then drops to the third row and moves right to left.
      • This alternating "snaking" pattern continues back and forth until all 36 sections are numbered.
  • Dividing Sections
    • Area Breakdown: Each section is exactly one square mile, which equals 640 acres.
    • Subdivisions: A section can be divided into halves (320 acres) and quarters (160 acres). These subdivisions can be further halved and quartered continuously.
    • Labeling: Each divided part of a section is labeled by its specific directional location within that section.
      • Example: The quarter located in the northeast corner of Section 1 is labeled as the "NE 1/4 of Section 1".
      • If you wanted to further divide that quarter into quarters and wanted to label the southwestern quarter of NE 1⁄4, it would be called the SW1⁄4 of NE 1⁄4.
      • You could then divide it again. For example, you could divide it into the NE 1⁄4 of SW1⁄4 of NE 1⁄4. That section would be 10 acres.
      • Important Measurements to Know
      • Key Area & Survey Conversions:
        • 1 township = 36 sections (36 square miles)
        • 1 section = 640 acres (1 square mile)
        • 1 acre = 43,560 square feet
      • Real estate agents must commit these key measurements to memory to explain survey drawings to clients, clearly identify property lines, and answer real estate licensing exam questions.
      • Math Practice: Finding Acreage and Square Footage
        • Problem: Find the acreage and square footage for the N 1/2 of SW 1/4 of NE 1/4.
        • Key Rules to Remember:
          ◦ 1 full section = 640 acres
          ◦ 1 acre = 43,560 sq. ft.
          • Step-by-Step Solution:
          ◦ Step 1: Identify the denominators from the fractions in the description.
          ▪ Fractions: 1/2, 1/4, 1/4
          ▪ Denominators: 2, 4, 4
          ◦ Step 2: Calculate the total acreage.
          ▪ Start with 640 acres and divide by each denominator:
          ▪ 640 \divide 2 \divide 4 \divide 4 = 20 acres
          ◦ Step 3: Convert acreage to square footage.
          ▪ Multiply the total acres by 43,560:
          ▪ 20 acres times 43,560 sq. ft. = 871,200 sq. ft.
          • Final Answer:
          ◦ Acreage: 20 acres
          ◦ Square Footage: 871,200 sq. ft.
    • Non-Rectangular and Irregular Properties
      • Limitation of Government Survey System: Works best for neat, rectangular, or square properties. It is less suited for irregularly shaped parcels, and Earth's curvature causes some sections to deviate slightly from exactly 640 acres.
      • Measuring Non-Rectangular Parcels: Irregularly shaped properties can still be described using the government survey system as a baseline.
      • Combining Survey Methods: To create a complete and accurate legal description for a non-rectangular property, a surveyor will include a metes and bounds or block and lot description alongside the government survey description.

Key Terms

  • Acre - A measurement of land equal to 43,560 square feet.
  • Baseline - A parallel of latitude running east-west, established from a designated initial point, used alongside a principal meridian to anchor government surveys and identify tiers, ranges, and townships.
  • Government survey system - A legal method of land description using lines of longitude and latitude to create a grid of meridians, baselines, townships, and ranges; also known as the rectangular survey system or public land survey system.
  • Meridian - An imaginary line of longitude running north-south, used to locate positions on the Earth's surface.
  • Parallel - A line of latitude running east-west, parallel to the equator.
  • Principal meridian - A designated true meridian running north-south through an initial point that, along with the baseline, forms the primary framework for government surveys in a specific region.
  • Range - A measure of distance east or west from a principal meridian in 6-mile increments; the column of land situated between two consecutive range lines.
  • Section - An individual square-mile block of land (640 acres) within a township. Each 36-square-mile township contains 36 sections, which can be further divided into halves (320 acres), quarters (160 acres), and smaller quarter-quarters.
  • Square foot - A unit of area measurement equal to a square measuring one foot on each side.
  • Tier - A measure of distance north or south from a baseline in 6-mile increments; the row of land situated between two consecutive township lines.
  • Township - A square parcel of land measuring 6 miles on each side (36 square miles total) formed by the intersection of 6-mile range columns and 6-mile township tiers. It is identified by its coordinates relative to a principal meridian and baseline, and contains 36 sections.

Key Concepts

  • Government Survey System
    • Grid Mapping: Uses global lines of longitude and latitude to locate properties on a grid, systematically breaking land down into smaller, uniform-sized pieces.
    • Ranges and Tiers:
    ◦ Range Columns: Run parallel to meridians (north-south) to create 6-mile-wide ranges.
    ◦ Township Lines: Run parallel to baselines (east-west) to create 6-mile-wide tiers.
    • Townships: Formed where a 6-mile range intersects with a 6-mile tier, creating a square area measuring 36 square miles ( 6 x 6 miles)
