Chapter 3 - Closing the Loan

Loan Origination & Pre-Closing Timeline

  • The Process: Loan origination and settlement is an extended process. It requires systematic steps to evaluate the borrower, assess the property collateral, verify title, prepare legal instruments, and execute closing documents before title transfers and funds disburse.
  • Loan Application (URLA): The process formally begins when the borrower completes the Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA / Fannie Mae Form 1003 / Freddie Mac Form 65) to provide complete financial and property details to the lender.
  • The Loan Estimate (LE): Within 3 business days of receiving the completed loan application, the lender must issue a Loan Estimate (LE).
    • Replaces the legacy Good Faith Estimate (GFE) and initial Truth-in-Lending (TIL) disclosure.
    • Highlights estimated interest rates, projected monthly payments (PITI), cash to close, and total closing costs.
    • Acts as an official disclosure for shopping across multiple lenders (does not constitute loan approval).
  • Underwriting & Verification: Lenders review income, credit, net worth, and liabilities using the Residential Mortgage Credit Report (RMCR) and formal Verification of Employment (VOE) forms. Lenders are legally bound by ECOA to evaluate creditworthiness strictly on financial metrics, prohibiting discrimination based on protected classes.
  • Appraisal & Valuation: The lender orders an independent third-party appraisal to establish market value and verify the Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio, determining required down payments and PMI/MIP requirements. Lenders must notify applicants within 3 business days of application that they will receive a copy of the completed appraisal report.
  • Property Inspections: Pest inspections (WDI/WDO) and general home inspections are conducted to verify structural and property integrity; formal reports are incorporated into the closing packet.
  • Title Search & Title Insurance:
    • A title search examines public records for existing liens, clouds, or encumbrances, yielding a preliminary title report.
    • Title Insurance Policy: Paid at closing via a single one-time premium. Unlike standard insurance protecting against future events, title insurance protects against financial losses arising from past defects, liens, or unrecorded claims discovered after closing.
  • Closing, Settlement & Possession Mechanics
    • Final Loan Approval & Loan Commitment: Lenders re-verify borrower credit and financial status right before closing. Clients must avoid making large purchases, opening new credit accounts, or changing employment during origination. Upon final underwriting approval, the lender issues a formal loan commitment.
    • Document Preparation: Lenders prepare required legal instruments to secure their first-lien priority and protect financial interests (sales contract, deed, mortgage/deed of trust, property survey, inspection reports, homeowner's insurance policy, bill of sale for personal property, and IRS Form 1099-S).
    • The Closing Disclosure (CD): Delivered to the borrower at least 3 business days prior to closing. Reflects exact final numbers, loan terms, and settlement charges, matching the figures presented at escrow signing.
    • Escrow Signing: Borrower deposits required down payment and closing costs into escrow while signing the loan packet, which includes:
      1. Promissory Note: The legal obligation establishing debt liability, repayment terms, and interest rate (holds negotiable value similar to a check).
      1. Security Instrument (Mortgage or Deed of Trust): Pledges the property as collateral for the note (hypothecation).
      1. Truth in Lending Statement & Closing Disclosure: Final accounting of total financing costs over the life of the loan.
      1. Title Insurance Commitment & Proration Statements: Details title status and prorated monthly expenses (HOA dues, property taxes, utilities).
      1. Supporting Affidavits & Reports: Statement of identity, inspection declarations, and abstract of title.
    • Recording & Constructive Notice: Executed documents (deed and mortgage/deed of trust) are recorded in county title records to establish legal ownership and provide constructive notice to the public regarding property interests.
    • Disbursement of Funds & Possession: Escrow releases funds to the seller once all closing conditions are satisfied and documents are verified. Upon funding and legal recording, the buyer receives the keys and officially takes possession as the new homeowner.
  • Government Influence in Loan Origination
    • Federal regulations and legislation shape mortgage loan origination, ensuring transparency, preventing abusive lending practices, standardized disclosures, and protecting consumer rights throughout the transaction.
  • Regulation Z (Truth in Lending Act / TILA)
    • Purpose: Enacted to ensure credit terms are disclosed clearly and meaningfully, enabling consumers to compare credit costs across lenders knowledgeably. Applies to all residential mortgage loans.
    • Core Lender Requirements:
      • Must disclose all loan fees and the Annual Percentage Rate (APR) during origination.
      • Must comply with strict advertising guidelines regarding trigger terms (e.g., down payment amounts, interest rates, monthly payments).
    • Right of Rescission: Provides borrowers taking out home equity loans or refinancing an existing mortgage a 3-business-day right of rescission to cancel the transaction after consummation (does not apply to purchase-money mortgages).
  • Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) Guidelines
    • RESPA Mandatory Requirements (The "DOs"):
      • CFPB Educational Booklet: Lenders must supply applicants with the CFPB booklet explaining loan options and the settlement process.
      • Standard Disclosure Delivery: Must provide the Loan Estimate within 3 business days of application and the Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days prior to closing.
      • Computerized Loan Origination (CLO) Fees: Brokers may charge up to 0.5% for CLO matching services provided the fee is paid by the borrower (not the lender), disclosed upfront, and reflects reasonable services rendered.
    • RESPA Restrictions & Prohibitions (The "DON'Ts"):
      • Escrow Cushion Limits: Lenders cannot hold excessive funds in escrow for taxes and insurance. RESPA caps maximum escrow collections at 14 months of payments (a 2-month safety cushion above the 12-month annual requirement). Surplus funds over 14 months must be refunded or applied to future obligations.
      • No Fee for Required Disclosures: Lenders are strictly prohibited from charging borrowers fees for preparing the Loan Estimate, Closing Disclosure, escrow statements, or TILA disclosures.
  • TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure (TRID) & The Dodd-Frank Act
    • Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010): Created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) as a centralized agency to enforce federal consumer financial laws.
    • TRID Integration (2015): Streamlined legacy TILA and RESPA disclosure rules into two unified, transparent forms:
      1. Loan Estimate (LE): Replaced the Good Faith Estimate (GFE) and initial Truth-in-Lending (TIL) statement.
      1. Closing Disclosure (CD): Replaced the HUD-1 Settlement Statement and final Truth-in-Lending statement.

  • CFPB Integrated Disclosures (TRID) Summary
    • The Loan Estimate (LE):
      • Replaces the legacy Good Faith Estimate (GFE) and the initial Truth-in-Lending (TIL) statement.
      • Designed to simplify comparison shopping among competing lenders by highlighting essential loan parameters early in the origination process.
    • The Closing Disclosure (CD):
      • Replaces the legacy HUD-1 Settlement Statement and the final Truth-in-Lending (TIL) statement.
      • Mirrors the structure, design, and line-item formatting of the Loan Estimate so borrowers can easily compare side-by-side and spot any cost increases or fee changes prior to closing.
  • TRID Statutory Delivery Deadlines (Exam Critical)
    • Loan Estimate (LE) Deadline: Must be delivered or placed in the mail by the lender within 3 business days of receiving the borrower's loan application.
    • Fee Collection Restriction: Lenders cannot charge application, appraisal, or processing fees (except a reasonable credit report fee) until the applicant has received the LE and given intent to proceed.
    • Closing Disclosure (CD) Deadline: The borrower must receive the CD at least 3 business days prior to closing day.
    • Impact of Missed CD Deadline: Failing to meet the 3-day receipt window forces the settlement date to be postponed, delaying closing and potential property possession.
  • How LE and CD Work Together (Page 1 Alignment)
    • Projected Payments: Both forms display a clear breakdown of estimated total monthly payments (PITI), helping borrowers evaluate ongoing housing expenses alongside unescrowed costs like HOA dues or property maintenance.
    • Costs at Closing: Page 1 of both documents explicitly states:
      • Total Closing Costs: Loan origination, title, appraisal, and third-party fees.
      • Estimated Cash to Close: Total liquid funds required at closing (down payment + closing costs - earnest money deposit/credits).
  • Triggers for Revised Loan Estimates
    • If material changes occur during underwriting, lenders must issue a revised Loan Estimate. Common change triggers include:
      • Switching loan products (e.g., from an adjustable-rate mortgage to a fixed-rate loan).
      • Altering the down payment amount or loan balance.
      • An appraisal coming in lower than purchase price.
      • Changes in the borrower's credit score or credit profile.
      • Income or employment verification discrepancies.
      • Floating interest rate lock changes (adjusting discount points or lender credits).

  • The Full Form
  • Discount Points vs. Lender Credits
    • Discount Points (Buydown): Upfront fees paid by the borrower at closing to lock in a lower ongoing interest rate over the life of the loan (prepaying interest upfront).
    • Lender Credits: Credit offered by the lender to reduce upfront closing costs in exchange for accepting a higher ongoing interest rate over the life of the loan.
    • The 1% Universal Rule: 1 point strictly equals 1% of the loan principal amount (applies equally to both loan origination points and discount points).
  • Interest Rate Reductions & Lender Rules of Thumb
    • Standard Threshold: As a general rule of thumb, paying 1 discount point (1% of loan principal) typically lowers the mortgage interest rate by 25 basis points (0.25%).
    • Diminishing Returns: Purchasing additional points does not guarantee linear rate reductions. Purchasing 2 points may yield less than a 50 basis point (0.50%) drop depending on individual lender pricing structures. Borrowers should always evaluate the specific break-even timeline for each point purchased.
  • Break-Even Analysis & Mathematical Formulas
    • The Decision Strategy: Prepaying interest via discount points makes economic sense only if the borrower intends to remain in the home (and retain the original mortgage without refinancing) beyond the financial break-even point.
    • The Break-Even Formula :Discount point fee ÷ Monthly payment savings = Months to break even
  • Worked Example (Break-Even Calculation)
    • Given Variables:
      • Upfront Discount Point Fee: $3,000
      • Monthly Mortgage Payment Reduction: $100
    • Calculation:$3,000 ÷ $100 = 30 months
    • Result & Takeaway: The borrower must stay in the loan for at least 30 months (2.5 years) to recover the $3,000 upfront cost. Keeping the loan beyond 30 months generates an ongoing net savings of $100 per month.

  • Calculating Cash to Close
    • Cash to close is the total amount a buyer needs to bring to closing. This includes cash for such things as fees, taxes, prepaid and escrow items, title policy, points, down payment, etc.
    • If you reviewed the Loan Estimate form I provided earlier, you might have noticed that the bottom of page two contains a section titled Calculating Cash to Close. It provides a line-item list of what would be added and subtracted to get to the total estimated cash to close. (This will also appear with the latest numbers in the Closing Disclosure form.)
  • Cash to Close Calculation Overview
    • Definition: The total liquid amount of cash a borrower must bring to the settlement table (via cashier's check or wire transfer) to execute the transaction.
    • The Core Formula: Total Closing Costs + Down Payment - Earnest Money Deposit - Seller Credits - Other Credits = Estimated Cash to Close

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Loan Origination Timeline
  • The Uniform Residential Loan Application
  • Fannie Mae's Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA) is the standard form most lenders use to collect the information necessary to evaluate the loan risk their applicants represent. The benefit of using this form is that it allows lenders to approach the task of underwriting without bias and with consistency.

  • RESPA: regulates the lending process related to real estate settlement procedures
  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB): created a single point of accountability for enforcing federal consumer financial laws and protecting consumers in the financial marketplace
  • The Loan Estimate
  • The Loan Estimate combines and replaces the Good Faith Estimate and the initial Truth-in-Lending (TIL) statement. The form highlights the most important elements of the transaction and allows for easy comparisons among competing lenders.
  • A lender is obligated to deliver or mail the Loan Estimate form by the third business day after the lender receives the loan application from the borrower.
  • The Closing Disclosure
  • The Closing Disclosure combines and replaces the HUD-1 Settlement Statement and the final Truth-in-Lending (TIL) statement. It provides the final details about the mortgage loan.
  • Regardless of how it's delivered, the borrower must receive the Closing Disclosure three business days before closing day. The implications of a missed Closing Disclosure deadline generally mean that closing itself gets moved.
  • Two Things to Remember about Discount Points
  • 1 point = 1% of the loan principal (this is true whether it is an origination point or a discount point).In the purchase of discount points (aka buydown), a borrower is basically agreeing to pay some interest upfront as a trade-off for a lower interest rate over the life of the mortgage.
  • 1 point = 1% of the loan principal (this is true whether it is an origination point or a discount point).
  • In the purchase of discount points (aka buydown), a borrower is basically agreeing to pay some interest upfront as a trade-off for a lower interest rate over the life of the mortgage.
  • To evaluate whether or not it makes sense to pay for discount points, use this formula:
  • Discount point fee ÷ Monthly payment savings = Months in loan
  • To solve for the cost of discount points, use this formula:
  • Loan principal x Loan points = Total cost of points
  • To find the loan principal, given the points and the total cost of points, use this formula:
  • Total cost of points ÷ Loan points = Loan principal
  • Lender Credits
  • Lender credits work the same way as points but in reverse. You pay a higher interest rate and the lender gives you money to offset your closing costs.
  • Calculating Cash to Close
  • Cash to close is the total amount a buyer needs to bring to closing. This includes such things as fees, taxes, prepaid and escrow, title policy, points, down payment, etc.
  • You'll find a Calculating Cash to Close section on both the Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure forms.
