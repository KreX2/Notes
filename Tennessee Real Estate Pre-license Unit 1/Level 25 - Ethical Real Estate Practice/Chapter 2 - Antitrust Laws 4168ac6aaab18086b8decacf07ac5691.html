Chapter 2 - Antitrust Laws

Antitrust Laws & Collusion

  • Definition: Federal and state legislation designed to promote open market competition and prohibit monopolies, restraints of trade, and unfair business practices.
  • Collusion: An unlawful agreement (formal or informal) between competitors to monopolize a market, restrict competition, disadvantage other competitors, or otherwise violate fair trade laws.
  • Four Primary Antitrust Violations:
    1. Price fixing
    1. Group boycotts
    1. Market or customer allocation
    1. Tie-in agreements
  • Price Fixing
    • Definition: Any conspiracy or joint effort among competitors to establish, stabilize, or manipulate a fixed rate, fee, price, or commission structure.
    • Prohibited Activities Among Competitors:
      • Agreeing to raise, fix, or standardize prices or commission rates for goods or services
      • Setting a minimum price or fee floor below which they will not operate
      • Agreeing to reduce or eliminate discounts
    • Core Real Estate Rule: Commission rates are always negotiable between an individual brokerage and its client. Representatives from separate brokerages may never discuss or agree upon commission rates—and neither can professional associations (such as NAR, state/local REALTOR® boards, or government agencies) set uniform commission rates.
      Permissible Conduct (Legal)Prohibited Conduct (Illegal Price Fixing)
      • A brokerage independently setting its own internal commission policies and discussing them at in-house sales meetings within the same firm.

      • Separate brokerages arriving at similar commission rates organically due to independent responses to market conditions.

      • Two cooperating brokers (e.g., Ben and Jerry) negotiating how a commission will be split within the context of a specific transaction.      		• Brokers or agents from different brokerages agreeing—formally or informally—to charge a specific commission rate or fee.

      • Discussing or analyzing commission rates with competitors at business meetings, social events, or casual encounters (even mere presence during such discussions creates legal risk).

      • Trade associations or local boards publishing suggested or uniform commission schedules.
  • Group Boycotts
    • Definition: An agreement between two or more competitors to refuse to do business with another competitor or third-party vendor in order to force that party to change its business practices or drive it out of the market.
    • Real Estate Examples & Consumer Harm:
      • Boycotting a Competitor: Two or more traditional full-service brokerages agreeing not to show listings or cooperate with a new discount brokerage—harming consumers by restricting their access to available homes and competitive pricing. (As illustrated in the coffee shop scenario, even an informal verbal pact between two veteran brokers over coffee to shun a low-commission competitor violates antitrust law.)
      • Boycotting Third-Party Entities: Two or more agents or brokers from competing firms agreeing to stop using—and persuading others to stop using—a website, newspaper, or advertising firm because they dislike its advertising rates, thereby artificially interfering with free-market pricing.
  • Market or Customer Allocation
    • Definition: An antitrust violation in which competing brokerages or licensees agree to divide or portion up geographic territories, property types, or specific customer groups among themselves rather than competing openly for business.
    • Illegal Restraint on Trade: Even if dividing territory makes geographic or logistical sense (e.g., one brokerage taking the north side of town and another taking the south side), agreeing to split markets or turning away clients because they reside in another firm's "designated area" is a per se violation of federal antitrust law because it restricts consumer choice and suppresses open competition.
  • Tie-In Agreements (Tying Arrangements)
    • Definition: A conditional agreement stipulating that a desired product or service (the tying product) will be provided only on the condition that the consumer also agrees to purchase a separate, second product or service (the tied product).
    • Real Estate Examples & Consumer Harm:
      • Attempting to require a client to use a specific title company, mortgage lender, or home inspector in order to obtain brokerage services or a discounted commission rate is an illegal tie-in arrangement.
      • Scenario Breakdown (Tina, Jim, and Harry): When buyer's agent Jim offers buyer Tina a 20% commission discount conditioned on Tina using his friend Harry's high-interest mortgage lending services (while Jim receives a referral cut from Harry), the arrangement unlawfully restricts competition and leaves Tina paying significantly more over the life of her loan.

  • Antitrust Violations: Let's Recap
  • Core Federal & State Antitrust Legislation
    • Historical Purpose: Enacted beginning in the late 1800s in response to Gilded Age "robber barons" forming monopolies and trusts, antitrust laws exist to protect consumers and businesses by promoting fair, open competition while keeping prices low and service quality high.
      StatuteYear EnactedKey Provisions & Scope
      Sherman Antitrust Act1890Landmark foundational federal antitrust law prohibiting monopolies, trusts, and any contract, combination, or conspiracy in restraint of trade (including price fixing, group boycotts, market/customer allocation, and tie-in agreements). Authorizes criminal and civil prosecution.
      Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Act1914Established the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) as the nation's consumer protection agency to investigate and prevent unfair methods of competition and deceptive acts. Overlaps with the Sherman Act.
      Clayton Antitrust Act1914Strengthened the Sherman Act by prohibiting anti-competitive mergers/acquisitions, predatory or discriminatory pricing, exclusive dealing, and tying arrangements; also permits private civil lawsuits for damages. Usually overlaps with Sherman Act liability.
      Tennessee Antitrust LawsState LawMirror federal antitrust prohibitions against restraints of trade and monopolies; enforced at the state level by the Tennessee Attorney General's Office.
  • Criminal & Civil Penalties, Enforcement & Risk Prevention
    • Criminal Felony Classification & Penalties:
      • Sherman Act violations involving horizontal agreements between competitors are typically prosecuted as criminal felonies.
      • Individual violators: Subject to steep criminal fines and federal prison (jail) time.
      • Corporate violators: Subject to heavy corporate fines.
      • Licensing Discipline: Violators also face suspension, revocation, or penalties from their state real estate commission / licensing board.
    • Enforcement Agencies & Investigative Powers:
      • U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) Antitrust Division: Handles both criminal and civil prosecutions under the Sherman Act and may coordinate with the FBI, monitor phone calls, use confidential informants, and employ covert listening devices.
      • Federal Trade Commission (FTC): Brings civil enforcement actions to stop anti-competitive practices.
      • Private Civil Lawsuits: Injured consumers or businesses may file private lawsuits to recover monetary damages suffered from antitrust violations.
    • Practical Risk Prevention for Licensees:
      • Strict Golden Rule: Never discuss prices, commission rates, fees, competitors, or money with licensees from competing brokerages—even casually or jokingly.
      • Immediate Exit Protocol: If a conversation with competitors drifts toward commissions, fees, or boycotts, leave immediately (and consider verbally announcing why you are departing so witnesses know you refused to participate).
  • Antitrust and the NAR Settlement
    • Background of the Lawsuit (2019): Home sellers filed a class-action antitrust lawsuit against the National Association of REALTORS® (NAR) alleging that requiring listing brokers to include offers of buyer-broker compensation in Multiple Listing Service (MLS) listings restrained competition, fixed prices, and inflated commission rates by pressuring sellers to offer higher payouts to prevent buyer agents from steering clients away.
    • Settlement Approval & Damages: Preliminary court approval was granted on April 24, 2024, followed by final court approval in November 2024. Without admitting wrongdoing, NAR agreed to pay over $400 million in damages and implement sweeping industry practice changes.
    • Two Mandatory Practice Changes (Effective August 17, 2024):
      1. Compensation Offers Removed from the MLS: Listing brokers and sellers may no longer publish offers of buyer-broker compensation on MLS platforms. Sellers may still offer cooperative compensation or buyer concessions, but any compensation offer must be communicated and negotiated outside of the MLS.
      1. Mandatory Written Buyer Representation Agreements: REALTORS® working with a buyer must enter into a written buyer representation agreement before touring a home (specifying clear, conspicuous, and negotiable compensation terms).
    • Best Practices for Communicating with Clients:
      • With Sellers: Clearly explain how commissions work, review the pros and cons of offering compensation or concessions to buyer brokers off-MLS, and emphasize that all commissions are fully negotiable and solely the seller's decision.
      • With Buyers: Explain upfront why a written agreement is required prior to touring homes, highlight the value of buyer representation, and walk through all options for paying buyer-agent compensation—especially if a seller chooses not to offer compensation.

Key Terms

  • antitrust laws - laws designed at preventing unfair or anti-competitive practices such as price fixing, collusion, monopolies, etc.
  • collusion - an unlawful agreement between competitors to monopolize a market, disadvantage other competitors, or otherwise undertake activities in violation of fair trade laws
  • market allocation - a violation of antitrust laws in which competitors agree to divide markets amongst themselves
  • price fixing - any effort made by competitors to jointly establish a fixed rate, price, or commission; considered an antitrust violation
  • Sherman Antitrust Act - a landmark law passed in 1890 that prohibits the formation of trusts, or monopolies, in the marketplace; this law governs business by regulating anti-competitive conduct to promote fair competition on behalf of American consumers

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Antitrust Behaviors
    • Most behaviors that fall under the umbrella of antitrust activity are forms of colluding, but they can be more specifically categorized as one of the following:
      • Price fixing
      • Group boycott
      • Market or customer allocation
      • Tie-in agreements
  • Avoiding Antitrust Violations
  • Violations of the Sherman Act
    • Sherman Act violations involving agreements between competitors are usually punished as criminal felonies. The penalties are:
      • Individual violators: fined up to $1 million and sentenced to up to 10 years in federal prison for each offense
      • Corporations: fined up to $100 million for each offense
      • In addition, violators are subject to discipline by their state license law board.
  • Antitrust and the NAR Settlement
    • Here are the two key practice changes implemented as of August 17, 2024:
      • Compensation offers moved off MLS - Seller offers of compensation for buyer brokers can still happen, but this must be negotiated outside of the MLS.
      • Written agreements for buyer representation are required - What was previously encouraged is now mandatory: Realtors working with buyers MUST have a written agreement in place before touring a home.
