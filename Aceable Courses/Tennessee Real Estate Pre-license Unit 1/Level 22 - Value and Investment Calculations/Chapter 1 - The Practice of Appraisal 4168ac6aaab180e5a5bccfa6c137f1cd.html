Chapter 1 - The Practice of Appraisal

What Is an Appraisal?

  • Core Definition: An appraisal is an impartial, licensed or certified appraiser's written opinion of the value of a specific property as of a specific date, supported by relevant market data.
  • Why It Is an "Opinion": Because there is no single, absolute dollar figure everyone would assign to a property, even qualified appraisers can reach slightly different conclusions. An appraisal is an educated, data-driven estimate informed by current market research, professional experience, and judgment—considering the nature, quality, and utility of an identified real estate interest.
  • Deal Impact: Because financing hinges on the appraised value, receiving the appraisal report is often one of the highest-stakes steps in a transaction and can make or break a deal.
  • Appraisal vs. Valuation vs. Evaluation
    MethodWho Performs ItCore Focus & Output
    AppraisalLicensed or certified appraiser onlyAn official, impartial opinion of a property's market value on a specific date, backed by market research.
    ValuationReal estate licensees (agents/brokers) or non-appraisersAn estimate of property value on a given date (e.g., a Comparative Market Analysis [CMA]); does not require an appraiser's license.
    EvaluationAnalysts, consultants, developers, or licenseesA study of a property's usefulness or utility (past, present, and future highest and best use) rather than assigning a dollar value estimate.
  • Appraisal Reports & Transaction Roles
    • The Appraisal Report: The formal written document summarizing the appraiser's value conclusion and supporting market data, typically ordered during the mortgage origination process.
    • Payment & Appraiser Selection:
      • Who Pays: Usually paid for by the buyer (often included in closing costs).
      • Who Chooses: Usually selected by the mortgage lender (rather than the buyer or seller) to prevent bias and ensure an objective valuation before lending funds.
      • Conflict-of-Interest Rule: Although lenders select the appraiser, it is illegal for a lender to be affiliated with the appraiser (to preserve independence and prevent conflicts of interest).
    • Why the Report Matters to Every Party:
      • Seller: Confirms how much they can realistically charge and support in the market.
      • Buyer: Verifies they are paying a fair market price and not overpaying.
      • Lender: Ensures the collateral is worth enough to secure the mortgage loan without over-lending.
  • Property Types That Can Be Appraised
    • Appraisals are not limited to residential houses—they are performed across virtually all categories of real property, particularly whenever financing is involved:
    • Residential: Single-family homes, condominiums, and multifamily apartment buildings
    • Commercial & Retail: Office buildings, shopping centers, and general commercial properties
    • Agricultural: Farms and rural land
  • Common Situations Requiring an Appraisal
    • Beyond standard mortgage financing, appraisals are routinely ordered for a wide range of legal, financial, and governmental purposes:
    • Sales & Lending: Any real estate sale, and mortgage lending (especially government-backed loans such as FHA or VA loans)
    • Leasing & Investing: Establishing market rental rates for leased properties and determining investment values
    • Government & Taxation: Government property acquisitions (such as eminent domain/condemnation) and property tax assessment appeals
    • Risk Management: Acquiring property insurance coverage
  • Value of Collateral & Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)
    • Why Collateral Value Matters:
      • Collateral: Property pledged as security by a borrower to guarantee repayment and protect a lender's investment.
      • Underwriting Protection: Even if a borrower has strong credit and income, a loan underwriter must verify the property's appraised value—if the borrower defaults and the lender must foreclose, selling the collateral may be the lender's only way to recover the unpaid loan balance.
    • Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV):
      • Core Concept: Compares the loan amount to the property's appraised value (or purchase price, whichever is lower).
      • Formula: Loan Amount ÷ Appraised Value = Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)
      • Risk Relationship: The higher the LTV (meaning a smaller equity gap between the loan balance and the property's appraised value), the greater the lender's risk of financial loss if foreclosure becomes necessary. Because LTV relies directly on an accurate valuation of the collateral, a valid appraisal is essential to mortgage lending.
  • Appraiser Regulation & Federal Oversight (FIRREA)
    • FIRREA (Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act): Established the first federal oversight of the appraisal profession and mandates that states regulate and license appraisers—preventing anyone from calling themselves an appraiser without a valid license.
    • The Appraisal Foundation: Officially recognized by FIRREA as the nonprofit authority (composed of major appraisal organizations) that establishes professional standards and licensing qualifications to guide state regulation.
    • Governing Boards & Subcommittee:
      Oversight BodyCore Function & Responsibilities
      Appraiser Qualifications Board (AQB)Establishes minimum licensing standards used by states, including mandatory training, examinations, and trainee registration.
      Appraisal Standards Board (ASB)Sets standards for actual appraisals by developing, interpreting, and amending USPAP.
      Appraisal Subcommittee (ASC)Monitors state-regulated appraisers to ensure compliance and maintains a searchable national database verifying whether an appraiser's credential is active, suspended, revoked, or surrendered due to disciplinary action.
  • Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP)
    • Core Definition: Adopted by Congress in 1989, USPAP is the generally recognized set of ethical and performance standards for the U.S. appraisal profession (covering real estate, personal property, business, and mass appraisal).
    • Update Cycle: Updated every two years to ensure appraisers deliver unbiased, well-supported opinions of value.
    • Federally Related Real Estate Transactions: Compliance with USPAP is legally mandatory for state-licensed and state-certified appraisers working on federally related transactions—any real estate transaction involving federal agencies or government-sponsored enterprises (such as FHA, VA, Fannie Mae, or Freddie Mac).
  • Who Are Real Estate Appraisers? & Tennessee Classifications
    • Role & Competence Rule: An appraiser is an individual legally authorized to perform formal property valuations, drawing on skills akin to a broker, surveyor, economist, and accountant. However, an appraiser must never undertake any assignment for which they lack the required education, license, or certification.
    • Dual Licensing Rule: It is legal to hold both a real estate license and an appraiser license/certification, provided the licensee strictly avoids any conflict of interest in their professional work.
    • Tennessee Appraiser Classifications:
      • State-Registered Real Estate Appraiser Trainee
      • State Licensed Real Estate Appraiser
      • State Certified Residential Appraiser
      • State Certified General Appraiser
    • Three Core Licensing/Certification Requirements:
      1. Education: Complete required qualifying coursework through an approved appraisal program (plus college credit hours, depending on the classification).
      1. Experience: Accumulate the required supervised appraisal experience hours for the target credential.
      1. Examination: Pass the state-approved written licensing or certification exam.
  • Overview of the Appraisal Process
    • To produce an official estimate of value, an appraiser researches relevant market areas, analyzes property data, and applies professional judgment across eight sequential steps (while keeping all headers formatted as bulleted points as requested).
  • The Eight Steps of an Appraisal
    • Step 1: State the Objective
      • Defines the exact scope, purpose, and parameters of the appraisal assignment before data collection begins:
        • Identify the Subject Property: Provide a complete legal description of the property being evaluated.
        • Establish Property Rights Appraised: Specify the ownership interest being valued—most commonly fee simple ownership (full ownership), though it may involve lesser interests such as a tenant's leasehold rights, an easement, or a right-of-way.
        • State Type of Value: Define the value sought, typically market value.
        • Set the Effective Date: Establish the specific date of valuation because market conditions and property values change over time.
        • Clarify Limitations: Document any limiting conditions or assumptions to define the scope and protect the appraiser.
    • Step 2: List Necessary Data and Sources
      • Identifies which appraisal approach(es) will be used and outlines the required data sources across two categories:
        • General Data: Information covering the surrounding market environment, including the city, region, and neighborhood.
        • Specific Data: Information detailing the subject property itself and comparable properties.
    • Step 3: Gather, Record, and Verify Data
      • Collects and verifies both general regional/economic data and detailed physical descriptions of the subject property and comparables (critical for the sales comparison approach).
      • Photographic Evidence: Appraisers routinely maintain a photo journal documenting property condition to substantiate findings and resolve any post-appraisal disputes.
    • Step 4: Determine the Highest and Best Use
      • Definition: The single most profitable use of a property that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible (viable), and maximally productive (produces the greatest yield).
      • Key Factors Analyzed: Zoning regulations, lot size, deed restrictions, physical characteristics, and alternative uses (e.g., whether a commercial building would yield more value if converted to residential use). The appraiser's final value estimate is based on this highest and best use—even if it differs from the property's current use.
    • Step 5: Estimate the Land Value
      • Evaluates the land parcel separately from any buildings or site improvements by locating, adjusting, and comparing similar vacant land parcels to the subject site.
    • Step 6: Estimate Value with Applicable Approaches
      • Applies one or more formal valuation approaches (such as the Sales Comparison, Cost, or Income Approach) best suited to the property's characteristics.
    • Step 7: Reconcile the Final Value Estimate
      • Reconciliation (Correlation): Combines and weighs the value indicators produced by multiple appraisal approaches into one final value conclusion.
      • No Simple Averaging: The appraiser must not simply average the numbers together; instead, the appraiser assigns the greatest weight to the approach and data most relevant and reliable for the subject property.
    • Step 8: Write and Present the Value Report
      • Compiles the final findings in strict compliance with USPAP standards and delivers the report to the client (written reports are the industry standard, though USPAP also permits oral reports).
  • Uniform Residential Appraisal Report (URAR)
    • Developed by our friends at Fannie Mae and Freddy Mac, the Uniform Residential Appraisal Report has become the standard — the report form of choice for appraisers and lenders alike.
  • The Eight Steps of an Appraisal: Recap
    • And remember, various procedures comprise each of these eight steps, and an appraiser completes all of them (depending upon the particularities of their job, of course).
  • Appraisals and Underwriting
    • Underwriter Verification: Once completed, the appraisal report is sent to the mortgage lender's underwriter to verify that the property and valuation meet all guidelines and collateral standards for the specific loan program.
    • Calling for an Appraisal Review: To double-check the accuracy, completeness, and credibility of the initial report, the underwriter may order an appraisal review conducted by another qualified specialist (either an in-house reviewer or an independent third-party appraiser).
    • Binding Underwriting Figure: When an appraisal review establishes a reviewed value, the lender uses the reviewer's appraised value for final loan underwriting decisions.
  • Types of Appraisal Reviews: Desk Review vs. Field Review
    • Depending on the loan's risk profile, property complexity, and required level of scrutiny, an appraisal review can range from a quick office check to an on-site re-verification:
      Review TypeWho Performs It & WhereScope & Typical Use Case
      Desk ReviewAn in-house specialist or review appraiser working directly at their desk (without visiting the property)• Examines the completed appraisal report, supporting data, and local market records to confirm the form is properly filled out and the appraiser's value conclusions are logically supported by the report's findings.

      • Faster and less costly than an on-site review.
      Field ReviewAn independent third-party appraiser who conducts an on-site exterior/property inspection and verifies comparables in the field• Thoroughly audits the validity of the original appraiser's physical observations, comparable selections, adjustments, and market conclusions.

      • Requires more time and expense, so lenders typically reserve it for higher-risk loans, complex properties, or significant value discrepancies.

Key Term

  • highest and best use - the use of a property that is legal, physically possible, financially viable, and produces the greatest yield
  • Key Concepts & Principles
    • Appraisal
      • An appraisal is an impartial, qualified appraiser's opinion of the value of a specific property as of a specific date, supported by relevant market information. Appraisals can be used for almost any type of property, and they can be ordered for a variety of purposes.
    • Valuation
      • Similar to an appraisal, except that a valuation does NOT have to be performed by a licensed appraiser.
    • Evaluation
      • Evaluation is a statement regarding the usefulness or utility of a property, but not the value of it.
    • Appraisal Reports
      • The tangible result of an appraisal is an appraisal report. This document, created by a licensed appraiser, sums up a property's market value based on collected data.
      • The appraisal report is usually paid for by the buyer. The appraiser who will complete the appraisal report is usually chosen by the lender.
    • Collateral
      • In the event of foreclosure, the value of the collateral property may be the lender's only means of recovering the loan amount. This makes the appraisal an important part of real estate financing.
    • Appraiser Regulation and Oversight
      • The Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (FIRREA) created the first federal oversight of appraisers. FIRREA mandates that states regulate and license appraisers.
      • All appraisers are regulated by the Appraiser Qualifications Board (AQB) of the Appraisal Foundation, which was authorized by Congress to set the minimum requirements for appraisers. The Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP) is the ethical code that appraisers in the United States must follow.

  • General and Specific Data
    • Appraisers use both general and specific data to appraise a property:
    • General data is information about the area surrounding the property. This could include the city, region, and neighborhood in which the property is situated. 🏙
    • Specific data, on the other hand, is information regarding the property itself. 🏠
  • Highest and Best Use
    • An appraiser determines whether a property is serving its highest and best use by considering:
      • Zoning regulations
      • Size of the lot
      • Deed restrictions
      • Potential value of the property if it were to be used for something else (such as if a commercial building was used as a single-family residence)
