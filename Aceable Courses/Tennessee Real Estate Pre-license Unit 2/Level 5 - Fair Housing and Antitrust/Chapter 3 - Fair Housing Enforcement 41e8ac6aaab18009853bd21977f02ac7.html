Chapter 3 - Fair Housing Enforcement

Enforcement Authority & Filing Deadlines (HUD / FHEO vs. Private Civil Lawsuits)

  • Administering Agency: The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), specifically through its Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO), administers and enforces the federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3601 et seq.).
  • Concurrent Jurisdiction & Multiple Venues: Because state and local fair housing laws operate alongside federal law, a single discriminatory act can expose a real estate licensee to simultaneous legal or regulatory actions across multiple venues (federal HUD/DOJ, state fair housing agencies, private civil courts, and state real estate licensing commissions like TREC).
  • Filing Deadlines (Statutes of Limitations):
    • HUD Administrative Complaint (42 U.S.C. § 3610): Must be filed within 1 year (365 days) after the alleged discriminatory housing practice occurred or terminated.
    • Private Civil Lawsuit in Federal or State Court (42 U.S.C. § 3613): Must be filed within 2 years after the most recent instance of discrimination (the 2year clock is paused/tolled while a HUD administrative complaint is pending).
  • Step-by-Step HUD Complaint, Investigation & Adjudication Process
    1. Intake & Submission: The complainant submits names, addresses, dates, and a description of the alleged violation via HUD's Housing Discrimination Complaint Form, letter, phone, or online portal.
      • Substantially Equivalent Agency Referral (FHAP): If the state or local fair housing agency enforces laws certified by HUD as "substantively equivalent" to the federal Fair Housing Act, HUD refers the complaint to that state/local agency and notifies the complainant.
    1. Notice & Respondent's Answer (10Day Windows):
      • Within 10 days of receiving a signed complaint, HUD sends written notice and a copy of the complaint to the respondent (and an acknowledgment to the complainant).
      • Within 10 days of receiving HUD's notice, the respondent must submit a written Answer to the complaint.
    1. Investigation (100Day Target) & Conciliation:
      • HUD investigates the allegations (aiming to complete the investigation within 100 days unless impracticable, in which case HUD notifies the parties in writing).
      • Conciliation (Voluntary Compliance): At any stage of the investigation, HUD attempts to resolve the dispute through informal negotiations. A binding Conciliation Agreement requires acceptance by both parties and approval by the HUD Secretary, providing individual relief to the complainant and safeguarding the public interest. Once signed, HUD closes its investigation—but if the respondent breaches the agreement, HUD refers the breach to the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) for federal court enforcement.
    1. Determination of Reasonable Cause & Charge of Discrimination:
      • If no conciliation agreement is reached, HUD determines whether reasonable cause exists to believe discrimination occurred.
      • If reasonable cause is found, HUD issues a Determination of Reasonable Cause and a Charge of Discrimination.
    1. Forum Election (20Day Window):
      • Within 20 days after receiving notice of the Charge, either the complainant or the respondent may elect to have the case decided in Federal District Court rather than before a HUD Administrative Law Judge (ALJ).

  • Private Civil Lawsuits & When That Right Ends
    • Independent Right to Sue (2Year Window): An aggrieved person does not have to file a HUD complaint first before filing a private civil lawsuit in federal or state court.
    • Filing While a HUD Case Is Pending: A complainant who filed with HUD may still file a private lawsuit if HUD declines to investigate or while the investigation is still ongoing—provided that:
      • No Conciliation Agreement has been signed by the parties, AND
      • A HUD Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) hearing has not yet begun on the record.
    • Once either a Conciliation Agreement is executed or an ALJ hearing commences, the complainant is legally barred from filing a separate private civil lawsuit over that complaint.
  • Filing a Complaint: Summary
  • Prompt Judicial Action for Urgent Fair Housing Complaints (42 U.S.C. § 3610(e))
    • When Immediate Action Is Triggered: Under the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3610(e) and 24 C.F.R. § 103.500), if the HUD Secretary concludes at any time after a complaint is filed that prompt judicial action is necessary to carry out the purposes of the Act, HUD authorizes the U.S. Attorney General (DOJ) to immediately file a civil action for temporary or preliminary relief while HUD's full investigation proceeds. Emergency intervention applies when two core conditions are met:
      1. Irreparable Harm: Serious, immediate injury (such as imminent homelessness or permanent loss of a unique housing opportunity) will likely occur without swift court intervention.
      1. Substantial, Compelling Evidence: Strong preliminary evidence indicates a clear violation of federal fair housing laws.
    • Application to the Landlord Scenario: When an approved tenant who has already given notice on her prior apartment has her lease abruptly revoked upon meeting the landlord because she appears to be of Middle Eastern descent (discrimination based on national origin / race / color), waiting months for a standard investigation would leave her without a home—justifying immediate referral to the Attorney General for emergency injunctive relief.
  • Injunctions: Temporary (Preliminary) vs. Permanent Relief
    • What an Injunction Is: A court order compelling an individual or entity either to refrain from doing a discriminatory act (prohibitory injunction) or to perform an affirmative action (mandatory injunction), such as honoring a lease or serving protected-class clients. Obtaining an injunction does not shield the respondent from ongoing HUD investigation, damages, or civil penalties.
      Type of InjunctionDuration & PurposePractical Effect on a Housing Provider
      Temporary (Preliminary) Injunction / TROShort-term order issued pending the final outcome of HUD's investigation or court trial.Freezes the status quo—for example, prohibiting a landlord or seller from renting or selling the disputed unit to anyone else (or requiring them to let the tenant move in) until a final determination is reached.
      Permanent InjunctionIssued as part of a final judgment after discrimination is proven; lasts indefinitely or for a court-specified period.Permanently bars future discriminatory practices and imposes ongoing compliance obligations (such as mandatory fair housing training, recordkeeping, and non-discriminatory marketing).
  • HUD's Fair Housing Accessibility Guidelines (1991) & Statutory Design Rules (42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(C))
    • Statutory Mandate vs. Technical Safe Harbor (56 FR 9472):
      • Under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(C), covered multifamily dwellings designed and constructed for first occupancy after March 13, 1991 (4 or more units in buildings with an elevator, and all ground-floor units in non-elevator buildings with 4 or more units) are legally required to include core accessible design features.
      • HUD's 1991 Fair Housing Accessibility Guidelines provide voluntary technical specifications (a "safe harbor") showing builders and landlords how to satisfy the statute. Following the Guidelines is not the sole mandatory method of compliance (builders may use other recognized accessibility standards such as ANSI A117.1), but strict compliance provides strong legal protection and safe-harbor proof of non-discrimination in the event of a complaint or lawsuit.
    • Core Accessible Design Standards Covered by the Statute & Guidelines (42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(C)):
      • Public and common-use areas (lobbies, mailrooms, laundry rooms, pools, parking, and walkways) must be readily accessible to and usable by persons with disabilities.
      • All doors into and within covered dwelling units must be sufficiently wide (32inch minimum clear opening) to allow passage by a person using a wheelchair.
      • All covered dwelling interiors must incorporate four adaptive-design features:
        1. An accessible route into and throughout the dwelling unit
        1. Light switches, electrical outlets, thermostats, and environmental controls placed in accessible reach ranges
        1. Reinforcements in bathroom walls to allow later installation of grab bars around toilets and tubs/showers
        1. Usable kitchens and bathrooms with sufficient clear floor space so an individual in a wheelchair can maneuver safely

  • Occupancy Standards & Familial Status (Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998 & Keating Memo)
    • Statutory & Regulatory Framework: Under the Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998 (QHWRA), Congress directed HUD to publish clear enforcement standards governing occupancy limits. In 1998–1999, HUD formally adopted the 1991 Keating Memo (written by former HUD General Counsel Frank Keating) as its official policy.
    • The "Two Occupants Per Bedroom" Guideline—Not a Rigid Rule:
      • As a general baseline, an occupancy standard of two persons per bedroom is presumptively reasonable under the Fair Housing Act.
      • However, HUD never evaluates familial status discrimination solely by counting people per bedroom. Investigators must evaluate contextual and physical factors, including:
        1. Size and configuration of the bedrooms and overall dwelling (e.g., whether there is a large living area, den, or study)
        1. Age of the children (e.g., an infant sharing a bedroom with parents)
        1. Physical capacity of building systems (such as septic tank, sewer, or water system limits)
        1. Discriminatory statements or selective enforcement (such as applying strict occupancy caps only to families with children or restricting common areas)
    • Keating Memo Comparison Example (Family of 5 in a 2-Bedroom Home):
      • Situation 1 (Spacious 2-Bedroom Apartment): A family of five applies to rent a two-bedroom apartment with oversized bedrooms and large living areas. Denying them under a strict two-person-per-bedroom rule likely violates the Fair Housing Act (familial status discrimination).
      • Situation 2 (Small 2-Bedroom Mobile Home): A family of five applies to rent a lot for a compact two-bedroom mobile home with tight living quarters. Denying them based on legitimate space constraints is reasonable and lawful.

  • HUD's Equal Access Rule (2012 & 2016 Updates):
    • Prohibits owners/operators of HUD-assisted or HUD-insured housing and FHA-approved lenders from discriminating based on sexual orientation, gender identity, or marital status. (Note: Federal rulemaking in 2025–2026 proposed revisions to the 2016 rule, while federal courts and HUD Fair Housing Act enforcement continue to treat sexual orientation and gender identity discrimination as prohibited sex discrimination.)
    • Key Regulatory Definitions (2016):
      • Sexual Orientation: One's emotional, romantic, or physical attraction to the same and/or opposite sex.
      • Gender Identity: Covers both actual and perceived gender identity—mirroring disability law, where discrimination based on a perceived protected trait is just as illegal as discrimination based on an actual trait.
  • Department of Justice (DOJ) Enforcement: Pattern or Practice & ADA Violations
    • Fair Housing Act "Pattern or Practice" Lawsuits (42 U.S.C. § 3614):
      • The U.S. Attorney General may file a civil lawsuit directly in Federal District Court whenever there is reasonable cause to believe a person or entity is engaged in a pattern or practice of housing discrimination (regular, systemic practice rather than an isolated incident) OR has denied rights to a group raising an issue of general public importance.
      • Statute of Limitations: To recover civil penalties in a DOJ pattern-or-practice suit, the Attorney General must file within 18 months after the discriminatory practice occurred or terminated.
      • Statutory Civil Penalties (DOJ Pattern-or-Practice Cases): Base statutory maximums in 42 U.S.C. § 3614(d)(1)(C) are $75,000 for a first violation and $150,000 for any subsequent violation (adjusted for inflation under 28 C.F.R. § 85.5 to $131,308 for a first violation and $262,614 for subsequent violations through 2026).
    • Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) Enforcement (Title II & Title III):
      • The Department of Justice enforces ADA accessibility and non-discrimination rules across public entities and places of public accommodation (including real estate brokerage offices and apartment leasing offices).
      • Base statutory civil penalties under the ADA are $55,000 for a first violation and $110,000 for any subsequent violation (likewise subject to periodic federal inflation adjustments).
  • Tennessee Human Rights Commission (THRC) Enforcement & Penalties (T.C.A. § 4-21-301 et seq.)
    • Filing Deadline & Dual-Filing Coordination:
      • Complaints under the Tennessee Human Rights Act (THRA) must be filed with the Tennessee Human Rights Commission (THRC) within 180 days of the alleged discriminatory housing act—compared to 1 year (365 days) for filing with federal HUD.
      • Because THRC is a HUD-certified substantially equivalent fair housing agency, THRC and HUD maintain work-sharing agreements so complaints are coordinated without duplicate investigations.
    • Statutory Civil Penalties Under the THRA (T.C.A. § 4-21-306):
      • First offense: Up to $10,000
      • Second offense (within 5 years): Up to $25,000
      • Further violations (two or more within 7 years): Up to $50,000 (in addition to actual damages, injunctive relief, and reasonable attorney's fees)

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Filing a Fair Housing Complaint Through HUD
    • The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) enforces the federal Fair Housing Act.
    • HUD requires that a fair housing complaint is filed within one year of the violation.
    • A person can also choose to file a civil suit. In that case, the individual has two years from the violation to file a private lawsuit.
    • Depending on state and local laws, there may be other venues in addition to HUD where fair housing cases can be filed. In some cases, multiple venues may have jurisdiction over one case.
    • In other words, a real estate license holder may be subject to more than one legal action for a single act of alleged discrimination.
