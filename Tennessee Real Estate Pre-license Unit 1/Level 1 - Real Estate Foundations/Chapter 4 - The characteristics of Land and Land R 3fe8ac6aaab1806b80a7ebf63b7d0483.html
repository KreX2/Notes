Chapter 4 - The characteristics of Land and Land Rights

Field Notes

    • Physical characteristics drive appeal: Just as the natural features of land (like water access) attract buyers, the physical traits of a home's improvements, especially how roomy it feels, heavily influence real estate decisions.

Land’s Unique Physical Characteristics

  • One way land is special and separate from personal property is that it has three unique physical characteristics:
    • Indestructibility
    • Immobility
    • Non-homogeneity

Indestructible

  • Land is pretty tough to destroy. It’s indestructible. No matter what humans or nature may try to do to a plot of land, it will still be there afterward.
  • Land may:
    • Go underwater 🌊
    • Become a crater 🌑
    • Have improvements added to it (like houses) 🏠
    • But whatever happens, the land will still be there. It’s indestructible, remember? And that’s something you can’t say for personal property.

Immobile

  • You can't move land. It's immobile. When a buyer purchases personal property, they can move it to a new location — wherever their little buyer heart desires. When a buyer purchases land, however, they must go to the land. They cannot move the land to where they are.

Non-Homogeneous

  • Every parcel of land is unique. There is no parcel of land that is exactly the same as another parcel of land.
  • Even two nearly identical lots in a subdivision differ in their position on the Earth. This means that every piece of land is different in some way, making it non-homogeneous (this is also referred to as heterogeneity).

Forces of Nature

  • There are also natural factors that can affect the physical characteristics of land we just learned about. These include:
    • Accession
      • the right to all that one's own property produces, including that which is added, either naturally or by human action, to the property already owned.
      • can also be defined as increasing, adding, or coming to possess a right.
      • an owner having the right to whatever is produced by what they already own (The person who owns the tree also owns the fruits)
    • Accretion
      • the process that results in the gradual increase in land area through deposits of soil by natural forces.
      • the gradual increase of land through the natural buildup of soil (known as alluvion) deposited by waterways. Because soil does not simply disappear, the earth washed away from one property during an event like a flood naturally settles onto another, resulting in a loss of land for one owner and a gain for another.
    • Erosion
      • the gradual loss of land over time. This most often happens when bodies of water wash away bordering land.
      • erosion can significantly alter a property's landscape over time, real estate agents must be transparent about these changes with potential buyers. An agent's duty is not to make the decision for the client, but to provide all factual physical risks so the buyer can make a fully informed purchase.
        • Erosion alters property value and boundaries: The gradual wearing away of land by water or wind can drastically change a property's physical characteristics over time.
        • Honesty is required: You cannot just highlight the beautiful, positive aspects of a natural feature; you must also disclose known environmental risks or changes.
        • Empower the buyer: Your role is to present the complete, unbiased facts of the property so your clients can confidently evaluate the risks and decide for themselves.
    • Reliction
      • a gradual increase in land area when water gradually withdraws.
      • A prolonged drought that causes a lake's water level to recede and expose new, dry land is an example of a natural process called reliction.
    • Avulsion
      • the sudden loss of land by flood or when a stream or river changes course. Because it's a sudden change, no title is lost. Avulsion is caused by water.
        • This is not to be confused with alluvion, which is the soil that gets deposited in accretion.
      • One way to remember avulsion is that it sounds like convulsion, which is a sudden movement, and avulsion is the sudden loss of land.

Land's Economic Characteristics

  • Land also has economic characteristics that set it apart from personal property:
    • Scarcity
      • the economic characteristic that informs the economic principle of supply and demand. The supply of land will never increase, but the demand for land will increase as the population grows. And this is because land is scarce.
    • Situs
      • refers to the economic significance of a property's location. The popular real estate phrase, “location, location, location,” is referring to the preferences people have for certain areas.
      • Situs takes into account not only the geographical location of a property, but also the economic qualities of that area that make it more or less desirable.
    • Modification
      • refers to the fact that the value of land can be affected by human-made changes to the land (Modifications often affect situs)
      • The farmland surrounding Disney World in Orlando is a prime example of modification (or situs), where the economic value of a property drastically increases not because of the land itself, but because of the man-made improvements and commercial developments built nearby. Human-made changes (modifications) can and will affect the value of land.
    • Fixity
      • refers to the fact that real estate exists in a fixed location and cannot be moved. (This economic characteristic is very similar to the physical characteristic of immobility.) Fixity describes this truth about land (it can't be moved) from an economic viewpoint.
    • Illiquidity
      • the relative difficulty of converting an asset to cash without loss of value. Real estate tends to be illiquid compared to other investments like stocks or bonds.
      • If you want to sell a stock, you can probably find a buyer that same day willing to pay the market price. That's not true of a piece of property. If you need to sell very quickly, you may not get the full market value of the property.
      • If you are forced to sell a property immediately, you will inevitably have to slash the price and sacrifice its true market value just to close the deal.

Appurtenances

  • ⤷ another type of right that comes with property, and they are related to the physical use of a property (as opposed to the more conceptual rights in the bundle of rights).
  • ⤷ air rights, water rights, subsurface rights, etc. Appurtenances are most often transferred with the property, but it's possible to sell them separately.
    • Because subsurface rights can be severed and sold independently from the land, any future buyer of a property, whose mineral rights were previously sold to an oil company, they will not acquire those mineral rights and must legally honor an oil company's pre-existing ownership beneath the surface
  • What Rights Are Appurtenances?
    • Surface rights (sometimes called the right of access)
      • ⤷ the rights to access the surface area of a parcel of real property. They govern both natural elements and structures (or improvements) built on or attached to the uppermost layer of the land.
      • Surface rights give a person the right to access, use, and build things on the surface of a piece of land.
      • A homeowner who elects to build a barn in his backyard to house his amiable herd of sheep is exercising his surface rights.
    • Subsurface rights (sometimes called mineral, oil, or gas rights)
      • ⤷ ownership rights to everything underneath a piece of land, extending downward to the center of the earth. Subsurface rights are important because things like oil, gas, or other valuable minerals may be found below the surface of the land.
      • commonly referred to as mineral rights
        • Subsurface rights can be sold separately from surface rights
      • Note: When surface and subsurface rights are owned by different people, the subsurface owner has the legal right to enter the land and extract resources, provided they do not materially disturb the surface. Therefore, it is crucial for real estate agents to verify exactly which rights their clients are purchasing so they aren't caught off guard by someone legally extracting minerals from beneath their new property.
      • Minerals as Property - In the process of being extracted from the land, they move from being real property to being personal property.
      • Rule of Capture - Under the rule of capture, a landowner is legally entitled to all the oil and gas extracted from a well on their property, even if those resources naturally migrated from beneath a neighbor's land.
        • Owner A and Owner B both have oil underneath their land.
        • But Owner B takes all of the oil because the oil flows towards the lowest point of pressure, i.e. the point from which Owner B is actively pumping oil.
        • Owner B has the right to take all that oil.
    • Air rights
      • ⤷ the right to use the air space above the surface of land
      • Air space is the area above ground that is owned along with a property. It could include space above a piece of land, or the space owned inside a condominium unit.
      • Air rights govern the usable vertical space above a property and can be sold or leased independently, allowing smaller buildings to sell their unused airspace to developers looking to exceed standard zoning heights. However, before building vertically, developers must verify these rights as they are always subject to local zoning limits and aviation flight paths.
    • Water rights
      • ⤷ the right of a property owner on a waterfront to access and use that water.
      • When thinking about water rights, license holders should ask themselves three questions:
        • Who controls the water in question? The state or the property owner?
        • Is the water moving?
        • Is the water navigable?
      • Unlike water-scarce states that use the doctrine of prior appropriation to strictly regulate water usage through government permits, Tennessee operates under common law riparian, littoral, and percolating water rights.
      • Common Law - There are two types of rights associated with waterfront properties
        • Riparian rights (River and Streams)
          • govern the use of flowing water, such as rivers and streams that pass through or border a property.
          • In accordance with riparian rights, a property owner does NOT own the water, but they can use the water. All property owners along a body of flowing water will have the right to use the water.
        • Littoral rights (Lake and Oceans)
          • govern lakefront or oceanfront property and usually allow the property owner to use the water bordering their property.
          • Littoral use prohibits the property owner from artificially changing the water’s location.
        • Percolating Water Rights (Underground)
          • refer to the use and ownership of underground water. Real property owners have a right to access that water by installing wells for their own use.
      • Ownership and Navigability
        • Under U.S. law, property boundaries along water depend on navigability: owners hold land only up to the water's edge for navigable rivers and all littoral (standing) waters. However, for non-navigable flowing waters, the adjoining property lines extend to the exact midpoint beneath the water.
  • Rights
    • Appurtenances secure a right for one party. An appurtenance might also limit the right of another party.
      • Let's say Linda owns the surface rights of a property and Maura owns the subsurface rights. When Linda sells the property to Kent, Maura's rights are protected and Kent's rights are slightly limited.
  • Supporting Rights
    • A person who owns property has the right to not have their land move around because a neighbor has decided to dig a hole. Even if someone is digging entirely on their own property, if it disturbs an adjacent property, that's not okay. This concept is known as supporting rights.
      • Lateral support is provided by adjacent properties.
      • Subjacent support comes from the underlying earth.
      • If a landowner doing an excavation shifted or disturbed a neighboring property, they could be held liable for damages.

    • Air rights: govern the area above the property, and can be sold separately
    • Surface rights: govern what happens on the surface of the land, and include natural elements and improvements
    • Subsurface rights: also known as mineral rights; they deal with what's beneath the surface of the land. They can also be sold separately. Subsurface right holders have priority over surface right holders, so long as the surface isn't being disturbed.

    • Water rights: either operate under prior appropriation or common law. If a state operates under common law, water rights are broken into riparian rights (for flowing bodies of water), littoral rights (for still bodies), and percolating rights (underground water). Remember, these rights are determined by states and even municipalities.
