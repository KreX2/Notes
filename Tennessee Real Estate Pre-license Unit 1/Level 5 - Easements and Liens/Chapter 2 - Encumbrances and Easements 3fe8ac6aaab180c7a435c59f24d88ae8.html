Chapter 2 - Encumbrances and Easements

Role of the Title Commitment & Agent Responsibility

  • Buyer Diligence: Buyers must thoroughly review the title commitment during transactions to identify all existing property encumbrances prior to closing.
  • License Holder Duty: Real estate agents guide buyers through identifying encumbrances and determining whether they can be resolved before closing or must be accepted.

  • Primary Encumbrance Types
    • Easements:
      • Definition: A non-possessory right to use another person's property for a specific, limited purpose (e.g., shared driveways).
      • Interest vs. Estate: Gives the holder an interest in the land, but not an estate or possessory ownership.
      • Runs with the Land: Attaches to the property and automatically transfers to new owners upon conveyance.
    • Liens:
      • Financial Burden: A creditor's monetary claim against real or personal property pledged by a debtor as collateral for a debt.
    • Deed Restrictions:
      • Private Controls: Restrictive covenants attached to a deed that establish non-governmental limits on land use.
    • Leases:
      • Limitation on Owner Possession: Encumbers property by transferring exclusive possessory rights to a tenant, preventing the property owner from occupying or freely using the property during the lease term.
    • Cloud on Title & Suit to Quiet Title:
      • Cloud on Title: A dispute, defect, or uncertainty affecting an otherwise clear title (often stemming from missing heirs, divorce settlements, unreleased claims, or fraud).
      • Suit to Quiet Title: A court lawsuit initiated by a property owner to resolve ownership disputes and extinguish unrecorded claims. A successful ruling clears the title record and permanently bars future challenges to ownership.
      • Causes of Clouds on Title
        • Common Triggers:
          • Property owner dying without a probated will (unresolved heirship).
          • Unresolved ownership claims following a divorce.
          • Fraudulent conveyances or forged deeds.
          • Outstanding or unreleased liens against the property.
          • Pending or active foreclosure proceedings.
      • Clearing Clouds via Quitclaim Deed
        • Function & Purpose: A legal instrument used to terminate or relinquish any interest, right, or potential claim an individual may hold in a property's title.
        • Application in Death & Divorce:
          • Divorce: Formally removes an ex-spouse's legal claim or interest from the title.
          • Estates/Death: Requires potential heirs to explicitly sign away any potential inheritance claims to ensure clear ownership.
        • Title Clearance Effect: The grantor "quits" or gives up any claim they might have, eliminating title uncertainty without making any warranties about title validity.
      • Title Clouds Caused by Fraud
        • Multiple Conveyances: Occurs when fraudulent actors transfer or attempt to sell the same property to multiple buyers.
        • Court Resolution: Fraudulent claims cannot be resolved simply with a quitclaim deed; they must be litigated in court (such as through a suit to quiet title), where a judge decides the single rightful owner.
  • Non-Encumbrances: Licenses vs. Encumbrance
    • Legal Classification: Licenses affect how land is used, but they are not encumbrances because they do not create a legal burden on the property title or run with the land.
    • Definition & Characteristics of a License
      • Definition: Revocable and temporary permission granted by a property owner to another individual for a specific, limited purpose.
      • Key Features:
        • Revocable: Can be canceled or withdrawn by the landowner at any time.
        • Personal Right: Granted to a specific individual rather than attached to the land; it cannot be assigned or transferred to anyone else.
        • Does Not Run with the Land: Does not attach to the deed or transfer upon sale.
      • Automatic Termination: Ends immediately if the property is sold, transferred, or if either the landowner or license holder dies.
      • Practical Example: A landowner granting verbal permission to a neighbor to hunt or camp on their property.
    • Key Differences: License vs. Easement
      • Revocability: A license can be revoked at any time by the owner; an easement is a non-revocable, permanent legal interest.
      • Creation: A license can be established through a simple oral agreement; an easement generally requires a formal written agreement, deed, or legal court action.
      • Duration: A license is temporary; an easement exists indefinitely (unless formally terminated).
      • Property Attachment: A license attaches to the person (personal privilege); an easement attaches to the property and runs with the land.
      • Transferability & Death: A license cannot be assigned and terminates upon the death of either party; an easement transfers automatically to future owners and survives the death of either party.
    • Non-Encumbrances: Encroachments
      • Definition & Core Characteristics:
        • An encroachment is the unauthorized physical intrusion of a structure, object, or property feature across a property boundary onto an adjacent parcel.
        • Occurs without the direct legal permission or consent of the affected neighboring landowner.
      • Common Examples:
        • Fences constructed beyond actual property lines.
        • Driveways or paved paths extending onto neighboring land.
        • Physical structures, roof eaves, sheds, or overhanging tree branches and roots crossing boundary lines.
      • Impact on Property & Title:
        • Represents a physical trespass rather than a legal encumbrance on title, though it can create major title defects or boundary disputes if discovered during a survey.
        • If left unaddressed for a long enough statutory period, an uncorrected encroachment can potentially mature into an easement by prescription or a claim of adverse possession.
    • Encroachment Types & Characteristics
      • Most Common Instances:
        • Fences constructed off the actual property line (either cutting into a neighbor's lot or leaving owned land outside the boundary).
        • Driveways crossing neighboring lot edges.
        • Roof overhangs or eaves of sheds and garages extending across property lines.
      • Nature of Infraction: Most encroachments are accidental and unintentional rather than malicious, making friendly communication the best initial remedy.
    • Strategies for Resolving Encroachments
      • Relocation Request: Asking the neighbor to move or remove the intruding structure.
      • Land Purchase / Boundary Adjustment: The encroaching party buys the small strip of land in question, legally adjusting the lot boundary lines.
      • Legal Remedy: Filing a lawsuit to compel the removal of the encroachment.
      • Prohibited Action (Self-Help): A property owner cannot legally take matters into their own hands to destroy, alter, or remove an encroaching structure or tree on their own initiative.
    • Identifying Encroachments: The Role of Surveys
      • Definition of Survey: The physical process and visual document mapping property boundaries, dimensions, improvements, easements, and physical encroachments.
      • Detection Tool: Surveys are typically required in real estate conveyances to verify clear property boundaries and uncover undisclosed encroachments before closing.
      • Agent Best Practice: License holders should review existing property surveys with sellers at the listing appointment and inquire if any new structures or neighbor improvements have been added since the drawing date.
      • Recording Documents & Constructive Notice
        • Public Records: Legal instruments such as deeds, mechanic's liens, judgment liens, and easements must be formally recorded in public land records.
        • Title Transparency: Recording documents creates a public history of title, allowing buyers to discover encumbrances and ensure title is transferred cleanly.
    • Identifying Encroachments via Property Surveys
      • Early Detection: Crucial to identify physical boundary overlaps as early as possible to protect land rights and guarantee complete conveyance in a sale.
        • Definition of a Survey: The professional process and physical document that measures property boundaries, dimensions, location of improvements, easements, and physical encroachments.
        • Agent Best Practice at Listing:
          • Request and inspect an existing property survey from the seller during the initial listing appointment.
          • Ask the seller if any new structures or additions have been built on the property since the survey was drawn.
          • Inquire whether adjacent neighbors have constructed any fences, driveways, or structures near the property lines.
      • Recording Public Documents & Title History
        • Mandatory Recording: Critical legal instruments involving property ownership and encumbrances—such as deeds, mechanic's liens, and judgment liens—must be officially recorded in public land records.
        • Constructive Notice & Title History: Recording creates an official public record of title history, allowing prospective buyers and title officers to verify encumbrances and ensure property transfers cleanly free from unrecorded claims.
    • Buyer Due Diligence & Discovery of Encumbrances
      • Buyer Responsibilities: Potential buyers are responsible for performing due diligence to identify existing liens, easements, encroachments, or clouds on title prior to closing.
      • Discovery Methods:
        • Title Search: Conducted by title companies reviewing public records to uncover recorded claims, liens, and ownership history.
        • Physical Inspection & Survey: Reveals unrecorded physical features, visible property uses, and boundary encroachments.
    • Constructive Notice
      • Definition & Legal Presumption: The legal presumption that an individual has knowledge of a claim or fact because the information is publicly available and accessible through reasonable due diligence.
      • How Constructive Notice Is Established:
        • Public Recording: Officially filing deeds, mortgages, liens, or easements in public land records serves as automatic constructive notice to the entire world.
        • Visible Physical Possession/Use: Physically occupying, improving, or visibly using a property also provides constructive notice of an ownership or usage interest.
      • Legal Impact of Failure to Record: If a party fails to record their legal interest in the public record, their claim to the property may be forfeited or rendered difficult to enforce against subsequent good-faith buyers.
    • Actual Notice
      • Definition: Direct, express knowledge or awareness that a person personally possesses regarding a fact or claim.
      • Progression from Inspection: Occurs when a buyer personally reviews public records, conducts a physical site inspection, or receives direct verbal/written communication revealing an encumbrance or claim.
    • Practical Scenario: Visual Notice of Easements (Utility Poles)
      • Scenario: A buyer inspects a property and sees utility poles erected across the yard.
      • Notice Mechanics:
        • The physical presence of the poles serves as visible constructive notice of the utility company's easement rights.
        • Observing the poles gives the buyer actual notice (direct knowledge), obligating them to inquire further and verify the utility company's usage rights—even if the underlying easement document was not formally recorded in public land records.
    • Recap: Encumbrances
      • Lien: a claim or charge against property, usually as security for a debt
      • Easement: an interest in, or a right to use, another individual’s land or property, generally for a specific, limited purpose
      • Deed restriction: a provision in a deed that limits the use of a property
      • Cloud on the title: a dispute on an otherwise clear title
    • While they're not encumbrances, these can also affect property owners:
      • License: the revocable and temporary permission to use someone else’s property for a specific purpose
      • Encroachment: physical property that crosses the boundary into a neighboring landowner's property
  • Easements: Core Concept & Characteristics
    • Definition: An interest or legal right granted to an individual or entity to access or use another person's real property for a specific, limited purpose.
    • Nature as an Encumbrance: Easements attach to the property title and run with the land, automatically transferring to future owners upon conveyance.
    • Scope of Interest: Grants a right of use or control, but does not convey ownership, possessory rights, or an estate in the property.
    • Classification: Affirmative vs. Negative Easements
      • Affirmative Easement:
        • Definition: Grants a non-owner the legal right to perform a specific physical action on or make active use of another owner's property.
        • Frequency: Represents the vast majority of easements created in real estate.
        • Practical Example: An easement granting members of the public or neighboring owners the right to cross a private path or gate to access a public beach.
      • Negative Easement:
        • Definition: Restricts or prevents a property owner from exercising a legal right on their own land that would otherwise be entirely lawful.
        • Purpose: Designed to protect specific benefits or features enjoyed by an adjacent property.
        • Practical Example: A view or light easement prohibiting a property owner from building a multi-story structure or planting tall trees that would obstruct a neighbor's scenic view or natural sunlight.
    • How Easements Are Created
      • Easement by Grant (Express Grant):
        • Created explicitly within a written deed or legal instrument.
        • Occurs when a grantor formally creates an easement provision during a property conveyance (e.g., selling a parcel while explicitly reserving or granting a right-of-way access).
      • Mutual Agreement:
        • Established when two or more property owners formally agree to create an easement for mutual convenience or access requirements.
      • Operation of Law (Easement by Implication / Implied Easement):
        • Arises automatically through operation of law rather than explicit deed language.
        • Occurs when a single property is subdivided and there is an obvious, longstanding, apparent use across one portion that is reasonably necessary for the enjoyment of another portion.
      • Public Recording of Easements:
        • To be legally enforceable against subsequent purchasers, easements are recorded in the public records of the county in which the property is located, establishing constructive notice.

    • Easement Appurtenant
      • Core Concept & Characteristics
        • Definition: An easement existing between two adjacent parcels of land owned by different parties, granting one owner the right to cross or use a portion of the neighboring land
        • Runs with the Land: Attaches directly to the real estate title and automatically transfers upon conveyance; cannot be unilaterally terminated or discontinued by a new property owner.
      • Appurtenances
        • Definition: Legal rights or privileges attached to real property ownership that run with the land.
        • Conveyance: Automatically transfers with the property deed during a sale (though certain appurtenances may be transferred or sold separately in specific cases).
      • Dominant vs. Servient Tenements (Estates)
        • Dominant Tenement / Estate: The parcel of land that benefits from the easement (holds the legal right to use or cross the adjacent property).
        • Servient Tenement / Estate: The parcel of land that is burdened by the easement (must allow the neighboring owner to cross or use the property).
      • Scenario Breakdown: The Shared Driveway (Sapna & Raphael)
        • Setup: Sapna and Raphael own neighboring properties. A shared driveway is located on Sapna's land, but Raphael must drive over a section of it to access his own driveway.
        • Creation Method: Established as an easement appurtenant by grant included in the original deed by the builder.
        • Estate Roles:
          • Sapna holds the servient tenement (her land is burdened by allowing access).
          • Raphael holds the dominant tenement (his property benefits from the access right).
        • Conveyance Impact: If Sapna sells her home, the easement remains intact and binding on the buyer, who must continue allowing Raphael (and future owners of his parcel) driveway access.
    • Easement in Gross
      • Core Concept & Characteristics
        • Definition: An easement granted directly to a specific person or entity (the dominant tenant) rather than attached to a specific parcel of dominant land.
        • Transferability: The holder of an easement in gross can sell, assign, or transfer the easement rights to another person or company.
        • Practical Example: An electric utility company holding the right to enter private land to clear tree branches around power lines.
      • Easement Appurtenant vs. Easement in Gross
        • Easement Appurtenant: Attaches directly to the land itself and runs with the land permanently, regardless of changes in property ownership.
        • Easement in Gross: Attaches to the individual or entity. If granted to an individual, it expires upon that person's death; if granted on a property, it terminates when the burdened property changes hands (unless explicitly renewed).
        • Mnemonic Device: "People are gross and land is permanent (appurtenant)."
      • Scenario Comparison: The Boat Ramp
        • Easement Appurtenant Version:
          • Party A holds a boat ramp easement over Party B's property.
          • If Party B sells to Party C, the easement remains in effect because it attaches to the land—Party A retains the right to use Party C's boat ramp.
        • Easement in Gross Version:
          • Party A holds a personal boat ramp easement over Party B's property.
          • If Party B sells to Party C, the easement terminates upon the transfer of title; Party A must negotiate a new easement with Party C to continue using the ramp.
    • Floating Easement
      • A floating easement just means that there is no exact, fixed location that the easement grants access to. So, instead of a clearly marked path, the dominant estate has access to cross the land wherever they need.
      • If the servient estate was to build a clearly marked path, then the easement might stop being considered a floating easement.
    • Easement by Necessity
      • Definition: A specific type of implied easement created by operation of law when a property becomes completely landlocked and unusable without access across adjacent land.
      • Triggering Event (Subdivision): Typically occurs when a landowner subdivides a larger parcel into smaller lots, leaving one of the resulting parcels completely surrounded by private land with no direct access to a public road.
      • Strict Legal Necessity: Arises because there is no other choice or alternative route for ingress and egress, making access across a neighboring property an absolute legal requirement for the reasonable use of the dominant estate.
    • Conservation Easement
      • Core Concept & Purpose
        • Definition: A voluntary, legally binding easement created to permanently protect and preserve land from commercial or residential development.
        • Relinquishment of Rights: The property owner surrenders certain rights (such as the right to subdivide or build structures) while retaining ownership and the ability to use the land for limited, non-destructive purposes (e.g., recreation, forestry, or agriculture).
      • Selling & Enforcement (Conservation Land Trusts)
        • Runs with the Land: The conservation easement remains attached to the title upon sale or transfer, permanently binding all future property owners.
        • Land Trusts: Enforced and managed by non-profit organizations (such as conservation land trusts or water trusts) that ensure the easement restrictions are upheld.
        • Public & Natural Use: Land trusts may manage or utilize conserved properties to maintain open spaces, parks, hiking trails, and natural habitats for public enjoyment.
      • Financial & Tax Incentives for Property Owners
        • Property Tax Relief: Lowered property assessment values leading to reduced local property tax obligations.
        • Income Tax Credits & Deductions: Eligibility for state and federal income tax credits or charitable contribution deductions.
        • Estate Tax Benefits: Significant estate tax reductions, making it easier to pass large land holdings down to heirs without forcing a land sale to pay estate taxes.
    • Easement by Prescription
      • Core Concept & Characteristics
        • Definition: An easement granted when a non-owner uses another person's property continuously, openly, and adversely without the owner's permission for a legally prescribed period of time.
        • Statutory Period (Tennessee Example): Requires at least 20 years of continuous, unpermitted use by the non-owner.
        • Legal Outcome: Established via a court order granting legal rights of use (not title ownership of the land).
      • Key Legal Requirements
        • Open & Notorious: The use cannot be secret or hidden; it must be obvious and visible to anyone observing the property, ensuring an attentive owner would be aware of it.
        • Hostile & Adverse: The user acts without the landowner's permission and against the owner's best legal interests.
          • Critical Distinction: If the property owner gives permission, an easement by prescription cannot be created (it becomes a license or permissive use instead).
      • Adverse Possession (Title by Prescription)
        • Definition: The legal process by which a non-owner can acquire full legal ownership/title to private property by taking physical possession and using the land for a statutory period when the actual owner fails to assert their rights.
        • Adverse Possession vs. Easement by Prescription:
          • Easement by Prescription: Grants legal rights of use only.
          • Adverse Possession: Grants actual legal ownership (title) of the property.
      • Statutory Durations & Color of Title
        • Tennessee Timeframes:
          • 7 Years: Duration required if the possessor claims under color of title.
          • 20 Years: Duration required if the possessor does not have color of title.
        • Color of Title: A written document or claim that appears to grant good and valid title to real property, but is actually legally defective or invalid.
      • Scope, Purpose & Licensee Responsibility
        • Government Land Exclusion: Adverse possession applies only to private real estate; public or government-owned land is immune to adverse possession claims.
        • Legal Purpose: Intended to resolve ambiguous title/boundary disputes and clear land records over time, rather than promote property theft.
        • License Holder Best Practice: Because adverse possession claims involve complex legal proceedings, real estate agents should refrain from offering legal advice and immediately advise clients facing title or boundary disputes to seek competent legal counsel.
    • Easement for Light and Air
      • Definition & Classification: A negative appurtenant easement that prevents an adjoining landowner from building or taking actions that block natural sunlight, ventilation, or air flow to the dominant tenement.
      • Alternative Term: Also commonly referred to as a view easement.
    • Easement by Condemnation
      • Definition: An easement created when private property is taken or encumbered for public use.
      • Eminent Domain Authority: Granted through the power of eminent domain, which gives the government the legal authority to take private property for public benefit.
      • Just Compensation Requirement: The government or condemning authority is constitutionally required to pay the property owner fair and just compensation for the taken rights or diminished property value.
      • Common Applications: Created to fulfill a public necessity, such as widening public highways, expanding utility lines, or establishing public infrastructure access across private land.
    • Party Walls
      • Definition & Core Concept: A single physical wall shared between two separate, adjoining properties.
      • Easement Mechanics: Each property owner holds a mutual appurtenant easement in the portion of the wall located on the neighboring property, granting both parties equal rights of use.
      • Common Occurrence: Frequently found in condominiums, townhomes, and attached row houses.
      • Maintenance Obligations: Maintenance and repair costs associated with the shared wall are split between both property owners.

Key Terms

  • Adverse Possession: Involuntary transfer of title from an owner who does not use or inspect their land for a number of years to another person who has some claim to the land and takes possession.
  • Alienation: The transfer of ownership (title) to real property; may be voluntary or involuntary.
  • Appurtenances: Rights that run with real property ownership; most often transferred with the property, but possible to sell separately.
  • Cloud: Any claim to or encumbrance on the title to a property that affects transferability.
  • Easement: An interest in, or a right to use, another individual’s land or property, generally for a specific, limited purpose.
  • Easement by Condemnation: An easement in which private property is taken for public use via eminent domain.
  • Easement in Gross: An easement that applies to a person or entity, not specific land.
  • Encroachment: Physical property that crosses the boundary into a neighboring landowner's property.
  • Involuntary Alienation: Transfer of title to property without the owner’s consent, usually carried out by operation of law.
  • Lien: The claim made by a creditor against real or personal property pledged by a borrower as collateral.
  • Quiet Title Suit: A lawsuit brought with the purpose of removing any outstanding ownership claim, title defect, or encumbrance on a property.

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Encumbrance Categories:
    • Lien: A claim or charge against property, usually as security for a debt.
    • Easement: An interest in, or right to use, another's land for a specific, limited purpose.
    • Deed Restriction: A provision in a deed that limits the use of a property.
    • Cloud on Title: A dispute or defect on an otherwise clear title.
  • Non-Encumbrance Property Impacts:
    • License: Revocable and temporary permission to use someone else’s property for a specific purpose.
    • Encroachment: Physical property intrusion across a neighboring property boundary line.
  • Affirmative vs. Negative Easements:
    • Affirmative Easement: Grants someone the right to physically use a property for a specific purpose (most common type).
    • Negative Easement: Prevents a property owner from doing something on their own land that would otherwise be legal (e.g., blocking views or light).
  • Creation of Easements
    • By Deed (Easement by Grant): Created explicitly within a deed or legal document.
    • By Agreement: Created via mutual consent between the involved parties.
    • By Operation of Law (Easement by Implication): Created through land division and longstanding, necessary use.
  • Easement Appurtenant vs. Easement in Gross:
    • Easement Appurtenant: Runs with the land and attaches to properties regardless of ownership changes.
    • Easement in Gross: Attached directly to specific individuals or entities rather than to a parcel of land.
  • Termination of Easements:
    • Abandonment: Non-use combined with explicit intent/action to give up the easement.
    • Release: Written, recorded agreement by the dominant tenant surrendering easement rights.
    • Prescription: Loss of the easement due to non-use for a continuous 10-year period.
    • Merger: Combining the dominant and servient estates under a single owner.
    • Expiration of Purpose: The underlying necessity or purpose for the easement no longer exists or the property is destroyed.
    • Court Action: Cancellation through a lawsuit (suit to quiet title).
