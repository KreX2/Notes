Chapter 1 - Fair Housing Law

Early Civil Rights Legislation & Executive Actions

  • Discrimination Defined: The unjust or prejudicial treatment of different categories of people based on race, sex, religion, disability, or other protected characteristics.
  • Foundational Federal Milestones Before 1968:
    YearLegislation / OrderScope & Real Estate Impact
    1866Civil Rights Act of 1866• The original federal fair housing statute; prohibited housing discrimination based on race or color.

    • Initially had limited practical impact due to prevailing discriminatory laws and practices, until later Supreme Court rulings confirmed that racial discrimination in housing is always illegal—with zero exceptions.
    1962Executive Order 11063• Issued by President John F. Kennedy to prohibit discrimination in the sale or lease of property owned or funded by the federal government, including homes financed with FHA or VA loans.

    • Had limited reach at the time because few transactions involved federal funding.
    1964Civil Rights Act of 1964• Landmark legislation prohibiting discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin.

    • In housing, its reach was narrow because it only barred discrimination in publicly funded housing projects.
  • Key U.S. Supreme Court Fair Housing Decisions
    • Buchanan v. Warley (1917): Ruled that racially based municipal zoning ordinances are unconstitutional and illegal.
    • Shelley v. Kraemer (1948): Ruled that judicial enforcement of racially restrictive deed covenants by state or federal courts is unconstitutional and illegal.
    • Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. (1968): Ruled that all racial discrimination—both public and private—is illegal in the sale or rental of real property, making the Civil Rights Act of 1866 fully enforceable without exceptions.
  • The Fair Housing Act of 1968 (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968)
    • Enactment & Goal: Passed by Congress in April 1968 shortly after the assassination of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., Title VIII—known as the Fair Housing Act—aims to create a unitary housing market where an individual's background never restricts their access to housing.
    • Core Prohibition: Makes it unlawful to refuse to sell, rent to, or negotiate with any person because of their membership in a federally protected class.
    • Comprehensive Transaction Coverage: Applies across every aspect of a real estate transaction, including sales, rentals, advertising, mortgage financing, brokerage services, and property appraisals.
  • Federal Fair Housing Act Protected Classes Overview
    • Definition: Under the federal Fair Housing Act (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, as amended), protected classes are seven specific categories of individuals whom it is illegal to discriminate against in housing transactions:
      1. Race
      1. Color
      1. National Origin
      1. Religion
      1. Sex
      1. Disability
      1. Familial Status
  • Detailed Breakdown of the Seven Federally Protected Classes
    • 1. Race (1968 / 1866):
      • Interpreted broadly by the U.S. Supreme Court to include individuals sharing a common history, language, culture, or ancestry.
      • As of the 2020 U.S. Census, racial categories include White, Black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, and Some Other Race.
    • 2. Color (1968 / 1866):
      • Defined by courts and the federal government as skin pigmentation, complexion, shade, or tone—distinct from race (since individuals of the same race can have different skin tones).
      • Colorism: Treating people with lighter skin more favorably than people with darker skin (e.g., a property manager favoring lighter-skinned Black tenants over darker-skinned Black tenants) is unlawful discrimination based on color.
    • 3. National Origin (1968):
      • Based on a person's country of birth, ancestral origin, ethnicity, culture, or language.
      • Prohibited Examples: Treating a tenant poorly for speaking with an accent or speaking a language other than English, or steering an applicant toward a particular neighborhood based on their surname.
      • Immigration Status & Retaliation Protections: Everyone in the U.S. is protected under the Fair Housing Act regardless of immigration status; threatening to report an individual to law enforcement or U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) for filing a fair housing complaint is illegal intimidation and retaliation.
    • 4. Religion (1968):
      • Protects a person's system of beliefs and practices relating to the divine (e.g., Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, Judaism) as well as non-belief.
      • While individuals may voluntarily choose to live near their religious community, real estate agents may never steer buyers or tenants into or out of neighborhoods based on religion.
    • 5. Sex (Added in 1974):
      • Added by Congress in 1974; covers sex-based discrimination as well as sexual harassment in housing (such as landlords demanding sexual favors or creating a sexually hostile living environment).
      • Sexual Orientation & Gender Identity: Federal fair housing enforcement and court precedent interpret the prohibition on sex discrimination to encompass sexual orientation and gender identity (reinforced by HUD's Equal Access Rule, which prohibits owners/operators of HUD-assisted housing and FHA-approved lenders from discriminating based on actual or perceived sexual orientation, gender identity, or marital status). Over 21 states, Washington, D.C., and more than 200 local jurisdictions also enforce explicit LGBTQIA+ housing protections.
    • 6. Disability (Added in 1988):
      • Added by the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988; defined as a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.
      • Examples: Blindness, deafness, mobility impairments, cognitive impairments, mental illness, HIV/AIDS, cancer, and alcoholism (note: current illegal use of a controlled substance is not protected).
    • 7. Familial Status (Added in 1988):
      • Also added by the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988; protects households with one or more children under age 18 living with a parent or legal custodian, as well as pregnant women and individuals in the process of securing legal custody (such as adoption or foster care).
      • Key Rules:
        • Legal guardians qualify: Applies equally whether children live with biological parents, step-parents, foster parents, grandparents, or any adult with legal custody.
        • Marital status is irrelevant: Single, divorced, widowed, or separated parents receive the exact same fair housing protection as married couples.
        • Landlords cannot refuse to rent to families with children unless the property meets strict statutory requirements for Housing for Older Persons.

  • Groups & Criteria Not Protected Under the Fair Housing Act
    • Real estate licensees and housing providers retain the legal right to screen out applicants based on objective, non-discriminatory business criteria:
      1. Minors (Under Age 18): Individuals who are too young to have legal capacity to enter into a binding contract (note: households with children under 18 are protected under familial status, whereas an unemancipated minor applying alone lacks contractual capacity).
      1. Financial Inability to Pay / Poor Credit: Applicants who have a poor credit score, insufficient income, or inability to qualify for a mortgage loan or pay rent.
    • Criminal Records & HUD Guidance:
      • While individuals with a criminal history are not a named protected class under the Fair Housing Act, blanket bans or arbitrary rejections based on criminal records can trigger liability for disparate impact discrimination.
      • Following federal court rulings and 2016 HUD guidance, housing providers are urged to exercise caution—evaluating criminal convictions individually (considering the nature, severity, and time elapsed since the offense) rather than relying on arrest records or blanket exclusions that disproportionately impact protected racial or ethnic groups.
  • Core Principles of Fair Housing Prohibitions
    • 1. Action Over Intent (Discriminatory Effect Matters): Under the Fair Housing Act, a violation does not require malicious intent or conscious prejudice—what matters is the discriminatory action or effect, not the person's underlying motivation.
    • 2. Causal Link to a Protected Class ("Because Of"): Housing refusals or adverse decisions violate the Fair Housing Act only when they are made because of a person's membership in a protected class. Turning down an applicant for a legitimate, consistently applied objective reason—such as rejecting an applicant with a mental illness strictly because of a verified poor credit history rather than their disability—is legally permissible.
  • Specific Examples of Fair Housing Violations
    • Housing discrimination occurs across many behaviors—ranging from overt refusals and unequal terms to subtle coding and selective responsiveness:
      Prohibited PracticeDefinition & MechanismReal Estate Example
      Unequal Service / Discriminatory TermsTreating prospects differently, withholding availability, or charging higher fees/deposits because of a protected class.• Insulting or ignoring a Latino customer while treating a white customer courteously.

      • Charging higher rents or security deposits to families with children.
      SteeringChanneling prospective buyers or tenants toward or away from specific neighborhoods or buildings based on a protected characteristic—even with "good intentions".• Showing Chinese prospects homes only in historically Chinese neighborhoods rather than across all suitable areas.
      Blockbusting (Panic Peddling)Inducing homeowners to sell at a discount by suggesting that members of a protected class are moving into the neighborhood and will cause property values to decline, then reselling at inflated prices.• Telling a white homeowner property values will tumble as Black tenants move in, buying low, and reselling to Black buyers at a higher price.
      RedliningRefusing to make mortgage loans, issue homeowners insurance, or provide financial services in specific geographic areas based on the racial or ethnic makeup of the neighborhood.• A mortgage lender denying loans in a neighborhood solely because most residents are people of color.
      Refusing Reasonable ModificationsRefusing to allow physical structural changes needed for a person with a disability to have full enjoyment of the premises.• A landlord refusing to allow or install a wheelchair ramp for a tenant with a mobility disability when it does not impose an undue burden.
      Refusing Reasonable AccommodationsRefusing to make a necessary exception to rules, policies, practices, or services so a person with a disability has an equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.• Refusing to allow a tenant with mobility impairments to keep a service or assistance animal in a building with a "no-pets" policy.
  • Real-World Enforcement Cases & Subtle / Coded Discrimination
    • Coded Steering (Long Island Investigation): An agent praised a predominantly Black and Hispanic neighborhood to a Black client as having "the nicest people," while emailing a white client about the same area warning them to "please kindly do some research on the gang-related events in that area for safety."
    • Selective Listing Packets (Atlanta Investigation): An agent brought two separate sets of property listings to an in-person meeting because he could not tell the client's race over the phone; upon seeing the client was white, he tossed one packet into the back of his car and showed homes exclusively in white neighborhoods.
    • Differential Responsiveness (Louisiana Rental Case): A landlord failed to return multiple voicemails over a week from a prospective tenant with a Latino surname, yet texted his white wife back within five minutes when she called using her own name.
    • Core Takeaway for Licensees: Discrimination does not require malicious intent—it can appear as unreturned calls, false claims that a unit is unavailable, or well-intentioned steering, and ignorance of the law is never a defense.
  • Persistence of Housing Discrimination: HUD & National Study Data
    • Complaint Volume & Breakdown (2024 HUD/FHAP & National Data):
      • While an estimated 4 million instances of housing discrimination occur annually in the U.S. rental market alone, formal complaints reported to HUD and Fair Housing Assistance Program (FHAP) agencies in 2024 broke down primarily across four protected classes:
        • Disability: 52.6% (also representing 54.59% of total nationwide complaints across HUD, FHAP, DOJ, and nonprofit fair housing organizations)
        • Race: 17%
        • Sex: 7.5%
        • Familial Status: 6.3%
    • HUD Nationwide Paired-Testing Study Findings (Compared to White Homeseekers):
      • Black homeseekers: Told about 11.4% fewer rental units and shown 4.2% fewer rental units; when buying, told about 17% fewer homes and shown 17.7% fewer homes.
      • Hispanic homeseekers: Told about 12.5% fewer rental units and shown 7.5% fewer rental units (with no statistically significant net difference in homebuying).
      • Asian homeseekers: Told about 9.8% fewer rental units and shown 6.6% fewer rental units; when buying, told about 15.5% fewer homes and shown 18.8% fewer homes.
  • All Them Acts: A Summary
  • Commercial Transactions & Property Scope
    • Non-Dwelling Buildings Excluded: The federal Fair Housing Act applies strictly to residential dwellings and does not cover purely commercial transactions or non-dwelling buildings (note: mixed-use developments ARE covered because at least a portion of the building is used as residential dwellings).
    • Professional Conduct Rule: Regardless of whether a transaction involves residential or commercial property, real estate license holders should never engage in discriminatory practices.
  • The Four Statutory Exemptions to the Fair Housing Act
    ExemptionQualifying Conditions & Statutory Rules
    1. Single-Family Residences Sold or Rented by OwnerExempts the sale or rental of a single-family home by its owner only if all four conditions are met:

    • The owner holds an interest in no more than three single-family residences at one time.

    • If the owner was not the most recent resident of the home being sold, they are allowed only one exempt sale in any 24-month period.

    • No discriminatory advertising or notices are used.

    • The owner is not a real estate broker, affiliate broker, or anyone in the business of selling or renting dwellings.
    2. Owner-Occupied 1-to-4 Family Dwellings ("Mrs. Murphy" Exemption)Named after a hypothetical elderly widow renting out rooms or units in her own home; applies federally across all states provided:

    • The property contains one to four living units and the owner actually occupies one of the units as their residence.

    • No discriminatory advertising is published or used.

    • The owner is not a real estate license holder or in the business of selling or renting dwellings.
    3. Dwellings Owned by Religious Organizations or Private Clubs• Religious Organizations: May limit the sale, rental, or occupancy of dwellings they own or operate for non-commercial residential purposes to persons of the same religion (including via restrictive covenants), provided membership in the religion is not restricted on the basis of race, color, or national origin.

    • Private Clubs: Private clubs not open to the general public may limit lodging they own or operate for non-commercial purposes to club members.
    4. Housing for Older Persons (Senior Housing)Exempts qualifying senior housing only from the familial status protection (allowing communities to exclude children under 18) if at least one of the following three criteria is met:

    1. 62+ Housing: Occupied solely (100%) by persons aged 62 or older.

    2. 55+ Housing: At least 80% of occupied units have at least one resident aged 55 or older, and the community publishes and adheres to policies/procedures demonstrating intent to operate as 55+ housing with required age verification.

    3. HUD-Designated Elderly Programs: Provided under any state or federal program determined by the Secretary of HUD to be designed and operated to assist elderly persons.
  • Critical Rules & Limitations on Fair Housing Exemptions
    • Zero Exemptions for Real Estate License Holders: None of the Fair Housing Act exemptions apply whenever a real estate broker or affiliate broker is involved in the transaction, when a party is otherwise in the business of selling or renting dwellings, or when discriminatory advertising is used. Participation by a license holder immediately voids the owner's exemption.
    • Zero Exemptions for Race or Color (Civil Rights Act of 1866): Even when a property qualifies for one of the Fair Housing Act exemptions above, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 strictly prohibits all discrimination based on race or color in any housing sale or rental—with no exceptions whatsoever.
  • Fair Lending Legislation & Lending Discrimination
    • Scope of Fair Lending Laws: Ensure that credit and mortgage lending practices do not discriminate against borrowers in either home purchases or refinances across every stage of the lending lifecycle—including marketing of mortgage products, loan applications and transactions, interest rates/terms and conditions, home appraisals, and loan servicing.
    • Examples & Warning Signs of Lending Discrimination:
      • Direct Disparate Treatment: Refusing to make mortgage loans to Black applicants, or requiring female applicants (but not male applicants) to provide a co-signer.
      • Predatory / Discriminatory Red Flags: Charging unnecessary closing costs, inflating broker or lender fees, adding unnecessary recording fees, imposing excessive prepayment penalties, or changing mortgage loan terms at closing without the borrower's consent.
  • Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 (ECOA)
    • Purpose: Federal law (implemented through Regulation B) prohibiting creditors from discriminating against credit applicants on any basis unrelated to creditworthiness.
    • ECOA Protected Classes (8 Categories):
      1. Race or color
      1. Religion
      1. National origin
      1. Sex (interpreted since 2021 to include sexual orientation and gender identity)
      1. Marital status (whether a person is married, single, in a domestic partnership, divorced, getting divorced, having a marriage annulled, separated, or widowed)
      1. Receipt of income from any public assistance program (e.g., Social Security Disability Insurance or housing assistance)
      1. Good-faith exercise of rights under the Consumer Credit Protection Act
      1. Age (provided the applicant has legal capacity to enter into a contract—meaning you can be too young to contract, but you can never be too old; an 80-year-old applicant qualifies for a 30-year mortgage on the exact same financial criteria as a younger applicant)
    • Key Distinction — Marital Status vs. Familial Status:
      • Marital status (protected under ECOA) concerns whether an applicant is married, single, divorced, separated, or widowed.
      • Familial status (protected under the Fair Housing Act) concerns households with children under age 18, pregnant individuals, or persons securing legal custody of a minor.
  • Tennessee Fair Housing Law: The Tennessee Human Rights Act (THRA)
    • Scope & Enforcement: Prohibits discrimination in employment, public accommodations, and housing across Tennessee. State-level housing discrimination claims are investigated and enforced by the Tennessee Human Rights Commission (THRC) (alongside the Civil Rights Enforcement Division of the Tennessee Attorney General's Office).
    • Additional State Protected Class — Creed:
      • In addition to all federal protected classes, Tennessee prohibits housing discrimination based on creed—defined as a system of moral or ethical beliefs and associated practices, encompassing both traditional religious beliefs and non-religious ethical belief systems.
      • Local cities and counties in Tennessee may also adopt additional municipal protected classes.
  • Tennessee Fair Housing Exemptions vs. Federal Fair Housing Act
    • Rule of Precedence: Whenever federal and state fair housing laws differ, the more restrictive law always applies (meaning narrower exemptions provide broader protection to consumers).
      Exemption RuleFederal Fair Housing Act StandardTennessee Human Rights Act Standard (More Restrictive)

Key Terms

  • Civil Rights Act of 1866 - the first federal law that began to promise equal rights for all citizens regardless of race or color; became enforceable after Jones v. Mayer (1968)
  • discrimination - the unjust or prejudicial treatment of different categories of people, especially on the grounds of race, sex, religion, disability, or other classifications
  • Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 (ECOA) - a fair lending law that aims to bar discriminatory lending practices
  • Fair Housing Act of 1968 - a federal law that prohibits discrimination in housing based upon race, color, religion, or national origin and was amended to include sex, disability, and familial status
  • familial status - under the Fair Housing Act, a protected class composed of families who have children under 18, pregnant women, and people in the process of obtaining legal custody of a child
  • marital status - refers to whether a person is married, single, divorced, or widowed; one of the Equal Credit Opportunity Act's protected classes
  • protected classes - groups of people whom it is illegal to discriminate against, as provided by the Fair Housing Act, ECOA, and other acts
