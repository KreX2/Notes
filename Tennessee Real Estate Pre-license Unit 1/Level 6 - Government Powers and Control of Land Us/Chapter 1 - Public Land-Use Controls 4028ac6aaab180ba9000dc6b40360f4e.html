Chapter 1 - Public Land-Use Controls

Public Land-Use Controls

How the Government Regulates Land

  • The Four Basic Public Land-Use Controls (PETE)
    • P – Police Power: The government's legal authority to regulate and restrict land use to protect public health, safety, morals, and general welfare (e.g., zoning, building codes).
    • E – Eminent Domain: The constitutional power of the government to seize private property for public use upon payment of just compensation.
    • T – Taxation: The government's statutory authority to levy ad valorem property taxes and special assessments against real estate to fund public operations and local improvements.
    • E – Escheat: The legal reversion of private property to state ownership when an owner dies intestate (without a valid will) and with no legal heirs.
  • Police Power
    • The fundamental authority of government entities (municipal, state, and federal) to regulate, restrict, and control private land use to protect public health, safety, morals, and general welfare.
    • Legal Basis: Serves as the foundation for state and municipal land-use regulations without requiring monetary compensation to property owners for restrictions placed on their land.
    • Four Primary Land-Use Controls Under Police Power
      • Zoning: Local municipal regulations specifying allowed property uses (residential, commercial, industrial), building heights, setbacks, and density limits within designated zones.
        • The most common application of police power; divides land within a jurisdiction into specific districts with designated permitted, prohibited, or conditional land uses.
        • Zoning Ordinances: Local municipal laws that regulate how land is used and dictate what types of structures can be built within defined districts.
        • Origin: Flow directly from the goals, policies, and priorities set forth in a municipality's master plan.
        • The Master Plan (Comprehensive Plan)
          • Definition: A long-range, written blueprint created by local planning boards to direct future growth and land development across a city, village, town, or county.
          • Core Objective: Guides sustainable community development by balancing social, economic, environmental, and aesthetic priorities.
      • Building Codes: Standards establishing mandatory health, structural, fire, plumbing, and electrical requirements for new construction, renovations, and occupancy.
        • Standardized municipal regulations governing building design, construction materials, structural integrity, fire safety, and sanitation.
        • Focus: Regulates structural soundness, safety, and health standards rather than visual aesthetics or architectural appearance.
        • Building Permits
          • Definition: Official authorization required from local municipal authorities prior to starting new construction or material property alterations.
          • Enforcement Mechanism: Allows city officials to review plans, keep track of development, and verify compliance with local zoning ordinances and building codes before work begins.
        • Certificate of Occupancy (Occupancy Permit)
        • Inspection Process: Local building inspectors conduct periodic site checks throughout the construction phase to verify code adherence.
        • Final Sign-Off: Issued upon final construction inspection to certify that the structure complies with all applicable codes and is legally fit for human habitation.
      • Subdivision Regulations: Rules governing how larger land tracts are subdivided into individual lots, ensuring proper infrastructure, street layout, utility placement, and public access.
        • Municipal rules and ordinances governing how larger tracts of land can be subdivided into smaller individual lots, establishing specific standards for lot density, layout, and zoning-adjacent development parameters.
        • Infrastructure Obligations: Authorizes local governments to require subdividers and developers to provide essential infrastructure, including paved roads, public utility connections, rights-of-way, and contributions toward public service tax requirements.
        • Subdivision Regulations vs. Deed Restrictions
          • Public vs. Private Origin:
            • Subdivision Regulations: Public land-use controls enacted as laws by municipal governments under police power.
            • Deed Restrictions: Private land-use controls created and enforced by private entities (such as developers or property owners).
          • Interplay: Developers frequently implement private deed restrictions (CC&Rs) as a legal mechanism to ensure individual lot owners continuously comply with overarching municipal subdivision regulations.
        • Subdivision Regulations vs. Deed Restrictions vs. Zoning Laws
        • Subdividers vs. Developers
          • Subdivider: The individual or entity that acquires a large tract of land and divides it into smaller, individual lots or parcels.
          • Developer: The entity that constructs improvements (roads, utilities, houses, commercial buildings) on the subdivided parcels and markets them for sale.
        • Subdivision Approval Process & Requirements
          • Planning Commission Mandates: Before granting approval, local planning commissions typically require developers or subdividers to:
            • Set aside land for public infrastructure, streets, schools, and parks/green spaces.
            • Guarantee payment and installation of sewer lines, water mains, and street paving.
            • Demonstrate compliance with local zoning ordinances, lot size rules, and fire safety codes.
          • FHA Building Standards: Subdivisions where buyers are expected to utilize FHA-insured mortgage loans must meet the Federal Housing Administration's minimum building and property standards.
          • Mandatory Pre-Sale Steps:
            1. Draw up and file an official plat map with the local municipality.
            1. Obtain all necessary municipal regulatory approvals and local planning commission sign-offs.
        • Plat Maps (Plats)
          • Definition & Contents: A detailed, scaled map showing property boundaries, individual lot lines, blocks (clusters of contiguous lots), streets, section lines, monuments, and dedicated public amenities.
          • Supplemental Information: May include engineering data, utility easements, or incorporated restrictive covenants (deed restrictions).
          • Design & Layout Flexibility: Subdividers can manipulate street layouts and utilize cluster zoning (grouping housing closer together) to preserve open spaces, parks, and natural topography.
        • Subdivision Development: Step-by-Step Process
          • 1. Initial Planning Stage:
            • Developer identifies suitable undeveloped land, orders a professional land survey, and drafts a preliminary plat map.
            • Community layout is planned on paper, detailing individual lots, roads, school sites, and park areas prior to purchasing land or starting physical work.
          • 2. City Review & Approval:
            • Preliminary subdivision plan is submitted to local municipal authorities for regulatory review.
            • City ensures alignment with the community master plan and local subdivision regulations; developer makes required modifications if necessary.
          • 3. Final Planning Stage:
            • Developer finalizes all engineering and structural details, resolves outstanding contingencies, and submits the completed final plat map.
            • Submitted for final binding approval to the designated governing body (e.g., planning commission, city council, or zoning board).
          • 4. Property Acquisition & Recording:
            • Upon receiving final municipal approval, the developer officially records the final subdivision plat in the county recorder's office.
            • City issues an official approval certificate, allowing the developer to complete the land purchase using pre-arranged financing.
          • 5. Start-Up / Disposition Stage:
            • Physical construction and site development commence.
            • Infrastructure is installed (paving streets, running gas, electric, water, and sewer lines to lot boundaries).
          • Finished, build-ready lots are marketed, sold (disposed of), and transferred to buyers or homebuilders.
      • Application of Rules: Administrative processes and procedures used by local planning boards and regulatory agencies to interpret, enforce, and manage land-use regulations or grant variances.
        • Strictest Rule Prevails
          • When a public land-use control (e.g., municipal zoning ordinance, building code) and a private restriction (e.g., deed restriction, CC&R, HOA rule) overlap or regulate the same subject matter, the stricter requirement must be followed.
          • Legality Requirement: A private restriction can only enforce a stricter standard if it remains fully compliant with all applicable federal, state, and local laws. Private covenants cannot override public statutes, permit illegal activity, or violate fair housing laws.
        • Practical Application Example
          • Municipal Zoning Law: Requires permanent structures to maintain at least a 5-foot setback from a shared property line/fence.
          • HOA CC&R Rule: Requires permanent structures to maintain a 10-foot setback from a shared property line/fence.
          • Enforceable Standard: The homeowner must abide by the 10-foot HOA setback, as it is the stricter restriction and does not violate local zoning law (satisfying the city's 5-foot minimum while upholding the HOA's higher standard).
  • Eminent Domain
    • The constitutional power of a government entity (or authorized private entity, such as a public utility company) to seize private real estate for public use.
    • Just Compensation: Mandatory legal requirement under the Fifth Amendment; the government must pay the property owner fair market value for the property being acquired.
    • Common Public Uses: Infrastructure and public welfare projects, including highways, railways, public utilities, municipal facilities, and parks.
    • The Condemnation Process
      • Initial Voluntary Negotiation: Eminent domain is a power of last resort. Government entities first attempt to negotiate a standard purchase directly with the property owner.
      • Condemnation Suit: The formal legal proceeding filed by the government to forcibly acquire title to the property if the owner refuses to sell or rejects the government's offered compensation.
      • Eminent Domain vs. Condemnation
        • Eminent Domain: The legal right or power of the government to take private property for public use.
        • Condemnation: The formal legal administrative or judicial process by which the government exercises its right of eminent domain.
      • Legal Requirements for Condemnation
        • Public Use / Benefit: The condemned property must be dedicated to a legitimate public purpose or provide a clear benefit to the public.
        • Just Compensation: The property owner must be paid fair market value for the property seized.
      • Direct vs. Inverse Condemnation
        • Direct Condemnation: The standard proceeding where the government acts as the plaintiff and sues the property owner to acquire title upon paying just compensation.
        • Inverse Condemnation: Occurs when the government appropriates, damages, or severely restricts private property without paying just compensation. The property owner must initiate a lawsuit as the plaintiff to compel the government to pay fair compensation.
      • The "Takings Clause" (Fifth Amendment)
        • Constitutional Basis: Grounded in the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution ("...nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation").
        • Legal Scope: Encompasses physical seizures as well as regulatory takings that deprive an owner of all economically beneficial use of their property; courts continuously define the specific boundaries of "public use" and "just compensation."
      • Kelo v. City of New London (2005): Case Background & Conflict
        • Context: New London, CT authorized an urban redevelopment project led by a private non-profit corporation (New London Development Corporation) to build commercial offices, housing, and facilities intended to support a proposed Pfizer pharmaceutical plant.
        • Eminent Domain Action: The city sought to acquire surrounding private land through eminent domain. While some owners sold, homeowner Suzette Kelo refused to sell her home.
        • Legal Challenge: Kelo challenged the condemnation suit, arguing that seizing non-blighted private property to turn over to private developers for private commercial use did not satisfy the "public use" mandate of the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause.
      • Supreme Court Ruling & Legal Legacy
        • Expansion of "Public Use": The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in favor of the city, establishing that economic development—creating jobs, boosting tax revenues, and revitalizing depressed municipal economies—constitutes a valid "public use" or public purpose under the Fifth Amendment.
        • Broader Scope of Eminent Domain: Significantly broadened government authority to condemn private property for transfer to third-party private entities as long as an overarching public economic benefit is cited.
        • Aftermath & Impact: The planned redevelopment project ultimately failed to materialize and Pfizer abandoned the location. The decision sparked national debate, leading many state legislatures to enact statutory limits restricting the use of eminent domain strictly for economic development.
  • Taxation
    • A statutory charge or levy assessed on real estate to generate revenue for funding public facilities and municipal services.
    • Jurisdiction & Authority: Property taxes are levied exclusively at the local level (counties, cities, local taxing districts). The U.S. Constitution explicitly prohibits the federal government from levying direct real property taxes.
    • Ad Valorem Valuation: Functionally structured as ad valorem taxes, meaning the tax amount owed is calculated directly from the assessed value of the property.
  • Escheat
    • The legal doctrine through which real property reverts to state ownership when an owner dies without a valid will and with no surviving spouse, lineal descendants, or identifiable legal heirs.
    • Property Abandonment: Escheat also applies when real estate is permanently abandoned by its legal owner with no verifiable tax payments or active ownership claims.
    • Dying Intestate
      • Definition: Dying without having executed a valid, legally binding last will and testament.
      • Impact: Prevents the decedent from designating how assets are distributed; subjects the estate to state statutory distribution rules and legal delays.
    • The Probate Process
      • Definition: The formal judicial procedure through which a legal court settles a deceased person’s estate.
      • Function: Identifies and satisfies claims from creditors and taxing authorities, validates heirship, and oversees the legal transfer of remaining assets.
      • Characteristics: Can be a costly, time-consuming, and strictly public administrative process for surviving family members.
    • Laws of Descent and Distribution
      • Definition: State statutes that dictate the exact legal succession hierarchy and proportion of property allocated to surviving heirs when an individual dies intestate.
      • Hierarchical Order: Typically prioritizes surviving spouses and direct lineal descendants (children), expanding outward to parents and siblings before escheat to the state occurs as a final resort.
  • Public Ownership of Land: Scope & Purpose
    • Essential Services & Infrastructure: Reserves land for public utility and civil operations, including public schools, municipal/government buildings, roads and transportation networks, and military bases.
    • Recreational & Environmental Preservation: Secures land for communal enjoyment and resource conservation, such as local municipal parks, state reserves, and national forests.
  • Governance & Community Representation
    • Jurisdictional Authority: Directly managed and regulated by elected officials across municipal, state, and federal government jurisdictions.
    • Civic Oversight: Designed to ensure community members have input through democratic processes into how public assets, natural resources, cultural landmarks, and civic institutions are developed, utilized, and preserved.

Key Terms

  • building code - a set of regulations pertaining to building design, materials, safety, sanitation, and structure
  • certificate of occupancy (CO) - legal document from a local authority that authorizes a building to be occupied; issued after the building has passed all required inspections
  • eminent domain - the power of government to seize private property for public use; government must pay property owner just compensation for exercising power
  • escheat - the legal doctrine by which the decedent’s property will pass to the state without their consent if that individual dies without a will, a surviving spouse, lineal descendants, or other known heirs
  • laws of descent and distribution - a set of laws that sets out the process of passing an estate to heirs when the owner dies intestate
  • master plan - a written document created by the local planning board to guide the future growth and development of a community
  • police power - the government right to regulate and restrict land use in order to protect the public; right includes the power to enforce zoning and building codes
  • probate - a process by which courts determine and grant the appropriate ownership interest of an estate to creditors, taxing entities, and heirs of an owner who dies intestate

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Police Power
    • Zoning
    • Building codes

    • Application of rules
  • Eminent Domain
    • The constitutional authority of the government to seize private property for public use.
    • Condemnation: The formal legal process through which the government exercises its right of eminent domain to acquire title.
    • Mandatory Requirements for Taking
      • Public Use / Benefit: The condemned real estate must be dedicated to a project that benefits or serves the public interest.
      • Just Compensation: The government is legally obligated under the Fifth Amendment to pay the property owner fair market value for the property seized.
    • Common Infrastructure Applications
      • Development and expansion of public roadways and highways
      • Railway networks and mass transit corridors
      • Public utility lines and municipal infrastructure facilities
  • Public Ownership of Land
    • The role of government to own and maintain public land such as streets, highways, and parks is known as public ownership of land. This is necessary in order to allow for the development of schools, government buildings, roads, military bases, public parks, and national parks.
