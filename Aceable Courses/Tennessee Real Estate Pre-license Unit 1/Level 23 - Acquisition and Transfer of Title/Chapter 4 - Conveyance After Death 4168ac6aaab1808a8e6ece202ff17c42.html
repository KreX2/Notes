Chapter 4 - Conveyance After Death

Transfer of a Deceased Person's Property & Wills Overview

  • Will (Last Will and Testament): A legal instrument made by an individual to voluntarily direct how title to their property will be conveyed after their death.
  • Amendatory Instrument: Because a will can be modified or revoked at any time while the maker is alive, it is referred to as an amendatory instrument.
  • Hereditament: Any property—whether real property or personal property—capable of being inherited and distributed via a will.
  • Testate vs. Intestate:
    • Testate: Dying with a valid will directing how property will be distributed.
    • Intestate: Dying without a valid will; the decedent's real and personal property passes to statutory heirs under state intestate succession laws (laws of descent and distribution).
  • Parties & Property Terminology in a Will
    TermRole / Property TypeDefinition & Example
    Testator (Maker / Devisor)Will creatorThe person who executes a will directing how their estate is distributed after death.
    Beneficiary / HeirRecipient under a willA person or entity named in the will to receive estate assets.
    Legacy (Bequest) & LegateePersonal propertyA gift of personal property left by will (e.g., a diamond ring) is a legacy or bequest; the person receiving it is a legatee.
    Devise & DeviseeReal propertyA gift of real property left by will is a devise; the person receiving the real property is a devisee.
  • Will vs. Deed: Key Legal Difference
    • Deed: Conveys a present ownership interest in real estate during the lifetime of the grantor.
    • Will: Conveys no ownership interest until after the death of the testator (and can be altered or revoked at any time prior to death).
  • Legal Requirements & Execution Rules for a Valid Will
    • Competent Testator: The testator must be a legal adult of sound mind at the time the will is executed and signed.
    • Voluntary Act: Must be drawn and signed freely without duress, fraud, or undue influence from others.
    • Lawful Execution: Must be prepared and executed in compliance with the laws of the state where the real estate is located.
    • Titled: Must be formally entitled "Last Will and Testament."
    • Disinterested Witnesses: Witnesses to the signing of the will should be disinterested parties—meaning they are not named as devisees or legatees in the will—to avoid conflicts of interest or invalidation of gifts.
    • Codicils (Amendments): Formal written modifications or additions made to an existing will after its initial execution (must be executed with the same legal formalities as the original will).
    • Passage of Title & Probate: Legally, a decedent's title to real estate passes immediately upon death either to the devisees named in the will or to statutory heirs by descent; however, probate is required to formally validate the will, settle creditor claims, clear title for future buyers, or resolve disputes among devisees.
  • The Three Types of Wills
    Type of WillFormat & Execution RequirementsRecognition & Tennessee Rules
    Formal (Witnessed) WillTyped out (or on a preprinted form) and signed by the testator plus at least two witnesses.Recognized by all states and courts; offers the strongest legal protection against challenges.
    Holographic WillEntirely handwritten, dated, and signed solely by the testator without witnesses (cannot be typed).Recognized in Tennessee (must be proved by witnesses familiar with the testator's handwriting), though not recommended as a first choice because it is more vulnerable to fraud and heir challenges.
    Oral Will (Nuncupative / Deathbed Will)Spoken verbally by a person directly before their death (in imminent peril of death).Recognized by only some states due to high risk of error and fraud; Tennessee DOES recognize nuncupative wills (subject to strict statutory limits, such as two disinterested witnesses and caps on personal property value).
  • Probate Proceedings (Surrogate Court)
    • Definition: The formal court-supervised legal process used to determine the validity of a will, inventory and establish the decedent's assets, settle debts, and determine who legally inherits the estate.
    • Alternative Name: Probate court is also known as surrogate court.
    • When Probate May Be Unnecessary: Probate is not always required, particularly in straightforward estates, when assets pass automatically outside of probate (such as joint tenancy with right of survivorship, tenancy by the entirety, or payable-on-death accounts), or when the estate qualifies under small-estate procedures.
  • How Probate Works: Initiation, Outcomes & Creditor Priority
    • 1. Starting Probate:
      • The custodian of the will, an heir, or another interested party files a petition presenting the will/issue to the probate court.
      • The court holds a hearing to verify the will's validity (or establish the statutory order of descent if no valid will exists) and hears any statutory life estate or spousal claims—including dower/curtesy rights, elective share, and homestead law protections.
    • 2. Probate Outcomes:
      • If the will is upheld: Estate assets are distributed in accordance with the terms of the will.
      • If the will is declared invalid (or too much time has passed since death): The decedent is treated as having died intestate, and all property passes according to state laws of descent and distribution.
    • 3. Mandatory Creditor Priority Rule:
      • Regardless of whether the will is ruled valid or invalid, all valid creditors and lienors must be paid off first from estate assets before any remaining property or funds are distributed to heirs or devisees.
  • The Probate People (Estate Administration Roles)
    Role / PartyHow Appointed & Primary Responsibilities
    Executor (Executrix — antiquated female term / Personal Representative)Named directly in the decedent's will to carry out its instructions, inventory estate assets, settle valid debts, and disburse remaining property to beneficiaries.
    Administrator (Personal Representative)Appointed by the probate court when a decedent dies intestate (without a will) or when no executor is named/able to serve; oversees estate administration and statutory distribution.
    Lienors and CreditorsParties holding valid liens or unpaid debts against the decedent; regardless of whether the decedent died testate or intestate, all legitimate creditor and lienor claims must be settled first—according to lien priority rules—before any property is distributed to heirs or devisees.
  • Intestate Probate Proceedings (Dying Without a Will)
    • General Rule: When a person dies intestate, title to their real estate and personal property passes by operation of law to their legal heirs in accordance with state laws of descent and distribution—only after all valid lienors and creditors are paid off.
    • Tennessee Rules of Descent and Distribution:
      1. Surviving Spouse and Children: The estate is divided among the surviving spouse and children, with the surviving spouse guaranteed at least one-third (1/3) of the estate (or a child's share, whichever is greater).
      1. No Known Heirs: If no legal heirs exist, all real and personal property reverts to the state (whereas in some other states, property reverts to the county).
      1. Custodial State Policy: Tennessee is a custodial state, meaning the state makes every reasonable effort to locate any heirs or distant relatives of the deceased before exercising its power of escheat.
  • Avoiding Probate via Survivorship & Escheat as a Last Resort
    • Joint Tenancy & Survivorship in Tennessee:
      • In Tennessee, joint tenancy with right of survivorship (alongside tenancy by the entirety) is available for married couples, allowing a surviving spouse to automatically inherit full ownership of the property outside of probate—even if the deceased spouse died intestate.
    • Escheat (Last Resort):
      • If no creditor claims consume the property and absolutely no living relatives or heirs can be found after exhaustive search efforts, the property escheats (reverts) to the state as a final resort so land never becomes ownerless.

Key Terms

  • intestate - the name for conditions in which the owner of a property dies without having left a valid will
  • testate - the name for conditions in which the owner of a property dies having left a valid will
  • testator - the party who makes a will
  • will - a written or verbal legal statement by an owner (testator) that prescribes the method of distribution of the owner's estate to heirs

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Intestate vs. Testate
    • Intestate is the name for conditions in which the owner of a property dies without having left a valid will.
    • Testate is the name for conditions in which the owner of a property dies having left a valid will.
  • Kinds of Wills
    • Formal will: created by a lawyer, signed by two witnesses (recognized everywhere)
    • Holographic will: created by the testator, handwritten, dated, and signed, not witnessed
    • Nuncupative (oral) will: created verbally by the testator, told to a witness before dying
  • Executor and Administrator
    • An executor is a person identified in a will to carry out its provisions, including the disbursement of property.
    • If no executor was named in the will, the court appoints an administrator – also known as a personal representative – to oversee the administration and distribution of the estate.
