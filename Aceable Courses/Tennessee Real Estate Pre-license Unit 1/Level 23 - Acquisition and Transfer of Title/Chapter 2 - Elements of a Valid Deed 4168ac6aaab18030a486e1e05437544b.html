Chapter 2 - Elements of a Valid Deed

Requirements for a Valid Deed

  • Statute of Frauds Requirement: Every deed transferring an interest in real property must be in writing.
  • Six Essential Elements Required for a Valid Deed:
    1. Competent and willing (intentional) grantor
    1. Name of a legitimate and living grantee
    1. Granting clause (also called words of conveyance)
    1. Legal description of the property being conveyed
    1. Acknowledgment of the grantor's signature (signature witnessed/notarized)
    1. Delivery and acceptance (title passes only upon actual delivery by the grantor and acceptance by the grantee)
  • Common Non-Mandatory Clauses (Recommended for Clarity):
    • Statement of consideration (e.g., monetary amount or "for love and affection")
    • Habendum clause (defines the extent of ownership/estate being conveyed, typically beginning with "to have and to hold")
    • Limitations, exceptions, and reservations (also called "reddendum" or "subject to" clauses, such as deed restrictions or retained mineral/easement rights)
    • Tenendum clause (historically identifies accompanying rights and appurtenances passing with the land)

  • Legal Qualifications of the Grantor and Grantee
    • Grantor Requirements:
      • Must be at least 18 years old (the age of legal majority)—or under 18 only if married, legally emancipated, or represented by a court-appointed guardian.
      • Must be legally competent (of sound mind) and acting willingly without duress or fraud.
    • Grantee Requirements:
      1. Clearly identified by name on the deed (cannot be a fictitious person or ambiguous group).
      1. Living at the time of conveyance (a deed to a deceased person is void; if conveying to a business or trust, it must be a legally existing entity).
  • Granting Clause (Words of Conveyance)
    • Essential Status: The one mandatory ("must-have") clause required for every valid deed; it formally expresses the grantor's intent to transfer ownership and identifies the specific type of deed being used.
    • Defining the Estate Conveyed: Specifies the exact degree of ownership interest being transferred:
      • Fee Simple Estate: Uses wording such as "to Jacqueline Smith and to her heirs and assigns forever."
      • Life Estate (Less Than Complete Interest): Must explicitly state the limitation, such as "to Jacqueline Smith for the duration of her natural life."
    • Multiple Grantees: When two or more grantees take title together, the granting clause must intentionally specify their co-ownership structure (such as joint tenancy with right of survivorship or tenancy by the entirety); otherwise, state default rules typically create a tenancy in common.
  • Consideration & Gift Deeds
    • Definition: Something of value exchanged by the parties as evidence of mutual agreement to the conveyance—which may consist of money (most common), promises, goods, or services.
    • Gift Deed: When real property is transferred as a gift without monetary payment, consideration may be stated as "for love and affection."
    • When Full, Exact Price Must Be Stated: Most standard deeds do not need to disclose the exact purchase price (often using nominal language like "for ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration"). Disclosing the full, specific consideration is required only when the deed is executed by:
      1. A referee (conducting a foreclosure or other court-ordered public sale)
      1. A fiduciary (such as an executor, trustee, or attorney-in-fact)
  • Legal Description
    • Validity Requirement: Every valid deed must contain a legal description precise enough to uniquely identify the parcel and distinguish it from all other properties so a surveyor can locate the land and accurately mark its boundaries.
    • Street Address Warning: A mailing or street address (e.g., "123 Main Street") is not legally sufficient for a deed.

  • Deed Restrictions (Restrictive Covenants)
    • Definition: Non-governmental, private limitations placed on land use by private parties—most commonly property developers or current/prior property owners.
    • Five Validity Requirements: To be legally valid and enforceable, deed restrictions must:
      1. Be non-discriminatory (cannot violate Fair Housing laws or public policy)
      1. Promote the general welfare of the public
      1. Be consistent (applied uniformly rather than arbitrarily)
      1. Be exercised reasonably
      1. Be clear and specific
    • Common Subjects Regulated by Developer Restrictions:
      • Type of building (e.g., single-family residential structures only)
      • Permitted land use (e.g., prohibiting commercial or industrial activity)
      • Architectural & dimensional standards: Type of construction, building height, boundary setbacks, and minimum/maximum square footage
      • Minimum construction cost (to maintain neighborhood quality and property values)
    • Binding on Subsequent Purchasers ("Run with the Land"):
      • Restrictive covenants generally run with the land, meaning they attach to the property itself rather than the individual owner and remain legally binding on all future buyers when the property is resold.
  • Reservations and Exceptions ("Subject To" Clauses)
    • Definition: Clauses in a deed—commonly known as "subject to" clauses—that identify specific rights retained by the grantor or pre-existing encumbrances limiting what is conveyed to the grantee.
    • Common Examples:
      • Pre-existing mortgage liens, property taxes, deed restrictions, mineral rights/leases, and easements that run with the land
      • The grantor reserving a specific right to use part of the land (e.g., reserving an easement across the parcel or retaining mineral rights)
      • The grantor imposing specific use restrictions on the grantee
      • Density limits, such as restrictions on the total number of homes permitted in a subdivision
  • Signature of Grantor & Attorney-in-Fact
    • All Named Grantors Must Sign: Every grantor named in the deed must sign it for the conveyance to be valid—if a grantor's spouse is named as a grantor (or holds marital/homestead rights requiring release), the spouse must also sign. (Note: The grantee does not need to sign the deed.)
    • Attorney-in-Fact: A grantor's signature may be executed on their behalf by an attorney-in-fact acting under a valid power of attorney (such as a general power of attorney granting authority to handle the principal's business and property dealings).
  • Acknowledgment
    • Definition: A formal declaration made by the person signing the deed confirming that they are signing voluntarily (by their own free choice) and that the signature is genuine.
    • Authorized Officials: Takes place in the presence of a notary public or another authorized public officer, such as a judge or justice of the peace.
    • Recording Prerequisite: While a signed, delivered deed transfers title between the parties, acknowledgment is required before the deed can be recorded in the public land records.
  • Delivery and Acceptance of a Deed
    • Core Legal Rule: Simply signing and acknowledging a valid deed does not transfer ownership. Title passes only when both delivery (by the grantor with the clear intention of transferring title) and acceptance (by the grantee receiving the property) have occurred.
  • How Delivery Works
    • Authorized Methods of Delivery: The grantor may deliver the deed personally to the grantee or to an authorized representative acting on the grantee's behalf, including:
      • The grantee's title company
      • The grantee's attorney
      • The grantee's real estate agent
    • Post-Closing Mail Delivery: It is also standard practice for the deed to be mailed to the grantee along with the closing documents shortly after closing day.
  • Effect of Acceptance
    • Moment Title Passes: Once the grantee accepts the delivered deed, legal title immediately passes from the grantor to the grantee.
    • Single-Use Purpose: Upon delivery and acceptance, the deed has fulfilled its legal purpose as an instrument of conveyance and cannot be reused to transfer the property again.
  • Lost or Returned Deeds
    • Effect of Losing a Deed: If the grantee loses or destroys the physical deed after delivery and acceptance, there is zero effect on the grantee's ownership or title to the property. The grantor cannot reclaim the estate simply because the physical paper was lost.
    • Returning Property Requires a New Deed: Similarly, a grantee cannot undo a transfer or return ownership simply by handing or mailing the original deed back to the grantor—to transfer title back, the grantee must execute a brand-new deed conveying the property back to the original owner.
  • Recording the Deed
    • Definition: The formal act of filing and documenting any transfer or claim of interest in real property title in the public land records in accordance with state law.
    • When to Record: A deed should be recorded only after it satisfies all legal requirements for validity (including proper execution and notarized acknowledgment).
    • Tennessee Recording Office: In Tennessee, deeds are recorded at the county clerk's office (specifically the Register of Deeds / county recording office) in the county where the property is physically located.
  • Constructive Notice vs. Actual Notice
    Type of NoticeDefinitionHow It Is Established
    Constructive NoticeLegal presumption that the public has knowledge of a property's ownership and recorded claims—even if a person has not actually inspected the records.Established by recording the deed in the public records (and/or by visible physical possession of the property).
    Actual NoticeDirect, first-hand, personal knowledge of a property's ownership or an existing claim.Obtained directly (e.g., being told in person or seeing an unrecorded document), even if the information is not in the public record.
  • Benefits of Recording a Deed
    • While recording is not required to make a deed legally valid between the grantor and grantee, recording protects the grantee in four key ways:
      1. Provides Constructive Notice: Alerts the general public and future buyers/lenders of the grantee's legal ownership.
      1. Creates an Indestructible Public Record: If the original physical deed is ever lost, stolen, or destroyed, an official certified copy can be retrieved from the county clerk's office.
      1. Establishes Priority of Ownership Claims: Protects the grantee's legal priority over competing or subsequent claims in the event of a title dispute.
      1. Preserves Future Marketability: Ensures a clear chain of title so the new owner will not face title defects or delays when selling or mortgaging the property later.
  • Unrecorded Deeds & Tennessee Rules
    • Validity Without Recording: Tennessee law does not require a deed to be recorded for the transfer to be valid between the original grantor and grantee.
    • Risk of Invalidation: Leaving a deed unrecorded exposes the buyer to serious risk—under Tennessee recording statutes, an unrecorded deed can be invalidated by a later-recorded deed to a subsequent good-faith purchaser for value without notice.
    • Escrow & Closing Standard: Because of this risk, almost all title and escrow companies require the deed to be recorded as a mandatory step to close a real estate transaction.

Key Terms

  • habendum clause - a clause within a deed that clarifies the type and extent of interest conveyed by the granting clause
  • recording - the act of documenting any transfer or claim of interest in the title to real property in public records per state law
