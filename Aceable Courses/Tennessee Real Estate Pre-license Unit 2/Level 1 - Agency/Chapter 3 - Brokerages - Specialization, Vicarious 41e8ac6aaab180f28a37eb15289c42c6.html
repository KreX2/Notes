Chapter 3 - Brokerages - Specialization, Vicarious Liability, and Imputed Knowledge

Brokerage Structure & Personnel in Tennessee

  • Brokerage Defined: A for-profit business entity formed specifically to broker real estate transactions—including the sale, purchase, lease, exchange, or management of real property—between sellers and buyers or landlords and tenants.
  • Members of a Tennessee Brokerage (T.C.A. § 62-13-309 / § 62-13-310):
    • Principal Broker: The designated license holder responsible for supervising all real estate brokerage activities, compliance, trust accounts, and affiliated licensees within the firm.
    • Affiliate Brokers: Licensed agents sponsored by the principal broker to conduct brokerage activities on the firm's behalf; a brokerage may sponsor any number of affiliate brokers.
    • Unlicensed Assistants: Administrative staff who support the principal broker and licensees with clerical, marketing, or office tasks without performing activities that require a real estate license.
  • Client vs. Customer Relationships & Permitted Agency Models
    • Working FOR vs. Working WITH:
      • Agency Relationship (Client / Principal): When a brokerage represents or works for a principal under a written bilateral agency agreement (T.C.A. § 62-13-401), that principal is a client owed statutory and fiduciary duties (T.C.A. § 62-13-404).
      • Non-Agency Relationship (Customer): When a brokerage works with a party without representing them, that party is a customer owed baseline consumer duties (T.C.A. § 62-13-403) rather than fiduciary advocacy.
    • Five Representation Models Permitted in Tennessee (T.C.A. § 62-13-401 et seq.):
      1. Single agency (representing only one side of a transaction)
      1. Subagency (acting as an agent of a primary listing broker)
      1. Dual agency (representing both sides in the same in-house transaction with written informed consent)
      1. Designated agency (appointing separate licensees in the same firm to represent opposing sides exclusively)
      1. Facilitator / Transaction brokerage (non-agency assistance to one or both parties without advocating for either side)
  • Strategic Benefits of Brokerage Specialization
    • Even though Tennessee law permits multiple forms of agency, a principal broker may choose to specialize by restricting the firm's services to specific representation models or client types to achieve three business advantages:
      1. Differentiate Through Marketing: Stand out from full-service competitors with a focused brand message tailored to a specific niche or exclusive representation promise.
      1. Leverage Team Talent: Capitalize on the specialized expertise, background, and strengths of the firm's affiliated licensees.
      1. Mitigate Risk: Reduce liability exposure by allowing newer or less experienced licensees to build mastery within a narrower, more predictable set of transactions and client types.
  • Single-Agency Brokerage Specialization Models
    • A brokerage operating exclusively under single agency avoids dual agency entirely by never representing both sides of the same transaction, and can niche its business model across three tiers:

  • In-House Representation Options in a Single Agency+ Brokerage
    • Brokerages that operate primarily under single agency while accommodating in-house transactions (where both parties work with the same firm) can structure representation in Tennessee through three models (T.C.A. § 62-13-401 et seq.):
      1. Dual Agency: Both parties are represented by the same principal broker (and potentially the same licensee), resulting in legally limited duties—specifically restricted loyalty and disclosure—because the dual agent cannot favor either party or disclose confidential negotiating terms.
      1. Designated Agency (T.C.A. § 62-13-406): Both parties have agency agreements with the same principal broker, but the managing/principal broker appoints separate affiliate brokers to represent each party individually. Designated agents do not share confidential client information with anyone else in the brokerage—including the principal broker—allowing full fiduciary and statutory client duties to be performed for each side.
      1. Non-Agency / Facilitator (Transaction Brokerage): Absent any agency representation, a facilitator works neutrally with both parties to complete the transaction, providing baseline consumer services (T.C.A. § 62-13-403) rather than fiduciary advocacy.

  • Vicarious Liability: Principal Broker vs. Principal (Client)
    • Definition: Vicarious liability is a common-law doctrine holding that one party is legally responsible for the acts, omissions, or misrepresentations of another person acting with authority on their behalf.
    • 1. The Principal Broker's Vicarious Liability:
      • Broad, Ongoing Exposure: Because affiliate brokers act as general agents of their sponsoring principal broker with ongoing authority to conduct brokerage business and fulfill fiduciary duties, the principal broker carries continuous vicarious liability for the actions of all sponsored affiliate brokers (as well as unlicensed assistants) (T.C.A. § 62-13-312, § 62-13-406(b), § 62-13-407).
      • Risk Mitigation: Principal brokers mitigate this exposure through comprehensive written company policy manuals, ongoing training, and active supervision.
    • 2. The Principal's (Client's) Vicarious Liability:
      • Narrower Exposure: Because a buyer or seller client grants the brokerage only special agency authority limited to a single specific transaction, the principal's vicarious liability is proportionally narrower.
      • Tennessee Statutory Limit (T.C.A. § 62-13-407): Under Tennessee statute, a client or party is not liable for damages caused by a licensee's misrepresentations unless the client directly authorized the statement/action or knew about the misrepresentation and failed to correct it.
    • Foundation of Trust: Appointing an agent or sponsoring an affiliate broker requires placing legal trust in that party to act ethically, competently, and within their scope of authority to minimize liability exposure for everyone involved.
  • Vicarious Liability with Cooperating Brokers (Subagency Risk)
    • How Cooperating Subagency Creates Vicarious Liability: When a listing principal broker enters into a subagency agreement with an unaffiliated cooperating broker, the listing principal broker authorizes that outside licensee to act on their behalf—and therefore assumes vicarious liability for the cooperating subagent's actions within the scope of the transaction.
    • Scenario Application (Newbie Realty & Cusco Realty):
      • Leanna (Principal Broker, Newbie Realty) lists Audrey's bungalow on the MLS and authorizes cooperating brokers to act as subagents.
      • Sarah (Cooperating Broker, Cusco Realty) accepts the subagency role to bring a buyer to Audrey's property.
      • Legal Consequence: Even though Leanna does not employ, sponsor, or directly supervise Sarah, Leanna authorized Sarah to act as her subagent—making Leanna vicariously liable for any misrepresentations, errors, or misconduct Sarah commits while representing Audrey's interests.
    • Why Many Principal Brokers Prohibit Subagency: While a principal broker already carries continuous vicarious liability for their own sponsored affiliate brokers, assuming vicarious liability for an outside cooperating broker over whom they have zero supervisory control poses an unacceptable legal risk—leading most principal brokers to decline offering subagency on MLS listings.
  • Three Types of Legal Knowledge in Real Estate
    Type of KnowledgeDefinitionReal Estate Example & Legal Effect
    Actual KnowledgeDirect, personal awareness of a fact or reality (either you know it or you do not).Knowing for certain whether you own a dog (versus not knowing whether you carry a hidden recessive gene for dimples).
    Constructive KnowledgeKnowledge the law deems a person to have because it could be discovered through reasonable inquiry or inspection, regardless of whether they actually checked.Recorded liens, easements, or deed restrictions filed in public county land records—a buyer is legally charged with knowing them even if they never searched the records.
    Imputed KnowledgeKnowledge legally attributed to a principal simply because an agent acting on the principal's behalf possesses that knowledge.If a listing agent learns of planned commercial rezoning or construction next door, the seller may be legally deemed to have that knowledge for property disclosure obligations even if the agent never told the seller.
  • How Tennessee Designated Agency Eliminates Imputed Knowledge (T.C.A. § 62-13-406)
    • One-Person Brokerage Firewall: In a Tennessee designated agency arrangement, each appointed designated agent operates as an independent one-person brokerage for their assigned client, treating all other licensees in the firm—including the principal/managing broker—as if they represent the opposing party.
    • Statutory Bar on Imputation (T.C.A. § 62-13-406(c)): Tennessee law explicitly mandates that there shall be no imputation of knowledge or information among or between clients, the managing broker, and any designated agent or agents in a designated agency transaction.
    • Principal Broker Protection: Because confidential client knowledge is not imputed from the designated agents to the managing broker, the managing broker is not considered a dual agent (T.C.A. § 62-13-406(a)), even though both designated agents are sponsored by that same managing broker.
  • Quick Core Distinction
    • Vicarious Liability: Legal responsibility for the actions of someone authorized to act on your behalf.
    • Imputed Knowledge: Legal assumption of—and responsibility for—the knowledge held by someone authorized to act on your behalf.

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Brokerage Specialization
    • A brokerage can choose to specialize in a number of ways, including by whom they represent and by what kind of agency relationships they offer:
    • Represent sellers, landlords, buyers, and tenants (but not both sides of the same transaction)
    • Represent sellers only, landlords only, buyers only, or renters only
    • Represent sellers and landlords only or buyers and renters only
    • Represent all kinds of clients in single agency or designated agency but not dual agency
    • Represent all kinds of clients in all permitted forms of agency
  • Vicarious Liability
    • Vicarious liability is a common law concept. It holds that a person is liable for the actions of those acting with authority on their behalf.
    • Through authorization they grant through general agency, principal brokers are vicariously liable for their affiliate brokers and any unaffiliated subagents they allow to act on their behalf.
    • Through authorization they grant through special agency, principals are vicariously liable for the agents (licensees) they allow to act on their behalf.
  • Imputed Knowledge
    • With imputed knowledge, a person is deemed to have awareness of a fact which they do not themselves have but which a person acting as their agent does have.
  • Designated Agents
    • There is NO legal assumption of imputed knowledge between the principal broker and either of the designated agents.
