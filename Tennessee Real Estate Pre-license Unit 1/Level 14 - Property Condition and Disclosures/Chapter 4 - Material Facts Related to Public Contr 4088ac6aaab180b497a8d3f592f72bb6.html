Chapter 4 - Material Facts Related to Public Controls

Public Controls

  • Definition: Public controls encompass legal restrictions, ordinances, and governmental regulations imposed by municipal or state authorities that dictate how land and property can be used.
  • Key Categories: Public controls heavily involve zoning and planning laws, municipal boundaries (such as school districts, utility districts, and taxation zones), and the availability or scope of municipal services.
  • Why Public Controls Matter in Real Estate
    • Buyers must understand these legal boundaries and restrictions before purchasing because they directly impact property utility, tax liabilities, schooling options, and future development rights.
    • In upcoming sections, we will break down the specific disclosures required under each of these municipal and zoning categories.
  • Zoning and Planning Purpose
    • Definition: Government ordinances that divide municipalities into distinct zones to regulate and restrict the location, height, bulk, and use of buildings, structures, and land.
    • Objective: To protect specific districts (residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, recreational, and special purpose) from the harmful encroachment of incompatible uses (e.g., preventing a heavy industrial factory or giant retail center from being built in the middle of a quiet single-family subdivision).
  • The Alexander Family Scenario: Zoning as a Material Fact
    • The Dilemma: The Alexanders love a home specifically because of the serene, undeveloped field behind it. However, you (the informed agent) know that the land is zoned commercial and slated for strip mall construction next year.
    • The Material Fact Test: A material fact is anything that, if known, would likely cause a buyer to change their course of action or decision. Because losing the open field and gaining a commercial strip mall would undoubtedly impact the Alexanders' purchasing decision, this zoning status and future development plan constitute a critical material fact.
    • Agent Duty: Licensees have an affirmative duty to disclose known zoning and planning facts that affect property enjoyment, value, or usability.
  • Pro Tip for Licensees
    • Official zoning classifications, maps, and future planning data can be verified and retrieved by inquiring directly with the city or municipal planning department (or checking public records).
  • The Game of Zones Scenario
    • The Dilemma: Sarah wants to move away from a football stadium to escape noise, but you (the agent) know from widespread public news that the stadium is scheduled to be demolished and rebuilt right next to the new neighborhood she has chosen.
    • Material Fact & Public Knowledge: Even though zoning maps currently show the area's designation, widely publicized future development plans (like relocating a major stadium) can constitute a material fact or critical factor that would influence the buyer's decision.
    • Agent Duty: Agents must share known public developments affecting property enjoyment, help buyers obtain local master plans, and encourage independent research into future municipal changes.
  • Nonconforming Uses
    • Definition: A nonconforming use occurs when a property use was entirely legal when established, but a subsequent change in zoning ordinances rendered it illegal (often grandfathered in under specific conditions).
    • Disclosure Rule: Sellers are legally required to disclose nonconforming uses as part of their material fact duties.
  • Municipal Boundaries & Public Controls
    • Public controls also encompass boundaries governing taxation districts, school districts, voting/legislative districts, public utilities, and municipal services.
    1. Taxation Districts
      • Source Attribution: Property tax data and special assessments should always be sourced directly from the county tax assessor's office to protect against liability.
      • Seller Duty: Sellers are generally not required to disclose general local tax rates, but they must disclose any outstanding municipal tax assessments or liens/fees tied to the property.
    1. School Districts
      • Seller Duty: Sellers are typically exempt from disclosing school district details.
      • The Tax Statement: The property tax statement will list the official school district code.
      • The Golden Rule on School Opinions: Never give personal opinions on school quality. Because school rankings and "good vs. bad" designations are subjective, agents exposing opinions take on unnecessary professional risk.
      • Standard Agent Response Script: When asked if a home is in a "good school district," agents should deflect professionally: "Real estate agents are not qualified to assess schools or school districts. Luckily though, there are plenty of qualified resources out there that do. I would definitely encourage you to reach out to the school district and do some research online!"
    1. Voting (Legislative) Districts
      • Clients can verify voting and legislative district assignments through official state government websites, keeping in mind that district lines are subject to periodic political redistricting.
  • Public Utilities Overview
    • Core Availability: Buyers must be fully informed about the availability, source, and reliability of all essential public utilities—including water, wastewater (sewer/septic), electricity, trash removal, and recycling.
    • Material Fact Status: The precise source and status of a property's utility services constitute a critical material fact that agents must help clients research and verify.
    • Water Services: Municipal vs. Private Supplies
      • Municipal Water: Most urban and suburban homes receive water from a community public water system. Public suppliers must provide customers with an annual Consumer Confidence Report (typically delivered with July water bills) disclosing detected contaminants, potential health effects, and the source of the water.
      • Private / Well Water: Rural or remote properties often rely on private or community wells.
        • Owner Responsibility: According to the EPA, private well owners bear sole responsibility for ensuring their drinking water is safe.
        • Testing: Routine testing for common contaminants is strongly recommended to safeguard health and establish a historical record of water quality for future liability or compensation claims.
    • Sanitary Waste & Wastewater Systems
      • Municipal Sewer Systems: Wastewater is channeled from household plumbing into public collection systems leading to municipal treatment plants. Public sewers are always preferred where available.
      • On-Site Wastewater Treatment (Septic Systems): Used in areas lacking municipal sewer access to store, distribute, and treat domestic wastewater safely without contaminating groundwater or surface water.
      • Septic System Goals & Regulations: Governed strictly by the Department of Health. Must be designed by professional engineers or registered architects, with specifications filed at the local health department.
      • Perc Tests for Vacant Land: When a buyer purchases a vacant lot intending to build a home with a septic system, they must conduct a soil and percolation ("perc" test) to verify that the ground can successfully and safely absorb and filter wastewater.
  • Specially Regulated Areas
    • Definition: Properties located within government-protected zones—such as wetlands, endangered species habitats, or wildlife preserves—carry strict land-use restrictions and require mandatory disclosure.
    • The Wetland Reality: Wetlands do not always look like traditional swamps or bogs; they can appear completely dry, making visual identification deceptive.
    • Licensee Best Practice: Real estate agents operating in markets with protected zones should educate themselves on local boundaries and direct clients to environmental specialists when regulatory questions arise.
  • Hazardous and Uninsurable Areas
    • Definition: Locations prone to severe natural disasters (such as flood zones or wildfire-prone regions) or unique environmental threats.
    • Uninsurability as a Material Fact: Some hazards are so severe that properties become ninsurable or prohibitively expensive to cover. Because hazard insurance is mandatory for nearly all mortgaged properties, an area's uninsurable status is a critical material fact that buyers must know before committing.
  • Airspace and Military Facilities
    • Sellers are legally required to disclose if a property is located in the vicinity of:
      • Public, private, or military airports
      • Ancillary military facilities, low-level training routes, or restricted airspace
    • Reason for Disclosure: While proximity to these facilities is not inherently a physical hazard, the chronic noise pollution and flight activity can drastically impact a buyer's enjoyment of the property.
  • Flood Zones & FEMA Guidance
    • FEMA Flood Maps: If a prospective buyer inquires whether a property sits within a flood zone, agents should direct them to check up-to-date flood maps available online through FEMA.
    • Flood Insurance Realities: Suggest that buyers in flood zones look into flood insurance and the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). While the ultimate decision rests with the buyer, mortgage lenders will almost universally require it as a loan condition.
  • Disclosures That Are NOT Required (Stigmatized Properties)
    • Definition: Stigmatized properties are homes that suffer from suppressed market value due to psychological or emotional factors entirely unrelated to the physical condition, safety, or structural features of the property.
    • Examples: Locations where a tragic event occurred, such as a natural death, murder, suicide, or rumored paranormal activity/hauntings.
    • Tennessee Stigmatized Property Law: Under Tennessee law, license holders are not obligated to disclose the psychological stigma of a property, as long as it has no material bearing on the physical condition of the property.
    • The Physical Exception: If a death or injury was caused directly by a physical defect or serious safety hazard on the property (e.g., carbon monoxide poisoning from a faulty furnace), that physical defect is a material fact and must be disclosed.
  • The Golden Rule on Stigmatized Properties: Never Lie
    • No Duty to Voluntarily Disclose vs. Absolute Duty to Tell the Truth: While agents have no legal duty to volunteer information about a property's stigma unprompted, they are strictly prohibited from lying or misrepresenting facts.
    • Handling Direct Inquiries: If a buyer specifically asks whether a stigmatized event occurred, the licensee has two choices: answer truthfully or decline to answer (never deceive, fabricate, or make intentional misrepresentations).
  • Information That CANNOT Be Disclosed (Non-Material & Protected Classes)
    • HIV/AIDS & Infectious Diseases:
      • Real estate licensees are strictly prohibited from disclosing whether a previous occupant suffered from any infectious disease, including HIV/AIDS.
      • Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and the Federal Fair Housing Act, persons with disabilities (including HIV/AIDS) are protected. The law explicitly deems this non-material because it has no bearing on the physical condition or safety of the property.
    • Protected Classes Under Federal Fair Housing Act:
      • Agents are legally barred from disclosing personal information regarding a previous occupant's protected class status: race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, or disability.
      • Prohibited disclosure examples include answering questions about previous occupants' religion, race/nationality, sex, family status (children), or specific disabilities.
      • Standard Agent Response Script for Protected Inquiries: When faced with forbidden questions from buyers, agents should respond professionally: "Legally, I am not allowed to answer that question." (Clients almost always understand when framed around clear legal obligations).
  • Megan's Law & Sex Offender Registries
    • Definition & Mandate: Megan’s Law (enacted federally in 1994) allows states to make registered sex offender information accessible to the public.
    • Licensee Liability: Real estate licensees are not legally required to investigate, track, or voluntarily disclose the presence of registered sex offenders near a property, and agents bear no legal liability for failing to do so.
    • Handling Client Inquiries: If a buyer specifically asks whether sex offenders live nearby, agents should step outside the transaction scope and direct the client to local law enforcement or official state registry databases to perform their own research.

Key Term

  • stigmatized property - property with suppressed market value unrelated to its physical condition or features; frequently as result of death of an occupant, murder, suicide, or suspected paranormal activity

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Public Controls
    • Public controls generally refer to restrictions or other controls imposed by law. Some examples include:
      • Zoning and planning
      • Municipal boundaries of school, utility, and taxation districts
      • Municipal services
    • Make sure details (including potential changes) related to the above are disclosed.
  • Location Issues
    • A property’s location within a specially regulated area requires disclosure. These areas can include government-protected wetlands or endangered species or wildlife habitats.
    • An area can be deemed hazardous (and even uninsurable) if it's prone to natural disasters or other threats to safety and property.
    • Lastly, buyers have a right to know if a home is in close proximity to an airport, landfill, or any other facility that could interfere with their enjoyment of the property.
  • Stigmatized Properties
    • License holders are not obligated to disclose the psychological stigma of a property so long as it has no material effect on the condition of the property. The death of an occupant is only considered a material fact if the condition of the property caused the death.
  • Megan's Law
    • Authorities must make information available to the public regarding registered sex offenders.
    • As a licensee, you are NOT required by law to disclose or investigate the presence of sex offenders. You will not be held liable for failing to disclose info on the Sex Offenders Registry.
