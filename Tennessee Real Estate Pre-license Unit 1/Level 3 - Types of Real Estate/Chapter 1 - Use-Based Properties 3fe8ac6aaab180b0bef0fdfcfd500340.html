Chapter 1 - Use-Based Properties

Before We Get Started

  • This level groups real estate into three ways of understanding property: by its use, shared ownership, and investment.

  • Use
    • This chapter covers broad property categories based on how land or buildings are used, such as farming or warehousing.
  • Common Interest in Ownership
    • Chapter 2 covers properties defined by shared ownership; they may also fit into different use-based categories.
  • Investment
    • Chapter 3 explores property used as investments and how those investments work.
  • Finding Your Niche
    • Build broad knowledge of real estate, then choose and master a specialty—such as condominiums, investment properties, or REITs—to grow as an effective agent.
  • Miscommunication Station
    • Christy assumed a referred, lender-qualified buyer understood the process and had the cash to close. After many showings and an accepted condo offer, she learned the buyer lacked funds for the down payment and closing costs; relying on credit cards could affect the buyer’s debt ratio.
    • Key Takeaways
      • Don’t assume a referral or lender qualification covers everything. Confirm the buyer’s understanding, financing, and funds for down payment and closing costs early.
      • Communicate and vet clients before investing significant time in showings and offers; financial changes can affect loan approval.

Types of Property

  • This chapter groups property by use into five types:
    • Residential
    • Commercial
    • Industrial
    • Agricultural
    • Special purpose
  • Residential Property
    • Residential property is used for habitation. Single-family homes are common, but residential real estate includes many other types.
    • Types of Residential Property
      • Single-family residences
      • Vacation properties
      • Townhouses
      • Condominiums
      • Apartment buildings
      • Manufactured homes
        • Built off-site and designed to be movable, manufactured homes are often called mobile homes.
        • Real or Personal Property? A home that can be moved is generally personal property. In Tennessee, a home becomes real property when permanently affixed to land and documented with an affidavit of affixation.
        • Example: Maria’s mobile home in a park is personal property while it remains movable. For it to be treated as real property, it must be affixed to land and the affidavit filed.
    • Residential property must be approved for habitation; a movie theater, for example, is not residential simply because it has comfortable seats.
    • Example: Residential Rita helps an elderly couple sell their townhome as they downsize to a smaller home on a larger rural lot. She needs to understand the rules for habitable, residential property.
  • Commercial Real Estate
    • Commercial property is generally used for business and revenue generation, and often treated as an investment. Property type determines which laws, contracts, and forms apply to a sale.
    • Commercial properties range in size and serve different business needs.
    • Types of Commercial Property
      • Office buildings
        • Office Building Classes
          • Classes indicate relative market desirability and may affect rents, occupancy, and perceived age and quality. Factors include building age, lease rates, construction, design, and amenities.
          • Class A: New or recently renovated, well-located, stylish, and amenity-rich; commands the highest rents and helps set market rates.
          • Class B: Older but maintained and generally desirable; rents are below Class A, and upgrades may improve the property.
          • Class C: Older, less maintained, and often less favorably located; typically rents for less and may be redeveloped.
      • Retail complexes
      • Gas stations
      • Entertainment venues
      • Restaurants
    • Example: Commercial Camila shows a restaurant buyer single-family homes, but zoning prevents them from being used as restaurants. Knowing the property type and intended use avoids wasted time.
    • Healthcare Real Estate: Medical properties leased to healthcare providers or organizations, including nursing homes, rehabilitation hospitals, assisted-living facilities, and medical office buildings.
    • Commercial Compliance: Commercial real estate follows different laws and processes than residential property. Agents need specialized education, and larger deals are often handled by firms focused on commercial real estate.
  • Industrial Real Estate
    • Industrial property supports manufacturing, distribution, and warehousing. Buildings often have specialized features, and leases are typically longer than residential or commercial leases.
    • Many industrial properties are investments, but specialized tenant needs also make owner-occupancy common.
    • Types of Industrial Property
      • Manufacturing plants
      • Distribution facilities
      • Warehouses
      • Storage facilities
      • Research facilities
      • Data centers
    • Example: Industrial Ingrid wants a warehouse for her new business, Nile, and seeks an agent with industrial expertise. Because the lease may last several years, she wants guidance through the transaction.
  • Agricultural Real Estate
    • Includes land and businesses used to grow or provide food, feed, fiber, livestock, plants, wood, and other resources. Properties range from raw land to highly improved farms.
    • Types of Agricultural Property
      • Family and commercial farms
      • Ranches
      • Orchards
      • Nurseries and greenhouses
      • Pastures
      • Feedlots
    • Property Taxes: An owner’s on-site home and related residential structures are generally assessed as residential property, while employee housing may be included with the agricultural property assessment.
    • Example: Agricultural Augustus is evaluating a cabbage farm with a home, barn, and employee housing. His agent should help him find the appropriate source to determine how each improvement is classified for property-tax purposes.
  • Special-Purpose Real Estate
    • These properties are designed or converted for a specific use and are not typically investment properties. They are often publicly owned, costly to repurpose, and less frequently sold than other property types.
    • Types of Special-Purpose Property
      • Places of worship
      • Schools
      • Recreational facilities
      • Cemeteries
      • Government-held land
      • Hotels, golf courses, and movie theaters may also be considered leisure or special-purpose properties.
    • Example: Special Sonia wants to open a school and works with an agent who understands special-purpose properties to find a site that meets zoning and legal requirements.
