Chapter 2 - Types of Agency and Agents in Real Estate

Tennessee Agent Types

  • Allowed Agency Types in Tennessee:
    • Single agents
    • Designated agents
    • Dual agents
    • Subagents
    • Facilitators
    • Brokerage Discretion: State law permits these agency types, but individual principal brokers decide which types their brokerage and specific licensees are authorized to practice based on experience and training.
  • Default Representation Status (Facilitator)
    • Written Agreement Requirement: Agency agreements in Tennessee must be in writing.
    • Default Status: Until a formal written agency agreement is executed, any interaction between a licensee and a party defaults to transaction brokerage (non-agency), with the licensee legally operating as a neutral facilitator.
  • The Single Agent
    • Definition: Single agency is an arrangement in which an agent represents only one party (either a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant) in a real estate transaction.
    • Brokerage-Level Representation: Because agency is established at the broker level, when a brokerage practices single agency, all affiliate brokers sponsored by that principal broker represent that single client in the transaction.
    • Key Characteristics & Benefits
      • Minimal Conflicts of Interest: Single agency is highly popular because it virtually eliminates the conflicts of interest and confusion common in dual agency.
      • Traditional Approach: Represents the traditional, undivided-loyalty model of real estate representation.
      • Working Relationships: A single agent works for their client (fiduciary duties owed) and works with customers on the opposing side (fair dealing and honesty owed).
    • Documentation & Creation
      • Must be formally documented in writing using either a listing agreement (for sellers/landlords) or a buyer representation agreement (for buyers/tenants).
    • Duties of the Single Agent
      • Single agents owe rigorous, undivided duties to their clients, whether representing a seller or a buyer. These duties reflect core fiduciary responsibilities combined with statutory real estate requirements.
    • Duties of a Seller's Agent (Listing Agent)
      • Fulfill all specific duties outlined in the listing agreement.
      • Seek a sales price and terms matching the agreement or acceptable to the seller.
      • Present all written offers and counteroffers in a timely manner.
      • Disclose all material adverse facts regarding the transaction.
      • Advise the seller to seek expert advice on matters beyond the agent's expertise.
      • Account for all money and property received in a timely manner.
      • Comply with all federal, state, and local laws.
      • Exercise reasonable skill and care.
      • Maintain strict seller confidentiality.
      • Note: These duties do not prevent a seller's agent from listing competing properties or showing other properties to buyers.
    • Duties of a Buyer's Agent
      • Fulfill all specific duties outlined in the buyer representation agreement.
      • Seek a suitable property at a price and terms matching the agreement or acceptable to the buyer.
      • Present all written offers and counteroffers in a timely manner.
      • Disclose all material adverse facts regarding the transaction.
      • Advise the buyer to seek expert advice on matters beyond the agent's expertise.
      • Account for all money and property received in a timely manner.
      • Comply with all federal, state, and local laws.
      • Exercise reasonable skill and care.
      • Maintain strict buyer confidentiality.
      • Note: These duties do not prevent a buyer's agent from showing properties of interest to multiple prospective buyers.
  • The Designated Agent
    • Definition: Designated agency is an arrangement where the principal broker assigns a specific licensee within the brokerage to exclusively represent a single client (buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant) in a transaction.
    • Exclusive Representation: The designated agent is the only licensee in the firm who holds an agency relationship with that client.
    • Key Characteristics & Confidentiality
      • Isolation of Confidential Info: Only the designated agent possesses intimate, confidential knowledge regarding the client (such as maximum affordability, true motivation, or time pressures).
      • Maintaining Arm's Length: To protect negotiation leverage, the designated agent must keep all other license holders—even those within the same brokerage—at arm's length.
      • Single-Agent Simulation: The designated agent operates essentially as a one-person brokerage representing a single client, treating all other in-house agents as if they represent the opposing party.
    • In-House Transactions
      • Importance: This strict segregation is critical when another licensee in the same brokerage represents the opposing principal, creating an in-house transaction
    • Two Clients; One Brokerage Overview
      • In-House Transaction: Occurs when both principals (buyer and seller, or landlord and tenant) to a transaction are represented by the same brokerage.
      • Common Scenarios:
      1. A buyer client decides to view properties listed within their own agent's brokerage.
      1. A seller client permits buyers represented by the same brokerage to tour their listing.
    • The Shift from Single Agency to Dual Agency
      • Single Agency Rule: In brokerages practicing standard single agency (without designated agency), every licensee in the firm represents all clients. Consequently, representing both opposing principals in an in-house transaction creates dual agency.
      • Brokerage Choices When a Second Client Appears:
      1. Proceed with dual agency (navigating competing interests and compromises, which is heavily regulated and relatively rare).
      1. Terminate the relationship with one of the principals to maintain single agency.
    • Documentation and Written Agreement
      • Mandatory Transition: Moving from single agency to dual agency requires terminating the old agreement and executing a new agency agreement in writing.
      • Example: If Buyer Colin (represented under single agency by Broker Selena) wants to view an in-house listing, Selena must fully disclose dual agency mechanics and secure a signed dual agency agreement before showing the property.
  • The Dual Agent
  • Definition: Dual agency is an agency relationship where both the buyer and the seller (or opposing principals) are represented by the same broker in an in-house real estate transaction.
    • Legal Status in Tennessee: While legal in Tennessee, dual agency is tricky, problematic, and rarely practiced because many brokerages choose to prohibit it. Always verify your specific brokerage's policy.
    • Informed Consent Requirement: Before entering into dual agency, both parties must give informed consent in writing.
    • Compromise of Fiduciary Duties
      • Because the two parties have inherently conflicting interests (e.g., sellers want the highest price and fewest contingencies; buyers want the lowest price and maximum protection), representation is severely limited.
      • In dual agency, licensees cannot provide:
        • Full disclosure (certain confidential information must be protected).
        • Undivided loyalty (the agent cannot champion one party's financial interests over the other's).
    • Dual Agency vs. Designated Agency Overview
      • In-House Transactions: Both agency types occur when an in-house transaction involves clients of the same brokerage, but they operate under vastly different rules.
      • Designated Agency (Full Representation): Allows designated agents to fulfill their full set of fiduciary duties—including undivided loyalty and full disclosure—because the principal broker assigns separate, isolated licensees to each opposing client.
      • Dual Agency (Compromised Representation): Involves shared representation where duties like full disclosure and undivided loyalty are inherently compromised due to conflicting client interests.
    • The Mechanics of Designated Agency
      • Strict Information Isolation: In a designated agency in-house transaction, the designated agents do not share confidential client details with anyone else in the firm—not even with each other or the principal broker.
      • Acting as a One-Person Brokerage: Each designated agent exclusively champions their assigned client's interests without conflicting obligations.
      • Vicarious Supervisory Responsibility: Although the principal broker and other affiliate brokers remain entirely outside the agency relationship, the principal broker still holds ultimate vicarious supervisory responsibility for the actions of both designated agents.
      • Legal Distinction: Because the firm isolates representation, designated agency is not considered dual agency.
  • The Subagent
    • Definition: Subagency is a form of agency created when an agent enlists the help of other agents to achieve a client's goal (essentially, an agent of an agent). While sponsored licensees are technically subagents to their broker's clients internally, the term traditionally refers to outside cooperation.
    • Traditional Application: Occurs when an unaffiliated cooperating broker (from a different brokerage) and their licensees assist a listing broker in finding a buyer for a seller-client.
    • Key Rules & Relationships
      • Exclusivity of Role: A cooperating broker can only operate as a subagent if they are not representing the buyer. If they represent the buyer, they are a buyer's agent, not a subagent.
      • Fiduciary Obligations: Subagents owe the principal (the seller) the exact same high standard of fiduciary duties as the primary listing agent.
      • Mandatory Disclosure: Cooperating brokers must disclose their agency relationships to all parties upfront.
    • Rarity and Risks in Tennessee
      • Legality vs. Practice: While legal in Tennessee, subagency is rare and risky.
      • Why It's Rare:
        • Listing brokers face heightened vicarious liability for the actions of unaffiliated licensees whom they do not directly supervise.
        • Because buyer's agents typically receive an equal split of the listing commission (just like subagents), cooperating brokers prefer the protections of representing buyers directly rather than acting as subagents for the seller.
  • Facilitators & Transaction Brokerage Overview
    • Definition: A facilitator practices transaction brokerage, which is a form of non-agency rather than an agency relationship. They assist both parties in completing a transaction without representing, favoring, or acting as an advocate for either party.
    • Required Disclosures: When working with a customer, a facilitator must state in writing that they are not an agent, are not an advocate, and will only provide limited confidentiality.
    • Facilitator Duties & Ministerial Acts
      • Core Responsibilities: Perform ministerial acts to move the transaction forward, such as:
        • Handling funds, documents, and materials.
        • Presenting all offers and counteroffers.
        • Keeping parties informed of progress and assisting with closing.
      • Standard of Conduct Owed to Both Parties:
        • Reasonable care and skill.
        • Honesty and fair dealing.
        • Confidentiality and impartiality.
        • Disclosure of material facts regarding the property.
        • Advising parties to seek expert or legal counsel when appropriate.
    • Facilitator by Default & Agreement
      • The Default Rule: Because Tennessee law strictly mandates that all agency agreements must be in writing, any relationship between a licensee and a party defaults to a neutral facilitator (transaction brokerage) until a formal written agency agreement is established.
      • Facilitator by Agreement: If a licensee (or someone associated with them) represents the opposing party, the licensee must execute a written agreement explicitly stating they are operating as a facilitator and not a dual agent.
      • The "Either/Or" Rule: A licensee cannot split their role in a transaction; they cannot act as a facilitator to one party and an agent to the other. They must serve as a facilitator to both or a dual agent to both.\
  • Recap

Key Term

  • designated agent - the only licensee in a brokerage who has been designated by the principal broker to act as agent of a party in a transaction; no other licensees in the brokerage have that agency relationship

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Tennessee Agent Types
    • Single agent - One agent represents one party in a real estate transaction; all agents in that brokerage share in the duties of that representation.
    • Designated agent - A licensee is designated to represent a single client in a transaction; the designated agent for this client is the ONLY licensee in the firm with an agency relationship with that client.
    • Dual agent - Both the buyer and the seller are represented by the same broker in an in-house real estate transaction.
    • Subagent - An agent of an agent; traditionally, an unaffiliated cooperating broker and/or their licensees helping a listing broker find a buyer for the listing broker's seller-client.
    • Facilitator - A licensee practicing a form of non-agency; fulfills ministerial acts to assist in the completion of the transaction for the parties who are considered customers.
  • In Writing - All agency relationships should be documented in writing with either a listing agreement or a buyer representation agreement.
