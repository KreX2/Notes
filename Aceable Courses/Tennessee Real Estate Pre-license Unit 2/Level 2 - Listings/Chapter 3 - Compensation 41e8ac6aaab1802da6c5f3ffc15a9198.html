Chapter 3 - Compensation

Negotiability of Compensation & Antitrust Rules

  • 100% Negotiable Between Broker and Client: Commission rates and fee structures are never fixed by law, real estate commissions, or REALTOR® associations. Agreeing on standard commission rates across competing brokerages, towns, or states constitutes illegal price fixing under the Sherman Antitrust Act.
  • Internal Brokerage Policy vs. Client Negotiation: A managing/principal broker may establish a typical commission range or minimum fee policy within their own firm, but the final compensation terms must always be negotiated and agreed upon in writing between the brokerage and the client.
  • Compensation ≠ Agency or Loyalty: Who pays the commission does not determine fiduciary loyalty or agency status. A buyer's agent whose fee is funded from seller proceeds still owes 100% fiduciary loyalty to the buyer.

  • Broker Compensation Mechanics: When Earned, Paid & Enforced
    • Six Essential Guideposts for Employment Agreements:
      1. Always negotiable between the parties.
      1. Must be in writing and signed by the parties (T.C.A. § 62-13-401).
      1. Can be based on any lawful condition (excluding illegal net listings under TREC Rule 1260-02-.07).
      1. Does not have to be a percentage commission—flat fees, hourly rates, or retainers are permitted.
      1. Must clearly state when payment is earned.
      1. May come from both buyer and seller only with full disclosure and written informed consent from all parties.
    • Earned vs. Payable Tradition: Traditionally, a broker's commission is earned once the broker produces a "ready, willing, and able buyer" and a binding meeting of the minds (accepted purchase contract) is achieved, while actual payment is deferred until closing.
    • Client Default Protection: Properly drafted employment agreements typically stipulate that if the broker's client defaults on an accepted purchase contract without legal excuse, the earned commission remains enforceable against the defaulting client despite the closing failing to occur.
  • Licensee Compensation & Commission Splits (T.C.A. § 62-13-312(b)(11))
    • Brokerage-to-Affiliate Payment Rule: Because every written agency contract is legally between the client and the sponsoring principal broker (firm), the brokerage receives the commission first and then pays the affiliate broker according to their separate independent-contractor or employment split agreement.
    • Strict Statutory Prohibition (T.C.A. § 62-13-312(b)(11)): An affiliate broker may accept a commission or valuable consideration only from their affiliated principal broker—accepting direct payment from a client, cooperating broker, or third party is grounds for TREC license suspension or revocation. (Note: Under Tennessee Attorney General Opinion 21-01, a principal broker may authorize a closing/title company to disburse the affiliate's split directly at closing, provided the payment originates legally from the principal broker's earned fee.)
    • The "Split Before the Split" Sequence:
      1. Brokerage-to-Brokerage Split (or Separate Fee Disbursement): First, the total transaction compensation is divided between the listing brokerage and the cooperating buyer's brokerage according to their written agreement.
      1. Broker-to-Affiliate Split: Next, each principal broker divides their brokerage's share with their sponsored affiliate broker according to their internal commission split schedule (e.g., 70/30, 80/20, or 100% minus transaction fees).

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Compensation Does Not Determine Agency
    • Oftentimes, compensation includes payment of a cooperating broker, regardless of whether or not the cooperating broker represented the best interests of the principal who is paying. This is why it’s important for license holders and clients alike to understand that compensation ≠ loyalty, agency, or fiduciary duty.
  • Who Pays Whom
    • When a listing broker takes on a client as their principal, the broker negotiates how compensation is earned, what form compensation will take, and who will be eligible for compensation (this includes cooperating brokers operating as the principal’s subagent or a buyer’s agent).
    • When a buyer enters into a representation agreement with a broker, they negotiate how the buyer's broker will be compensated. Often, there is language stating that the broker's compensation will come from the commission split offered by the seller. Even then, the buyer-broker agreement typically ensures compensation for the buyer's broker in the event compensation is not derived from the seller.
    • There may even be occasions when a broker is eligible to receive compensation from both the buyer and seller. While that sounds like heaven, a license holder needs to tread carefully in these situations to stay on the right side of license law.
  • Licensee Compensation
    • Because a written employment agreement is always between the client and the broker, it will always be the broker, not the affiliate broker, who earns compensation. This means that affiliate brokers always receive their commission from their sponsoring broker.
    • The broker then turns around and pays the affiliate broker according to the employment agreement (which is completely separate from any contract the client signed).
