Chapter 1 - Easements and Liens

Real Property and Land-Use Controls

  • The Bundle of Rights:
    • Defines the core ownership rights: Possession, Control, Enjoyment, Exclusion, and Disposition.
    • Grants property owners authority over their land, though these rights are not absolute.
  • Purpose of Land-Use Controls:
    • Regulate how land is used and protect surrounding ownership rights.
    • Set guidelines on construction, environmental protections, and community development standards to prevent unregulated land use.
  • Three Types of Land-Use Controls:
    • Public Land-Use Controls: Government-issued regulations, such as zoning ordinances, subdivision regulations, and building codes.
    • Private Land-Use Controls: Rules set by non-governmental entities like real estate developers, most commonly executed as deed restrictions.
    • Public Ownership of Land: Government ownership and maintenance of public infrastructure and spaces, such as streets, highways, and public parks.
  • Private Land-Use Controls: Restrictive Covenants
    • Definition & Mechanism:
      • Restrictive Covenant: A limit on land use imposed directly by a property owner or developer.
      • Application: Applied through a property's conveyance instrument (most commonly recorded within the deed).
    • "Runs With the Land":
      • Title Chain Integration: Once a restrictive covenant is recorded in the chain of title, all future owners are legally subject to it.
      • Core Rule: "Runs with the land" means the restriction attaches permanently to the physical property itself, not to the specific individual who owned it when created.
    • Practical Example:
      • Scenario: Raquel purchases a home with a deed restriction banning poultry/chickens on the property.
      • Outcome: When Raquel eventually sells the home, the new buyer is also prohibited from raising chickens because the covenant stays with the property.
    • Deed Restrictions and CC&Rs
      • Definition & Key Distinction:
        • Deed Restriction: A specific provision attached directly to a property's deed that limits land use, runs with the land, and legally binds all future owners.
        • Restrictive Covenant vs. Deed Restriction: While often used interchangeably, a restrictive covenant is a general limit on land use that may exist in various legal instruments, whereas a deed restriction is specifically embedded in the deed itself.
        • CC&Rs: Both terms are broadly categorized under Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs).
      • Developer Implementation & Standards:
        • Most deed restrictions are created by developers and disclosed to buyers within initial sales contracts.
        • To be valid and legally enforceable, deed restrictions must:
          • Not be discriminatory
          • Promote the general welfare of the public
          • Be applied consistently
          • Be exercised reasonably
          • Be clear and specific
      • Incorporation & Plat Reference:
        • In subdivisions, individual deeds reference a recorded plat map (a detailed map establishing lot boundary lines).
        • Restrictions are included in the deed by reference, creating binding title limitations for every owner within the development.
      • What Deed Restrictions Can and Can't Do
        • Scope of Valid Deed Restrictions:
          • Types of buildings and acceptable land uses.
          • Construction specifications (height, setbacks, minimum square footage).
          • Minimum construction costs or quality requirements.
          • Aesthetic limitations (exterior materials, paint colors, design styles).
          • Prohibited structures or additions (e.g., banning unapproved sheds, exterior antennas, or parked boats/RVs).
        • Legal Limits & Fair Housing Protections:
          • Restrictions must be reasonable and intended for the general benefit of subdivision owners.
          • Cannot violate local, state, or federal laws.
          • Restrictions violating Fair Housing laws (e.g., restricting property sales or occupancy based on race or religion) are illegal, void, and completely unenforceable.
        • Court Rulings & Impact on Title:
          • If a court voids a restrictive covenant, the deed itself remains fully valid—the property title simply stands free from the invalid condition.
          • Deed restrictions regulate property use, not how title is legally transferred to a buyer.
        • Practical Examples:
          • Valid: Requiring homes to have at least 2,500 sq. ft. with 75% brick exterior, and prohibiting street parking for boats/RVs.
          • Invalid: Restricting property sales exclusively to individuals of a specific religion or race.
      • Zoning Ordinance vs. Deed Restriction
        • Relative Strictness:
          • Deed restrictions frequently set tighter limits on land use than local zoning laws (e.g., zoning allows general residential use, but deed restrictions permit only single-family detached homes).
        • The "Strictest Rule" Principle:
          • When zoning ordinances and private deed restrictions overlap, property owners must comply with whichever rule is more restrictive.
        • Legal Conflicts & Unenforceability:
          • If a deed restriction directly conflicts with local, state, or federal laws—such as discriminatory covenants violating the Fair Housing/Civil Rights Act of 1968—it is void and legally unenforceable.
        • Right of Alienation:
          • Deed restrictions and covenants cannot legally restrict or prevent an owner from selling, transferring, or mortgaging their property in the future.
      • Conditional Deed Restrictions
        • Standard vs. Conditional Restrictions:
          • Standard Deed Restrictions: Violations can result in legal action or lawsuits from neighbors or former owners, but do not cause a loss of property ownership.
          • Conditional Deed Restrictions: Stipulate that if an owner violates the restriction, title/ownership of the property reverts to the previous owner.
        • Qualified Fee Estate:
          • Properties bound by conditional deed restrictions are held as a qualified fee estate (or defeasible fee estate) rather than a fee simple absolute estate.
        • Title Company Role:
          • Title companies specifically search for conditional deed restrictions prior to closing to verify that no reversionary conditions cloud the buyer's fee simple title.
      • Enforcement of Restrictive Covenants and Deed Restrictions
        • Legal Remedy (Injunction): If a property owner violates a restrictive covenant or deed restriction, neighboring property owners or the homeowners association (HOA) can file for a court injunction.
        • Court Order: An injunction legally mandates the violator to fix the issue or immediately cease the prohibited activity.
        • Contempt of Court: Disregarding or refusing to comply with a court-issued injunction places the violator in contempt of court, which carries severe legal penalties, including fines or enforcement actions.
      • The Doctrine of Laches
        • Definition: A legal principle that bars dated or delayed legal claims if property owners fail to enforce a deed restriction or covenant within a reasonable time frame.
        • Core Principle: "Enforce it or lose the privilege of enforcing it." Derived from the equitable maxim: "Equity aids the vigilant, not the indolent."
        • Purpose: Prevents selective or arbitrary enforcement. It ensures that restrictions are applied fairly and uniformly to all neighbors rather than selectively targeting specific individuals.
        • Legal Consequence of Inaction: Failing to file a timely court injunction against a known violation can cause the community to forfeit the legal right to enforce that restriction in the future, potentially terminating the covenant altogether.
        • Practical Scenario (Indiana vs. Belloq):
          • Belloq installs prohibited backyard floodlights.
          • If Indiana and the surrounding neighbors do not file for an injunction within the legally defined time frame, the doctrine of laches prevents them from forcing Belloq to remove the lights later.
      • Ways to Terminate a Deed Restriction or Restrictive Covenant
        • Built-In Expiration: Many restrictions include baked-in end dates (e.g., expiring automatically 25 years after creation).
        • Formal Release: The owner can be formally released from the covenant by a court, the homeowners association (HOA) or neighborhood association, or the original creator of the restriction.
        • Drastic Change in Conditions: If surrounding conditions change so significantly that the covenant no longer serves its original purpose or provides benefit to anyone, a court may terminate it.
        • Enactment of New Laws: If a new local, state, or federal law makes the restriction illegal or unenforceable (such as anti-discrimination legislation), the restriction is voided.
        • Abandonment: The restriction can end if the property itself is abandoned, or if the covenant is consistently ignored and universally unenforced over a period of time.
      • Key Takeaways
        • Enforcement & Mechanism: Created by private entities (most commonly real estate developers) through restrictive covenants or deed restrictions recorded within the property's chain of title.
        • Runs With the Land: Restrictions attach directly to the physical real estate rather than the owner, permanently binding all current and future property owners until legally terminated or expired.
        • Legal Requirements & Fair Housing: Must be reasonable, consistent, specific, and designed for the general welfare of the community. Restrictions violating local, state, or federal laws—especially discriminatory covenants violating Fair Housing laws—are null, void, and completely unenforceable.
        • The Strictest Rule Principle: When private deed restrictions and public zoning laws address the same land-use aspect, property owners must comply with whichever rule is more restrictive (as long as the restriction is legal).
        • Enforcement Remedies & Injunctions: Enforced by neighboring owners or an HOA filing for a court injunction. Continued non-compliance results in contempt of court.
        • Doctrine of Laches: Property owners must enforce violations in a timely manner. Unreasonable delays in seeking legal remedies can result in losing the right to enforce the restriction altogether under the principle of laches ("equity aids the vigilant").
        • Termination Methods: Restrictions end via baked-in expiration dates (e.g., 25-year terms), formal legal release, drastic changes in surrounding conditions, passage of conflicting laws, or property abandonment/universal lack of enforcement.
      • Scenario Practice: Religious Discrimination in Deed Restrictions (Kyber Heights)
        • Real estate developer Master Jason creates a residential subdivision ("Kyber Heights") and attaches a deed restriction specifying that only members of the Church of Jediism may purchase property. Nora, a Methodist, buys a house in the development and displays Christmas decorations. Jason threatens legal action to enforce the covenant, demanding she convert or cease celebrating non-Jedi holidays.
        • Court Ruling & Enforceability: Unenforceable. A court will not enforce Master Jason's deed restriction.
        • Key Legal Principles & Takeaways:
          • Fair Housing Act Violation: Federal law (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968) prohibits discrimination in housing based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability.
          • Illegal Restrictions Are Void: Any restrictive covenant or deed restriction that discriminates based on a protected class violates federal law, making it automatically void and legally unenforceable—regardless of whether the buyer signed the contract or agreed to the deed terms.
          • Property Rights Preserved: Because the discriminatory restriction is void, Nora retains her home and full rights of property use without being subject to the illegal condition.
          • Deed Restrictions vs. HOA Decorative Rules: While an HOA or deed restriction cannot ban specific religious holidays or restrict ownership by faith, communities can enforce content-neutral aesthetic rules regarding holiday displays (e.g., limits on noise, lighting intensity, display sizes, or time frames for putting up and taking down decorations) as long as those rules apply equally to all residents.
  • Encumbrances and Property Interests
    • Possessory vs. Non-Possessory Interests:
      • Possessory Interest: Includes the legal right to physically possess and occupy the property now or in the future (e.g., homeownership).
      • Non-Possessory Interest: An interest in real property that does not grant the right to possess or occupy the land.
    • Definition of an Encumbrance:
      • A non-possessory interest that places a burden, restriction, or financial obligation on a property's title.
      • Limits the owner's full use of the property and affects clear title.
      • Attaches to the Title (Not the Owner): Encumbrances run with the land—they stay attached to the specific property and automatically transfer to the new owner upon conveyance.
    • Impact on Buyers and Marketability:
      • Reduced Value & Marketability: Certain encumbrances can make a home less appealing, lower its market value, or complicate financing/closing.
      • Inherited Liabilities: Buyers inherit existing encumbrances if they are not cleared prior to or at closing.
      • Deal Risk: Undisclosed or unexpected "surprise" encumbrances discovered during title searches often cause transactions to fall through.
    • Common Types of Encumbrances:
      • Deed Restrictions & Restrictive Covenants (private land-use limits)
      • Easements (right of another party to use a portion of the land)
      • Liens (financial claims against the property for unpaid debts)
      • Leases (tenant rights to possession)
      • Clouds on Title (claims or encumbrances that impair title clarity)

Key Terms

  • conveyance - an instrument that transfers a grantor’s interest in real property
  • covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CCRs) - private restrictions similar to deed restrictions that dictate what homeowner may do with their property
  • deed restriction - a provision in a deed that restricts the land use of a property
  • doctrine of laches - a legal principle used to bar dated claims; used in conjunction with an unreasonable delay or negligence in asserting or defending one’s rights
  • encumbrance - non-possessory interest in a property that burdens the title
  • land-use control - regulations that specify how the owners of individual parcels of real estate in a given area may use property

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Three Types of Land-Use Control
    • Public Land-Use Controls - These are government-issued land-use controls such as zoning ordinances, subdivision regulations, and building codes.
    • Private Land-Use Controls - Private land-use controls are put into place by non-governmental entities, such as real estate developers — most commonly, these come in the form of deed restrictions.
    • Public Ownership of Land - This concerns the role of the government to own and maintain public land such as streets, highways, and parks.
  • What Deed Restrictions Can't Do
    • Legal Compliance Requirement: Deed restrictions are legally valid only when they represent reasonable restraints for the collective benefit of all subdivision property owners and do not violate local, state, or federal laws.
    • Fair Housing Violations: Any deed restriction that violates fair housing laws—such as restricting property sales, occupancy, or leases based on race, religion, or other protected classes—is completely void and legally unenforceable.
  • The Strictest Rule Principle
    • Zoning vs. Deed Restrictions: When local zoning ordinances and private deed restrictions address the same property usage but have differing standards, property owners must abide by whichever rule is more restrictive.
    • Legal Conflicts: A deed restriction cannot override or conflict with municipal, state, or federal law; if a conflict exists, the illegal deed restriction cannot be enforced.
  • The Doctrine of Laches
    • Requirement for Prompt Enforcement: Property owners and homeowners associations must act within a reasonable time frame when a deed restriction is violated.
    • Forfeiture of Rights: Unreasonable delay or neglect in asserting enforcement rights causes the community to forfeit the ability to enforce the restriction later under the doctrine of laches.
    • Uniform Enforcement: Ensures rules are applied fairly and consistently across the entire community rather than enforced selectively against specific neighbors.
  • Impact of Encumbrances on Property Buyers
    • Reduced Marketability: Encumbrances place non-possessory burdens on property titles. Severe encumbrances (such as liens or unresolvable easements) can render a property unmarketable unless cleared before or at closing.
    • Inherited Liabilities: Buyers are cautious of encumbered properties because encumbrances attach to the physical property title and automatically transfer to the new owner upon purchase.
