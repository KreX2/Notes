Chapter 2 - Federal Disability Law

Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) vs. Fair Housing Act (FHA)

  • Passed in 1990, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is a federal civil rights law prohibiting discrimination against—and establishing accessibility standards for—individuals with disabilities.
    FeatureFair Housing Act (FHA)Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
    Protected Classes CoveredCovers seven protected classes (race, color, national origin, religion, sex, disability, and familial status).Covers only disability.
    Property & Sector ScopeApplies to residential dwellings (apartments, condos, single-family rentals, HOAs, and sales).Applies to employment, public accommodations and commercial facilities (including public leasing offices), public/government services, public transportation, and telecommunications.
  • Legal Definition of Disability (Shared by Both ADA and FHA)
    • Under both statutes, a person with a disability is any individual who:
      1. Has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, or
      1. Has a record of such an impairment (e.g., cancer in remission), or
      1. Is regarded as having such an impairment.
    • (Terminology Note: While older statutes and forms still use the word "handicap," "disability" is the preferred modern standard.)
    • Qualifying Physical or Mental Impairments:
      • Hearing impairments, mobility impairments, visual impairments, chronic alcoholism, chronic mental illness, AIDS and AIDS-related complex, and intellectual/developmental disabilities.
    • Substance Use — Current vs. In Recovery:
      • Not Protected: Current illegal use of controlled substances is excluded from disability protection.
      • Protected: Individuals with drug or alcohol addiction who are in recovery (e.g., a recovering drug addict or sober alcoholic) are protected under both laws.
    • Major Life Activities:
      • Basic functions such as seeing, hearing, eating, sleeping, walking, standing, caring for oneself, and performing manual tasks.
  • Reasonable Modifications (Changes to Property)
    • Definition: Structural alterations or physical changes made to a dwelling or common area so that a person with a disability can fully use and enjoy the premises.
    • Examples:
      • Structural alterations: Installing a wheelchair ramp or widening doorways.
      • Environmental controls: Lowering light switches/thermostats or installing grab bars in bathtubs.
    • Cost & Restoration Rules:
      • Landlords and housing providers must allow reasonable modifications related to a tenant's disability.
      • In private housing, the tenant is generally responsible for paying for the modification and may also be required to pay to restore the interior of the unit to its original, unmodified condition at the end of the lease (if the modification would interfere with future tenants' use).
      • In some cases, a landlord may ask that a helpful modification (such as an exterior wheelchair ramp) remain in place for future tenants rather than requiring removal.
    • Undue Financial Burden Limit:
      • Housing providers are not required to approve or install modifications that impose an undue financial and administrative burden or a fundamental alteration of the property (e.g., requiring the owner of a small, older walk-up building to install an elevator for prospective tenant Darius would be an unreasonable financial burden).
  • Reasonable Accommodations (Changes to Policy)
    • Memory Rule — Policy, Not Property: An accommodation is a change or exception to a rule, policy, practice, or service, whereas a modification is a physical alteration to the property.
    • Common Examples of Reasonable Accommodations:
      • Providing interpreters or auxiliary aids to communicate effectively with a deaf or hard-of-hearing applicant or tenant.
      • Assigning a reserved accessible parking space close to a unit for a tenant with a mobility disability.
      • Granting an exception to a "no-pets" policy to allow a service or assistance animal for a tenant with a disability.
    • High-Risk Compliance Warning for Licensees:
      • Improperly denying a reasonable accommodation request is one of the highest-risk danger zones for a Fair Housing Act violation. Because a person's disability or need for an accommodation is not always visibly obvious, property managers and licensees must carefully evaluate every request before issuing a denial.
  • Service Animals vs. Emotional Support Animals (Assistance Animals)
    • Under housing and public access rules, requests to waive a "no pets" policy involve two distinct categories of animals:

      • Tasks include guiding blind individuals, alerting deaf individuals, pulling a wheelchair, seizure alert, or balance support.      		• Protected in public accommodations under the ADA and in residential housing under the Fair Housing Act (FHA).

      • Not considered pets; exempt from pet deposits and pet rent.
      Emotional Support Animals (ESAs)Also referred to as assistance animals; they are not individually trained to perform disability-related tasks.• Provide therapeutic comfort, companionship, and emotional support for individuals with mental or psychological conditions.• Not covered by the ADA in public places.

  • Physical Accessibility Standards: Fair Housing Act vs. ADA
    • Definition of Accessibility: Designing and constructing buildings or spaces so that they are usable and navigable by everyone, including individuals with mobility, visual, or hearing disabilities.
    • Statutory Coverage Deadlines:
      • Fair Housing Act (Residential): Applies to all newly constructed multi-family dwellings containing four or more units intended for first occupancy after March 13, 1991 (covering ground-floor units in non-elevator buildings and all units in elevator-equipped buildings).
      • Americans with Disabilities Act (Commercial & Public): Applies to newly constructed commercial facilities and places of public accommodation built after 1993 (and requires readily achievable barrier removal in existing public facilities).
    • Seven Mandatory Fair Housing Act Design & Construction Features:
      1. An accessible building entrance located on an accessible route
      1. Accessible common and public-use areas (such as lobbies, laundry rooms, mailrooms, and parking)
      1. Doors sufficiently wide to accommodate wheelchairs
      1. Accessible routes into and throughout each covered dwelling unit
      1. Light switches, electrical outlets, thermostats, and environmental controls placed in accessible locations
      1. Reinforcements in bathroom walls to allow safe installation of grab bars
      1. Usable kitchens and bathrooms configured with sufficient clear floor space for a wheelchair to maneuver
  • The Five Titles of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) & Enforcement
    ADA TitleArea GovernedCore Scope & Requirements
    Title IEmploymentProhibits disability discrimination in hiring, firing, promotions, and pay; requires employers with 15 or more employees to provide reasonable accommodations.
    Title IIPublic Entities & Public TransportationProhibits disability discrimination by state and local governments and covers public transportation systems, including bus and passenger train services.
    Title IIIPublic Accommodations & Commercial FacilitiesGoverns privately owned and operated facilities open to the public—including hotels, restaurants, retail stores, real estate brokerage offices open to the public, doctor's offices, golf courses, private schools, sports stadiums, and movie theaters.
    Title IVTelecommunicationsRequires telephone companies to provide telecommunications relay services (TRS) for individuals with hearing or speech impairments, and mandates closed captioning on federally funded public service announcements.
    Title VMiscellaneous ProvisionsAddresses the ADA's relationship to other federal/state laws, insurance and benefit plans, illegal drug use exclusions, anti-retaliation rules, and recovery of attorney's fees.
  • ADA Enforcement & Civil Penalties:
    • Enforced by the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ).
    • Curriculum Exam Benchmark: Civil penalties of up to $55,000 for a first violation and up to $110,000 for any subsequent violation (note: under current inflation-adjusted federal regulations, maximum DOJ Title III civil penalties reach $75,000 for a first violation and $150,000 for repeat violations).
  • Affected Businesses
    • The ADA affects a wide array of businesses and establishments, such as:
      1. Businesses with 15 or more employees
      1. Any facilities or offices affiliated with state or local government
      1. Public and private schools
      1. Hospitals and dental offices
      1. Restaurants
      1. Hotels and motels
      1. Grocery and retail stores

      1. Banks

Key Terms

  • disability - a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, the record of such impairment, or being regarded as having such an impairment

  • Modifications and Accommodations
    • The Fair Housing Act requires that landlords, property managers, and other housing operators make modifications to a property per a disabled tenant's request, as long as it does not impose an undue financial burden on the owner.
    • A modification will likely be a structural alteration to the property, like installing a wheelchair ramp or widening doorways. It can also refer to environmental controls, like lowered light switches or grab bars for bathtubs.
    • Along similar lines, a landlord or real estate professional must make any accommodation that helps a person with a disability acquire and enjoy their dwelling, as long as it does not cause them undue financial burden.
      • Modification: alteration to property
      • Accommodation: alteration to policy
  • Fair Housing Act vs. ADA
    • Protected Classes: The Fair Housing Act covers multiple protected classes, including disability. The ADA covers only disability.
    • Application: The Fair Housing Act applies to residential dwellings. The ADA applies to employment, public accommodations and commercial facilities, public transportation, and telecommunications.
    • Accessibility: The Fair Housing Act requires all newly constructed multi-family dwellings of four or more units intended for first occupancy after March 13, 1991 to have certain features. The Americans with Disability Act (ADA) requires that commercial and public buildings that are newly constructed (built after 1993) be accessible.
