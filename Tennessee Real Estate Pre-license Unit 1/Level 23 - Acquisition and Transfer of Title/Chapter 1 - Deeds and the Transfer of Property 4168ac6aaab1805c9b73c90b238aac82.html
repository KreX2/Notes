Chapter 1 - Deeds and the Transfer of Property

Acquisitions and Transfers Overview

  • Core Transaction Concept: When a buyer purchases real property, they acquire an ownership interest in that parcel along with all legal rights attached to it.
  • Conveyance & Instrument of Conveyance:
    • Conveyance: The legal act of transferring an ownership interest in real property from one party (the grantor) to another (the grantee).
    • Instrument of Conveyance: The formal legal tool used to execute that transfer—most commonly a deed.
  • Title vs. Deed: Key Distinctions
    • Although often confused in everyday conversation, title and deed refer to two distinct legal concepts in real estate transfers:
      ConceptNatureDefinition & Legal Role
      TitleAbstract legal conceptRepresents the bundle of legal ownership rights attached to a piece of real property (such as the rights of possession, control, enjoyment, exclusion, and disposition).
      DeedPhysical, written legal documentThe formal instrument of conveyance that transfers real estate title from one party to another, establishes an official public record of when property changes hands, and serves as proof of ownership transfer.
  • Grantor and Grantee
    • Every deed involves two primary parties:
      • Grantor: The current property owner who transfers (conveys) title to real property in a transaction.
      • Grantee: The party who receives the conveyance of title to real property in a transaction.
      • Example: When Alan sells his farm to Garrett, Alan is the grantor and Garrett is the grantee.
  • Alienation & Conveyance
    • Alienation: The general legal act of transferring title/ownership of real property from one party to another.
    • Conveyance: The formal transfer of title when put into writing using an instrument of conveyance (such as a deed).
      Type of AlienationDefinitionCommon Methods / Mechanisms
      Voluntary AlienationTransfer of title made willingly by the current owner of their own free will• By Deed (sale or gift)

      • Public Grant (government transfer of public land to a private party)

      • By Will (testate transfer upon the owner's death)
      Involuntary AlienationTransfer of title occurring without the owner's consent• Operation of Law (e.g., foreclosure, tax sale, eminent domain/condemnation, adverse possession, intestate succession, or court-ordered partition/judgment)
  • How Voluntary Alienation Works
    • Instrument Used: The owner (grantor) executes a deed to formally convey property title to the recipient (grantee).
    • Statute of Frauds Requirement: To be legally valid and enforceable, a deed transferring real property ownership must be in writing in compliance with the statute of frauds.
  • Types of Voluntary Alienation
    • Voluntary alienation occurs when an owner intentionally transfers title to real property of their own free will through four primary methods:
      Method of Voluntary AlienationDefinition & MechanismInstrument / Key Details
      Private GrantThe voluntary transfer of real property by a private party via sale or gift—the most common form of conveyance.Executed using a deed (grantor conveys title to grantee).
      DedicationThe voluntary gift of private land to the public for public use.Includes statutory dedication, which occurs during subdivision development when a developer dedicates paved streets, sidewalks, or utility easements to the local municipality.
      Public GrantThe transfer of government-owned public land to a private individual.Uses a land patent (rather than a deed) to convey title.
      DeviseThe transfer of real property upon the owner's death according to the instructions in a valid will.Transfers title at death (the recipient is called a devisee, and the will writer is the testator).
  • Involuntary Alienation Overview
    • Definition: The transfer of title to real property without the owner's consent, typically carried out by operation of law.
    • Seven Forms of Involuntary Alienation:
      1. Eminent domain (and condemnation)
      1. Foreclosure and lawsuits (e.g., mortgage foreclosure, tax sales, judgment liens, partition suits)
      1. Adverse possession (acquiring title through continuous, hostile, open, and notorious possession for a statutory period)
      1. Descent (intestate succession—transfer of property to legal heirs by state statute when an owner dies without a valid will)
      1. Escheat (reversion of property to the state when an owner dies without a valid will and leaves no identifiable heirs)
      1. Forfeiture of title (loss of title due to breaching a condition subsequent in a defeasible fee deed)
      1. Accession (acquisition of additional land or improvements through natural forces like accretion/alluvion or annexation)
  • Eminent Domain vs. Condemnation
    • Eminent Domain: The inherent constitutional right/power of the government (and authorized public utilities/agencies) to take private property for public use, subject to the payment of just compensation to the owner.
    • Condemnation (Appropriation): The formal legal proceeding/action through which the government exercises its power of eminent domain to acquire the private property and determine just compensation.
    • Key Distinction: Eminent domain is the right; condemnation is the action.
  • Foreclosure & Lawsuits (Legal Forms of Involuntary Alienation)
    • Foreclosure: One of the most common forms of involuntary alienation; when a loan is secured by real property (such as a mortgage or deed of trust) and the borrower defaults, the lender has the legal right to seize and sell the property without the borrower's consent.
    • Lawsuits: Court actions can resolve ownership disputes or compel a transfer of title when co-owners or claimants cannot reach a voluntary agreement:
      • Quiet Title Action: A legal remedy filed in court to clear a cloud on the title (such as an unresolved claim, boundary dispute, or defect) when an amicable agreement and a quitclaim deed cannot be obtained.
      • Partition Suit: A legal action used to sever joint ownership (such as tenancy in common or joint tenancy) when co-owners disagree on how to use, divide, or sell a property—resulting in either a physical division of the land (partition in kind) or a court-ordered sale with divided proceeds (partition by sale).
  • Adverse Possession Fundamentals
    • Definition: The involuntary transfer of title from a property owner who fails to use, inspect, or defend their land over a statutory period to another person who openly occupies and claims the land.
    • Purpose: Designed not to encourage theft, but to resolve long-standing boundary and title disputes when an owner "sleeps on their rights" and fails to eject a trespasser within the statutory timeframe.
    • Five Essential Attributes of Adverse Possession (Memory Aid: OCEAN / HAEOC):
      AttributeLegal Requirement
      Hostile (Adverse)Possession is without the true owner's permission and directly in opposition to the owner's rights and wishes.
      ActualPhysical dominion, occupation, or active use of the property must be apparent (not merely a verbal claim).
      ExclusiveThe adverse possessor holds possession solely for themselves, excluding the true owner and the general public.
      Open and NotoriousPossession is visible, obvious, and unconcealed so that a reasonably diligent owner inspecting the property would discover it.
      ContinuousPossession must be uninterrupted throughout the entire statutory period—the claimant cannot abandon the property and restart at will.
  • Adverse Possession Rules in Tennessee
    • Statutory Possession Periods:
      • 7 Years (With Color of Title): Required when the possessor holds color of title—a recorded instrument/document that appears to convey title on its face but is legally defective or invalid.
      • 20 Years (Without Color of Title): Required under common law prescription when the claimant has no written instrument purporting to convey title.
    • Property Tax Payment Presumption: Under Tennessee law, an adverse possessor who pays state and county property taxes on the land continuously for 20 or more years is legally presumed to be the prima facie owner of the property.
  • Descent vs. Escheat (Intestate Property Transfers)
    • Both descent and escheat occur when an owner dies intestate (without a valid will) and are classified as involuntary alienation because the decedent did not direct the transfer in writing. The core distinction is whether the decedent leaves behind known legal heirs:
      Transfer MethodCondition of HeirsWhat Happens to the Property
      Descent (Laws of Descent & Distribution / Intestate Succession)Decedent dies without a will with known heirs (surviving spouse, children, or next of kin)State intestacy statutes determine who inherits the property and in what shares.
      EscheatDecedent dies without a will with no surviving spouse, lineal descendants, or known heirsOwnership of the property reverts directly to the state so the land does not become ownerless.
  • Forfeiture of Title
    • Definition: A form of involuntary alienation occurring when an owner violates a deed restriction or condition attached to a defeasible fee estate, causing title to automatically revert back to the original grantor (or the grantor's heirs).
    • Example: Geanna is granted a 100-acre estate on the condition that it be used strictly for residential purposes. When she attempts to sell it to a commercial developer in violation of that condition, she forfeits title and the estate reverts back to the grantor.
  • Accession (Natural & Artificial Additions to Property)
    • Definition: The legal right of a property owner to all that their property produces, as well as any land or improvements added to it—whether through natural forces or human action (artificial accession)—without requiring a deed or other instrument of conveyance.
    • Artificial Accession: Occurs through human labor, construction, or annexation of permanent improvements onto land.
    • Natural Forces Affecting Land Area:
      Natural ForceDefinition & Effect on Land
      AccretionThe gradual increase in land area caused by natural deposits of soil/sediment along a waterway. (The actual soil deposited during accretion is called alluvion.)
      AvulsionThe sudden, violent loss (or tearing away) of land caused by an abrupt act of nature (such as a flash flood, earthquake, or sudden shift in a river's channel).
      RelictionThe gradual exposure of previously submerged land caused by the permanent recession of water (such as a lake, river, or sea receding).

Key Terms

  • devise - the transfer of real or personal property through a will
  • grantee - the person who receives a conveyance of real property in a transaction
  • grantor - the person who conveys real property in a real estate transaction
  • instrument of conveyance - a tool that transfers a grantor’s interest in real property
  • title - the actual ownership of a real property that includes the bundle of rights in which a party may own a legal or equitable interest; not an actual document

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Title vs. Deed
  • Two Types of Alienation
    • Voluntary, usually by sale or gift
    • Involuntary, usually by an operation of the law
  • Types of Voluntary Alienation
    • Private grant: selling the property or gifting the property to an individual
    • Dedication: giving the property to the government for public use
    • Public grant: the government giving a private entity land
    • Devise: willing the land to someone
  • Types of Involuntary Alienation
    • Eminent domain: The government takes the land for a public good and compensates the landowner.
    • Foreclosure: The lender seizes the property after the owner defaults on the loan.
    • Lawsuits: Legal action is taken to quiet title or sever joint ownership by partition.
    • Adverse possession: This is the involuntary transfer of title from an owner who does not use or inspect their land to another person who has some claim to the land and takes possession.
    • Escheat: A property owner dies with no will and no heirs, so the state takes the property.
    • Forfeiture of title: A deed contains some kind of condition or restriction that gets violated and causes a change in ownership.
    • Accession: Natural forces remove some of a property's land (doesn't result in a title or deed change).
