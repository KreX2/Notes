Chapter 2 - Offers and Counteroffers Review

The Players in an Offer

  • 1. Offeror: The party making the offer (typically the buyer submitting the initial written purchase offer on a listed property—though roles reverse on every counteroffer).
  • 2. Offeree: The party receiving the offer (typically the seller on an initial purchase offer).
  • "Time Is of the Essence" & Expiration Deadlines
    • In an Offer: Sets a strict expiration date and time by which the offeree must accept and communicate acceptance; if the deadline passes without communicated acceptance, the offer lapses automatically.
    • In a Binding Contract: Makes every contractual date and deadline strictly enforceable—missing a deadline by even one minute constitutes a breach of contract unless waived or extended in writing.
    • Offers Without an Explicit Expiration Date: If an offer omits a specific expiration deadline, the offeree has a reasonable time under the circumstances to accept before it lapses.
  • The Offeree's Three Options (TREC Rule 1260-02-.08)
    Offeree OptionLegal Effect & MechanicsTennessee Rule & Practice Notes
    1. Accept ItUnconditional agreement to every term and condition of the offer (Mirror Image Rule).The offer becomes a binding contract only when signed acceptance is communicated/delivered back to the offeror (or offeror's authorized agent) before the expiration deadline.
    2. Counter ItChanges any detail, big or small, which terminates and voids the original offer and replaces it with a new offer from the former offeree (Form RF651).Because a counteroffer kills the original offer, a seller cannot submit a counteroffer, have it rejected, and then go back to accept the buyer's original offer. Counteroffers must be in writing.
    3. Reject ItOutright refusal of the offer without proposing new terms; the offeree has no legal obligation to negotiate.Under TREC Rule 1260-02-.08, when an offer is rejected, the broker or affiliate broker shall request the seller to note the rejection in writing on the offer (sign and date) and return it to the offeror or offeror's agent. (Note: Also remember a licensee must promptly tender every written offer until a contract is signed by all parties.)
  • Review: How an Offer Becomes a Contract
  • Offer Presentation Best Practices & Seller Decision Support
    • Duty to Present All Offers Promptly (T.C.A. § 62-13-404(3) & TREC Rule 1260-02-.08): Licensees must promptly and objectively present every written offer until a purchase contract is signed by all parties—even offers the licensee expects the seller to reject.
    • Sole Exception (Prior Client Instruction): A licensee may screen out or decline offers only if the client gives explicit permission (such as a written minimum net/price threshold), though licensees should never encourage a seller to set rigid filters that block potential negotiations.
    • Helping Clients Evaluate Beyond Purchase Price: Guide sellers to weigh all material offer terms:
      • Closing date flexibility and possession timing
      • Buyer financial strength (pre-approval quality, cash vs. financing, down payment)
      • Earnest money deposit amount
      • Seller-paid closing cost concessions or repair demands
      • Contingencies (financing, appraisal, inspection, home-sale) that could delay or derail closing
  • Counteroffers & Negotiation Mechanics (Form RF651)
    • Legal Effect of a Counteroffer: Submitting a counteroffer (typically on Tennessee REALTORS® Counter Offer Form RF651) acts as a rejection of the prior offer, immediately voiding the previous offer and creating a brand-new offer with reversed roles (offeror becomes offeree). Under RF651, terms proposed in earlier counteroffers are excluded unless explicitly restated.
    • Written Confirmation Rule: Counteroffers must be in writing. Always qualify verbal negotiations as "subject to written confirmation" because no party is bound until all parties sign and deliver acceptance.
    • Revocation While Countering:
      • Either party may withdraw an offer or counteroffer at any time before acceptance is communicated.
      • If a seller has an outstanding counteroffer to Buyer A and receives a superior offer from Buyer B, the seller must withdraw the counteroffer to Buyer A in writing before accepting Buyer B's offer to avoid being bound to two buyers simultaneously.
      • Remind buyers that while a counteroffer is pending, the property remains on the market and the seller may accept another offer.
    • Presentation Order: Offers do not have to be presented in strict chronological order of receipt, though all offers and counteroffers must be presented objectively and as promptly as possible.
  • Multiple Offers, Seller Net Sheets & Escalation Clauses
    • Multiple-Offer Confidentiality (NAR Standard of Practice 1-15):
      • Only the seller controls whether the existence of multiple offers is disclosed—never disclose competing offers without the seller's consent.
      • The seller may choose to disclose competing offers to all buyers, selected buyers, or none. Advise the seller on market strategy: disclosure can spark competitive bidding in a seller's market, whereas it may deter cautious buyers in a buyer's market.
    • Seller Net Sheet: Prepare an itemized net sheet for every offer subtracting mortgage payoffs, prorated taxes, closing costs, concessions, and brokerage fees from the offered price so the seller can compare exact net proceeds at closing rather than headline price alone.
    • Escalation Clause (Escalator): A buyer provision stating a base offer price that automatically increases by a specified increment above any competing bona fide higher offer (typically up to a stated maximum cap). If no competing offer exists, the base price applies.
  • Post-Acceptance Marketing, Binding the Agreement & Commencement Date
    • Stopping Marketing Upon Acceptance: Once the seller accepts an offer, the listing agent is no longer obligated to continue marketing or showing the property unless the seller instructs otherwise in writing (such as soliciting backup offers).
    • Who Binds the Agreement ("Last Person Holding the Ball"):
      • A contract becomes binding when written acceptance of the final offer or counteroffer is communicated/delivered back to the final offeror (or that offeror's agent).
      • The agent who receives final communicated acceptance fills in the Binding Agreement Date (and time) on the purchase contract or final RF651 counteroffer.
    • Commencement Date ("Day One") Calculation:
      • Under Tennessee REALTORS® Form RF401, days are calendar days ending at 11:59 p.m. local time, and the commencement date (Day 1) is the day immediately following the Binding Agreement Date.
      • Example: If the Binding Agreement Date is November 2, then November 3 is the commencement date (Day 1) for all "time is of the essence" contractual deadlines (such as inspection and loan application periods). (Note: Under RF401, if a general performance deadline lands on a Saturday, Sunday, or federal holiday, it rolls to the next business day—except for the Closing Date, Date of Possession, Completion of Repairs Deadline, and Offer Expiration Date, which do not roll.)
  • Eight Ways an Offer Can Terminate
    • Like contracts, offers terminate either through the actions of the parties or by operation of law:
      Termination MethodTriggering Party / EventLegal Effect on the Offer
      1. AcceptanceOfferee unconditionally accepts and communicates acceptance before expiration.Terminates the open offer by converting it into a binding bilateral contract.
      2. CounterofferOfferee proposes any change—large or small—to the original offer terms.Immediately rejects and voids the original offer, replacing it with a new offer where offeror/offeree roles reverse.
      3. RejectionOfferee declines the offer outright without proposing new terms.Extinguish the offer immediately; the offeree cannot later change their mind and accept it. (Under TREC Rule 1260-02-.08, Tennessee licensees must request the seller note rejection in writing on the offer and return it.)
      4. Expiration (Lapse of Time)The offer's stated expiration date/time passes—or, if no deadline is stated, a reasonable time elapses.Terminates the offer automatically by operation of law; late acceptance acts only as a new offer.
      5. RevocationOfferor withdraws the offer and communicates notice to the offeree before acceptance.Cancels the offeree's power to accept—even if the stated expiration deadline has not yet arrived.
      6. Death or Mental IncapacityEither the offeror or the offeree dies or becomes legally incompetent prior to communicated acceptance.Terminates the unaccepted offer automatically by operation of law (unlike a binding purchase contract, which generally survives death).
      7. Destruction of the PropertyThe subject property is materially damaged or destroyed (e.g., by fire, flood, or tornado) before acceptance.Terminates the offer by operation of law due to impossibility / destruction of the subject matter.
      8. Improper AcceptanceOfferee attempts to accept in a manner violating the method, form, or delivery conditions stipulated in the offer.Fails to create a contract and instead operates as a counteroffer requiring the original offeror's assent.
  • Timing & Communication Rules for Revoking an Offer
    • Revocable Any Time Before Proper Communication of Acceptance: An offeror may withdraw an offer at any moment before acceptance is properly communicated back to the offeror (or offeror's authorized agent)—even if the offer specifies a future expiration deadline (unless supported by separate consideration as an irrevocable option contract).
    • Effective Upon Receipt / Notice to the Offeree: A revocation is not effective merely when the offeror decides to cancel or sends a message; it becomes effective only when communicated to and received by the offeree (or their agent) before acceptance is communicated.
    • Point of No Return: The moment the offeree's unconditional acceptance is properly communicated to the offeror, a binding contract is formed and the offeror's right to revoke ends.
  • Scenario: Thelma and Louise signed a contract to start a tour guide business together. Days later, before they bought the company car, Thelma wanted out. Louise agreed, so they cancelled the contract. Thelma also turned down a new venture, a pet shop, so nothing replaced the old contract. Their friendship survived.
    • Key takeaways:
      • Discharge by acts of the parties: Thelma's choice to end the contract was an act of the parties, not an operation of law, since no outside event forced it to end.
      • Mutual agreement: Both parties agreed to cancel and nothing replaced the contract, so this was a mutual agreement.
      • Not novation: Novation needs a new contract or party to replace the old one, and Thelma declined the pet shop.
      • Not accord and satisfaction: No substitute performance was offered or accepted.
      • If Louise had refused: Thelma backing out anyway would have been a breach, and Louise could have sought remedies.

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Offer and Acceptance
    • Offer and acceptance is the series of steps that leads to a contract.
      • Offeror: the party making the offer
      • Offeree: the party receiving the offer
    • Once an offer has been made, the offeree has three possible options regarding the offer:
      • They can accept it.
      • They can counter it.
      • They can reject it.

Binding the Agreement

Once the final offeree accepts the offer (or counteroffer), it's time to bind the agreement. This can be done on the final counteroffer form or on the purchase contract. When an offer is accepted, the person accepting communicates in writing to the offeror that they've accepted the offer. That person's agent is in charge of binding the agreement.

The date the agreement is bound is really important, because that starts the clock on all of the time-bound elements of the contract (like the inspection period, etc.). The "commencement date" or "start date" is the day after the binding agreement date.
