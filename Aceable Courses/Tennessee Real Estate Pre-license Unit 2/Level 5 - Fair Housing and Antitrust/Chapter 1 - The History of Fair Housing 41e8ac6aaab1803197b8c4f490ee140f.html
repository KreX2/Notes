Chapter 1 - The History of Fair Housing

Early Federal Civil Rights Acts & Fair Housing Milestones

  • Prior to the comprehensive Fair Housing Act of 1968, federal measures gradually established protections against housing discrimination, initially focusing on race and federally funded properties:
    Legislation / Landmark RulingYearProtected Classes CoveredScope & Practical Impact
    1. Civil Rights Act of 1866 (42 U.S.C. § 1982)1866 (enforced 1968)Race and color• The original federal fair housing statute, granting all citizens the same right to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property.

    • Largely unenforced for over a century until the U.S. Supreme Court ruling in Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. (1968), which held that all racial discrimination in housing—public or private—is strictly illegal with zero exceptions (even where Fair Housing Act exemptions would otherwise apply).
    2. Executive Order 110631962Race, color, creed (religion), and national origin• Prohibited discrimination in the sale, leasing, or occupancy of residential property owned, operated, or funded by the federal government (including properties financed with FHA or VA loans).

    • Limited overall impact because relatively few transactions involved federal funding at the time.
    3. Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 19641964Race, color, and national origin (within Title VI federally assisted programs)• Prohibited discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance, including federally assisted public housing programs.

    • Like Executive Order 11063, it did not reach purely private housing transactions without federal funding.
  • The Fair Housing Act (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968)
    • Enactment & Purpose: Passed in April 1968 following the assassination of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. (and strengthened by the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 and the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988). Its core purpose is to establish a unitary, open housing market by making it unlawful to refuse to sell, rent, finance, insure, or negotiate with any person—or to impose discriminatory terms—because of membership in a protected class.
    • The Seven Federal Protected Classes (FRESH CORN):
      1. Race: A person's racial heritage (U.S. Census categories include White, Black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, or another racial identity).
      1. Color: Pigmentation, complexion, skin shade, or tone—distinct from race (discrimination can occur between individuals of the same race based on skin shade).
      1. Religion: A system of religious beliefs, practices, or non-belief (e.g., Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, atheism).
      1. National Origin: Country of birth, ancestry, ethnicity, cultural heritage, or primary language.
      1. Sex (added in 1974): Biological sex, gender stereotyping, sexual harassment in housing, sexual orientation, and gender identity (recognized under federal Fair Housing Act enforcement alongside state and local LGBTQIA+ ordinances).
      1. Familial Status (added in 1988): Households with one or more individuals under age 18 living with a parent, legal custodian, or designee, as well as pregnant women and persons in the process of securing legal custody (adoption or foster care). (Note: Qualified housing for older persons—55+ or 62+ communities meeting federal criteria—carries a statutory exemption from familial status rules.)
      1. Disability / Handicap (added in 1988): A physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, a record of such impairment, or being regarded as having such an impairment (including blindness, deafness, mobility impairments, cognitive/mental illness, HIV/AIDS, cancer, and recovering alcoholism—though current illegal use of or addiction to a controlled substance is excluded).
  • Fair Housing Act Prohibitions: In Brief
    • Note 1: It doesn't matter if someone intends to discriminate or not. It's the action, not the motivation, that matters.
    • Note 2: These prohibitions are only prohibitions if the agent (or whoever) discriminates BECAUSE of the protected category. If a landlord refuses to rent a property to a man because of his bad credit history, and NOT because of his mental illness, then that is acceptable.
  • Groups Not Protected Under the Fair Housing Act (Lawful Grounds to Turn Down Prospects)
    • Real estate licensees and housing providers have the legal right to decline prospects based on objective, non-discriminatory business and legal qualifications—applied consistently to everyone:
      1. Minors (Under Age 18): Individuals who lack legal capacity to enter into a binding real estate contract or lease (though families with children under 18 are protected under familial status).
      1. Financial Inability / Poor Credit: Applicants with insufficient income, poor credit scores, inability to qualify for a mortgage loan, or inability to pay rent/purchase funds.
    • Criminal History Nuance (HUD Guidance & Disparate Impact):
      • Having a criminal record is not a standalone protected class under the Fair Housing Act.
      • However, because Black and Hispanic individuals are disproportionately represented in the criminal justice system, HUD guidance (2016) warns that blanket bans or overly broad criminal-history screening policies can create an unlawful disparate impact on race or national origin.
      • Best Practice for Housing Providers: Never use arrest records (without conviction) as a basis for denial; evaluate convictions only on a case-by-case basis, considering the nature and severity of the offense, time elapsed since conviction, and direct relevance to resident safety or property protection.
  • Fair Housing Act Prohibitions (42 U.S.C. §§ 3604–3617) — Specific Violations & Examples
    Prohibited PracticeCore Legal Rule & Real-World Example
    1. Refusing to Rent or Sell HousingOutright refusal to work with, sell to, or rent to a prospect because of a protected class (e.g., refusing to represent or show homes to a Black woman because of her race).
    2. Making Housing Otherwise UnavailableSelectively restricting access to certain buildings, floors, or units (e.g., showing women units in Complex B while refusing to show them available units in Complex A).
    3. Refusing to Negotiate for HousingCutting off or refusing to conduct negotiations once a prospect's protected status is learned (e.g., stopping negotiations after discovering a buyer is Jewish).
    4. Refusing to Rent or Sell After a Bona Fide OfferFailing to transmit or rejecting a genuine, good-faith offer because of the offeror's protected class (e.g., withholding a Black buyer's bona fide offer from the seller).
    5. Providing Unequal Services or FacilitiesDelivering inferior service, responsiveness, or access to amenities to members of a protected class—licensees must provide the same standard of service to every prospect.
    6. Setting Unequal Terms, Conditions, or PrivilegesCharging higher rent, higher security deposits, or stricter lease rules to a protected group (e.g., charging families with children higher deposits than childless tenants).
    7. Misrepresenting Property Availability or Sales TermsFalsely claiming a property or neighborhood has no available homes to keep a protected class out (e.g., telling an unmarried couple with children that Neighborhood 1 is sold out when homes are available).
    8. Denying or Restricting Access to an MLS or Brokerage OrganizationBlocking a licensed colleague from accessing the Multiple Listing Service or professional real estate associations due to race, religion, national origin, or another protected class.
    9. Discriminating in Brokering or AppraisingAltering valuation methods, fees, or financing terms based on protected status (e.g., overcharging a buyer with a cognitive impairment for an appraisal).
    10. SteeringChanneling buyers or renters toward or away from particular neighborhoods, schools, or building sections based on protected characteristics (e.g., showing Asian buyers only historically Asian neighborhoods).
    11. Blockbusting (Panic Peddling)Inducing homeowners to sell at discounted prices by claiming that the entry of a protected class will lower property values, increase crime, or hurt schools—then reselling at a markup.
    12. RedliningLenders or insurers refusing to issue mortgages/policies—or imposing harsher terms—within specific geographic areas based on the racial or ethnic makeup of the neighborhood.
    13. Discriminatory AdvertisingPublishing any notice, statement, or ad indicating a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on a protected class (e.g., "White tenants only"). (Exception: Qualified 55+ or 62+ Housing for Older Persons communities may lawfully advertise "no children".)
    14. Coercion, Intimidation, Threats, or Interference (§ 3617)Retaliating against, threatening, or interfering with anyone exercising their fair housing rights—or any licensee/person assisting others in exercising those rights (e.g., threatening to blacklist a tenant who objects to floor steering).
  • Disability Protections: Reasonable Modifications, Reasonable Accommodations & Accessible Design
    • Reasonable Modifications (Physical Changes): Structural or physical alterations to a dwelling or common area needed by a person with a disability to have full enjoyment of the premises (e.g., installing a wheelchair ramp, grab bars, or widening doorways). In private housing, the tenant generally pays for the modification (and may be required to restore interior changes at lease end if reasonable), though in federally assisted housing the provider generally bears the cost.
    • Reasonable Accommodations (Rule / Policy Exceptions): Exceptions or adjustments to rules, policies, practices, or services necessary to afford a person with a disability equal opportunity to use and enjoy housing (e.g., waiving a "no-pets" policy or pet deposit for a service animal or emotional support/assistance animal, or assigning a reserved accessible parking space near the entrance).
    • Accessible Design and Construction (Multifamily Buildings): Covered multifamily buildings built for first occupancy after March 13, 1991 (with 4 or more units) must meet federal Fair Housing Act accessibility design standards—including accessible public/common areas, doors wide enough for wheelchairs, accessible routes into and through units, reinforced bathroom walls for grab bars, and usable kitchens/bathrooms.
  • Fair Housing Act: The Four Exemptions
    • Always Remember
      • For the real estate license holder, there are no exemptions.
      • Under the Civil Rights Act of 1866, one can NEVER discriminate in housing on the basis of race.
  • Post-1968 Federal Fair Housing & Civil Rights Legislation
    • Following the passage of the Fair Housing Act of 1968, Congress and the executive branch expanded enforcement authority, funding rules, and protected classes across housing and public programs:
      Law / Executive OrderYearCore Provisions & Scope
      1. Housing and Community Development Act (Section 109 of Title I)1974• Created Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) programs, distributing formula-based HUD grants to states, cities, and counties to support decent housing and living environments for low-to-moderate income residents.

      • Section 109 prohibits excluding anyone from CDBG-funded programs or activities on the basis of race, color, national origin, or sex (and this same 1974 Act also added sex as a protected class under the Fair Housing Act).
      2. Executive Order 122591980• Established leadership and coordination of fair housing across federal programs.

      • Designated the Secretary of HUD as the primary federal official responsible for administering and enforcing the Fair Housing Act, while amending parts of Executive Order 11063.
      3. Federal Fair Housing Amendments Act1988• Added disability and familial status to the Fair Housing Act's protected classes.

      • Explicitly protected the right of individuals with disabilities to live together in group homes, making it unlawful for local zoning or housing rules to exclude group homes from residential neighborhoods.
      4. Americans with Disabilities Act (Title II of the ADA)1990• Prohibits disability discrimination and sets accessibility standards across state and local government entities, services, programs, activities (including public housing authorities), and public transportation systems.
  • Master Timeline of Federal Fair Housing Laws (1866–1990)
    • Civil Rights Act of 1866 (42 U.S.C. § 1982): Prohibited housing discrimination based on race or color with zero exceptions (enforced privately via Jones v. Mayer, 1968).
    • Executive Order 11063 (1962): Prohibited discrimination based on race, color, creed, or national origin in federally owned or federally funded housing (including FHA and VA loans).
    • Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VI): Prohibited discrimination in federally assisted programs, including public housing.
    • Fair Housing Act (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968): Prohibited discrimination in private and public real estate sales, rentals, lending, and brokerage based on protected classes.
    • Housing and Community Development Act (Section 109, 1974): Created CDBG grants and barred discrimination in CDBG-funded programs (and added sex to the Fair Housing Act).
    • Executive Order 12259 (1980): Centralized federal fair housing coordination under the Secretary of HUD.
    • Fair Housing Amendments Act (1988): Added disability and familial status and protected residential group homes.
    • Americans with Disabilities Act (Title II, 1990): Barred disability discrimination in state/local government services and public transit.
  • Tennessee Human Rights Act (T.C.A. § 4-21-101 et seq.) vs. Federal Fair Housing Act
    • Enforcement & Scope: Enforced by the Tennessee Human Rights Commission (THRC), prohibiting discrimination in employment, public accommodations, and housing. Whenever federal, state, and local laws overlap, the stricter, more protective standard governs.
    • Additional State Protected Class (Creed): Alongside all seven federal protected classes (race, color, religion, national origin, sex, familial status, and disability), Tennessee explicitly protects creed—encompassing any system of moral or ethical beliefs and associated practices, even outside an organized religion. (Individual Tennessee cities and counties may also enforce additional local protected classes.)
    • Stricter Tennessee Exemptions (More Restrictive Than Federal Law):
      Exemption AreaFederal Fair Housing Act StandardTennessee Human Rights Act (THRA) StandardKey Takeaway
      Owner-Occupied Multi-Unit ("Mrs. Murphy" Exemption)Exempts rentals in owner-occupied buildings with up to 4 units (1–4 family dwellings) when no broker/agent is used and no discriminatory ads are published.Exempts rentals in owner-occupied buildings housing no more than 2 families living independently ("Two or less in Tennessee").Tennessee cuts the owner-occupied exemption from 4 units down to 2 units (and never excuses racial discrimination under the 1866 Act or licensed brokerage activity).
      Housing for Older Persons (55+ Exemption)Exempts 55+ communities from familial status rules if at least 80% of occupied units have at least one resident aged 55 or older.Requires the community to be intended for and at least 90% occupied by at least one person aged 55 or older per unit.Tennessee raises the 55+ occupancy threshold from 80% to 90%.
      Single-Sex DormitoriesApplied through administrative/judicial interpretation rather than an explicit statutory carve-out.Explicitly exempts single-sex dormitory rental properties (including those operated by colleges and universities).Expressly codified in Tennessee's housing statute.

  • Landmark U.S. Supreme Court Cases Shaping Fair Housing (1896–1968)
    • Alongside federal civil rights statutes, five foundational U.S. Supreme Court decisions defined—and ultimately dismantled—legal segregation and racial discrimination in American housing:
      Supreme Court CaseYearCore Facts & IssueHolding & Impact on Fair Housing
      1. Plessy v. Ferguson1896Homer Plessy, a Black passenger, sat in a white-only Louisiana railcar and challenged the state's racial segregation statute after Judge John H. Ferguson upheld his arrest.• Upheld racial segregation (7–1 vote) under the "separate but equal" doctrine.

      • Sanctioned widespread state and local segregation across public accommodations, schools, and residential neighborhoods for nearly six decades.
      2. Buchanan v. Warley1917Louisville, Kentucky enacted a municipal zoning ordinance barring Black residents from moving onto blocks where white residents were the majority. White seller Charles Buchanan sued Black buyer William Warley for specific performance when Warley cited the ordinance to back out of purchasing Buchanan's home.• Struck down racially based municipal zoning as unconstitutional under the 14th Amendment because it unlawfully interfered with property owners' rights to sell and acquire property.
      3. Shelley v. Kraemer1948White St. Louis homeowners (the Kraemers) sued to enforce a private subdivision racially restrictive covenant to evict a Black family (the Shelleys) who had purchased a home in the neighborhood.• Ruled that while private individuals could voluntarily enter into private agreements, judicial (court) enforcement of racially restrictive covenants is unconstitutional state action violating the 14th Amendment's Equal Protection Clause—rendering racial covenants legally unenforceable.
      4. Brown v. Board of Education1954Oliver Brown and NAACP attorneys challenged segregated public schools in Topeka, Kansas as inherently unequal and unconstitutional.• Unanimously (9–0) held that racial segregation in public education violates the 14th Amendment's Equal Protection Clause, overturning Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine and establishing the constitutional precedent used to dismantle segregation across public housing and facilities.
      5. Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.1968Joseph Lee Jones, a Black buyer, sued a private St. Louis County real estate developer (Alfred H. Mayer Co.) for refusing to sell him a home solely because of his race.• Held that 13th Amendment authority and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 (42 U.S.C. § 1982) prohibit all racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property—public or private—with zero exceptions, finally giving the 1866 Act full private enforcement power.
  • Key Exam Takeaways: How These Rulings Fit Together
    • Public Zoning vs. Private Covenants vs. Private Sales:
      • Buchanan v. Warley (1917): Ended government racial zoning ordinances.
      • Shelley v. Kraemer (1948): Ended court enforcement of private racial deed covenants.
      • Jones v. Mayer (1968): Ended purely private racial discrimination in any real estate sale or rental—meaning no statutory exemption (not even owner-occupied "Mrs. Murphy" or FSBO exemptions under the 1968 Fair Housing Act) ever permits discrimination based on race or color.
