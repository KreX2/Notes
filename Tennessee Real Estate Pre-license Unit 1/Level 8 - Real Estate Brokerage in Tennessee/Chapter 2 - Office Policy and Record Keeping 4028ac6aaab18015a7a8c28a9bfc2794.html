Chapter 2 - Office Policy and Record Keeping

The Significance of Real Estate Record Keeping

  • Because buying a home is typically one of the most expensive and consequential purchases in a person's life, maintaining thorough transaction records is vital.
  • These records preserve the sales prices, contractual terms, legal agreements, and property condition disclosures necessary to protect all parties and ensure full buyer awareness.
  • Principal Broker & Affiliate Responsibilities
    • Principal Broker: Bears ultimate legal responsibility for establishing, maintaining, exercising due diligence over, and safeguarding all required brokerage and transaction records, and ensuring they are accessible to the Tennessee Real Estate Commission (TREC).
    • Affiliated Licensees: Must, at a minimum, ensure adequate maintenance of their own transaction records.
  • Mandatory Transaction Documents to Retain
    • Listing agreements
    • Inspection reports
    • Agency disclosures and property condition disclosures
    • Both accepted and rejected offers/contracts
    • Material correspondence between parties
    • Property management and lease agreements
  • TREC Access & Inspection Mandates
    • 24-Hour Rule: TREC has the legal authority to inspect and copy any broker records (accounting, transaction, or general files), which must be fully accessible and produced within 24 hours of a formal request.
    • Electronic Storage Standards: Records may be kept digitally, provided the principal broker implements a secure, authentic, accurate, readable, and readily available system that still complies with the 24-hour TREC production timeline.
    • The Three-Year Retention Rule
    • Under Tennessee law, all essential transaction documents and escrow/trust records must be securely stored and retained for a minimum of three years from the date of closing or transaction termination.
  • Core Definition of a Trust
    • A legal entity where asset or property control is transferred by a grantor (trustor) to a third party (trustee) to be held for the benefit of another (beneficiary).
    • Classic Example: A wealth-management trust created by a trustor to distribute assets to heirs (beneficiaries).
    • The Brownie Scenario Analogy:
      • Ariana = Grantor / Trustor (the person providing the funds)
      • Clara = Trustee (the neutral third party holding the funds until conditions are met)
      • John = Beneficiary (the recipient of the funds upon delivering the brownie)
  • Trust Accounts
    • Definition: Special financial accounts designated to hold client assets (such as earnest money deposits, advance fees, or security deposits) temporarily until they can be properly disbursed according to a contract.
    • Function: Serves the exact same institutional purpose as Clara holding the $5 in the brownie scenario—safeguarding funds until transaction terms are fulfilled.
  • Deed of Trust
    • A specialized legal instrument used in real estate financing to secure a borrower's debt to a lender.
    • Unlike a standard mortgage (which creates a lien), a deed of trust temporarily conveys legal title of the property to a neutral third-party trustee to hold "in trust" until the loan is fully paid off, ensuring the lender's security interest
  • Purpose and Function of Real Estate Trust Accounts
    • Definition & Use: Designated accounts used exclusively to hold transaction-related client funds—such as earnest money deposits or tenant security deposits for property managers—separate from the broker's personal or operational accounts.
    • Common Aliases: Often referred to interchangeably as escrow accounts or custodial trusts.
    • Fiduciary Safeguard: They ensure licensees uphold their fiduciary duties by preventing anyone from accessing or benefiting from client funds until a transaction is fully closed and properly disbursed according to the contract.
  • Account Location & Legal Ownership
    • Geographic Rule: Trust accounts are typically established in a financial institution located within the same state where the licensee resides.
    • Legal Ownership During Holding: From the moment funds are placed in the trust until they are disbursed to the beneficiary, the designated trustee holds legal ownership/control over the assets, relying heavily on strict ethics and state regulations to prevent misuse.
  • Commingling
    • Is the illegal act of mixing personal or operating funds with client trust funds (e.g., a broker depositing their personal salary or business operating revenue into the company’s escrow/trust account).
    • Even accidental commingling—such as misremembering which funds belong to whom or failing to promptly remove earned commissions and fees from a trust account—creates severe financial exposure and violates TREC regulations (e.g., accidentally spending client earnest money on a car down payment because personal and trust funds were mixed).
  • Best Practices & Industry Safeguards
    • To prevent accidental commingling, brokers frequently send earnest money checks directly to a title company or closing attorney for safekeeping.
  • Tennessee Trust Account Rules for Earnest Money
    • Timing: Earnest money must be deposited into the designated trust account immediately upon the acceptance of an offer.
    • Restriction on Use: Earnest money deposits must remain untouched and fully accounted for until the transaction successfully closes, or until the buyer and seller execute a written agreement on how the funds should be disbursed if the sale falls through.
    • Disciplinary Consequence: Any unauthorized handling, mixing, or misuse of earnest money triggers a TREC investigation and potential disciplinary action against the broker.
  • Conversion in Real Estate
    • Is the illegal act of misappropriating or stealing trust account funds by using them for any purpose other than what they were intended for—such as paying for a brokerage's operational expenses (e.g., electricity bills, rent, or payroll).
    • The Relationship Between Commingling and Conversion
    • Commingling is simply the act of mixing personal and client funds together.
    • Conversion is the next step where those mixed funds are actually spent or appropriated for unauthorized use. Commingling frequently serves as the gateway that leads directly to accidental or intentional conversion (e.g., depositing client funds into an operating account, then unknowingly spending the client's money to pay company bills).
  • Key Distinction Summary
    • Commingling: Mixing the money (even if no funds are ever spent).
    • Conversion: Spending or using the money for unauthorized purposes (essentially theft).
  • Property Management Trust Accounts Overview
    • Definition & Purpose: A specialized type of trust/escrow account used exclusively in property management to hold funds belonging to other parties—such as tenant security deposits, collected monthly rents, and capital funds provided by property owners.
    • Governing Terms: The specific structure, rules, and record-keeping requirements of the account (including whether it is interest-bearing and the exact timelines for deposits and withdrawals) are dictated by the underlying property management agreement.
    • Lifecycle Example: Collecting a tenant's security deposit, holding it safely in the account during the lease term, and returning it promptly upon move-out if the property is undamaged (e.g., Daisy receiving her $300 deposit back).
  • Strict Separation & Commingling Prohibitions
    • Funds belonging to the property management firm or broker must never be commingled with client capital, rent collections, or security deposits.
  • Role of Unlicensed Assistants in Tennessee
    • Under Tennessee regulations, unlicensed assistants are permitted to accept, record, and deposit security deposits and advance rents into the designated trust account, but this must be done strictly under the direct supervision of the principal broker.
  • Principal Broker Responsibility & Definition of Trust Money
    • Definition: Trust money includes any funds belonging to others received by a licensee acting as an agent or facilitator, or any money held temporarily as a custodian.
    • Principal Broker Oversight: The principal broker is strictly responsible at all times for all trust money accepted by them or their affiliated licensees.
    • Affiliate Rule: Affiliate brokers must turn over all trust funds to their principal broker immediately upon receipt.
    • Required Escrow Records & Contract Specifications
      • Mandatory Log Details: Brokers must maintain complete escrow records detailing the depositor, date of deposit, date of withdrawal, payee, and any other information required by TREC.
      • Contract Terms: When a contract authorizes a broker to hold trust money, it must explicitly state the terms/conditions of disbursement and the name/address of the escrow holder.
      • Retention Period: Under Tennessee's three-year rule, all escrow records must be securely maintained for a minimum of three years.
    • Lawful Methods of Trust Money Disbursal
      Principal brokers may disburse escrow funds under specific circumstances, without unreasonable delay:
      • Upon a reasonable interpretation of the contract authorizing the holding of funds.
      • Upon securing a separate written agreement signed by all interested parties.
      • At the official closing of the transaction.
      • Upon the rejection of an offer.
      • Upon the withdrawal of an unaccepted offer.
      • Upon filing an interpleader action in a court of competent jurisdiction.
      • Upon the order of a court.
      • Handling Disputed Funds & Interpleader Actions
      • If a dispute arises between a buyer and seller that cannot be resolved via contract interpretation, funds are held until resolved by mediation, arbitration, or alternative dispute resolution.
      • If requested in writing regarding disputed funds, Tennessee law mandates turning the funds over to an attorney within 21 days from receipt of the request to be interpleaded.
    • Violations & Prohibitions
      • Failing to account for or remit money belonging to others within a reasonable time is a direct license law violation.
      • Commingling and conversion are strictly prohibited.

Key Terms

  • commingling - the act of mixing personal funds with a client’s funds
  • conversion - the illegal act of appropriating trust account funds for payment of an agency's operating expenses

Key Concepts & Principles

  • Required Records - Under Tennessee law, transaction documents and escrow records must be maintained for three year
  • Reconciliation - When it comes to trust accounts, reconciliation means that bank and checkbook amounts agree (i.e., they "reconcile" with each other). Furthermore, the amount of trust funds in both locations are the same. By the end of the process, the broker hopefully has confirmed that the trust account is in balance with the bank account record.
  • Electronic Record Keeping - Records may be retained electronically. However, the principal broker must develop a system so that the records will be secure, authentic, and accurate. If access is required, the records must be readable and available. Like other records, electronic records must be made available to TREC within 24 hours of a request.
  • Trust Accounts in Real Estate - The purpose of trust accounts is to provide an account that is separate from operational or personal accounts in which to maintain and account for transaction-related funds.

The rules:

  • Earnest money must be deposited in trust account upon acceptance of an offer.
  • Records of trust accounts must be retained for three years after the closing date of the transaction.

Commingling vs. Conversion

  • Commingling is simply the mixing of funds. Even if a client's money is not spent, the mixing is still a no-go.
  • Conversion occurs when funds (which have likely been commingled) are actually spent on something.
